# The Buddhist Sangha as Voluntary Cross-Community Cooperation

## Why this document exists

[noticeable_hand.md](noticeable_hand.md) applied a voluntary-peer +
longevity filter to the case set in
[historical_cases.md](historical_cases.md) and identified the Buddhist
*sangha* as "the single sharpest challenge to the strong Christian
reading" — the longest-running voluntary cross-community network in
the dataset, non-theistic in its philosophical core, and obviously not
Christian. The open question at the end of that document asked for a
serious analysis of the sangha on the same metrics the Christian cases
are evaluated on. This document is that analysis.

The document reaches four principal findings, stated up front so the
detailed treatment can be read against them:

1. **The sangha is a real case of voluntary cross-community
   cooperation, but it is a different *kind* of case than Westphalia
   or the Hanse.** It is a trans-polity religious order as much as it
   is a cooperation regime among polities that stay separate. The
   closer Christian analogue is not Westphalia but the Roman
   Catholic monastic orders, or the medieval papacy as a
   cross-sovereign ecclesiastical authority.
2. **Treated at this properly specified scale, the sangha splits into
   three distinct regimes with distinct durations, mechanisms, and
   decay patterns** — the Theravada monastic network across Sri Lanka
   and mainland Southeast Asia, the East Asian Buddhist network
   across China, Korea, and Japan, and the Tibetan Vajrayana network
   across the Tibetan plateau and the Inner Asian steppe. Each has
   lasted between ~1100 and ~2300 years; each has worked through
   different mechanisms; each has a different relationship to
   political power.
3. **The sangha supplies the five anti-spiral features
   ([justus_hostis.md](justus_hostis.md#the-anti-spiral-features-why-anchor-above-self-is-necessary-but-not-sufficient))
   — substantially on (1), (2), (4), and (5), and in functional-but-not-identical form on (3).**
   This is a genuine result. Buddhism is not a partial or degraded
   substrate on the anti-spiral features; it supplies them, in
   recognizably different form, with comparable depth to Christianity
   on most of them and narrower scope on the scapegoat-revelation
   feature where Christianity's form is structurally distinctive.
4. **The verdict on the Christian-particularist reading is mixed
   and specific.** The strong form ("only Christianity can do the
   load-bearing work") is weakened by the sangha case. The
   weaker form ("Christianity supplies the most complete single
   combination of the features while other traditions supply real
   partial versions") survives and is consistent with the sangha
   evidence. The Christian-particularist reading's strongest move
   — the claim that the *specific kind* of cross-polity cooperation
   Christendom produced (Westphalia, Hanse, lex mercatoria) has no
   close Buddhist analogue — also survives, but at the cost of
   conceding that the sangha's kind of cross-polity cooperation is
   a real and durable achievement of a different form.

The document proceeds: methodological note; three regime analyses on
the Christian-cases template (Founding context / Mechanism / Output /
Decay / Lesson); cross-regime comparison on the metrics used for the
Christian cases; the five anti-spiral features applied to the sangha;
verdict on the Christian-particularist and catholic readings; lessons
for kith-covenant; open questions; references.

## Methodological note: what is being compared

The Christian cases in [historical_cases.md](historical_cases.md) share
a specific structural feature: they are regimes in which *separate
polities*, each with its own sovereign and its own institutions,
cooperate across their boundaries on a framework they jointly
recognize. Westphalia is peer cooperation among sovereigns;
the Hanse is peer cooperation among commercial cities; lex mercatoria
is cross-polity law among merchants; the Iroquois Confederacy is peer
cooperation among nations under a shared federal instrument.

The sangha does not straightforwardly fit this shape. It is
simultaneously:

- **A trans-polity institution** — a single religious community whose
  members (monks and nuns under the Vinaya) share an identity that
  persists across sovereign boundaries. Analogously, one does not
  describe the Benedictine order or the Society of Jesus as
  "cross-polity cooperation among the communities they inhabit";
  they are themselves cross-polity institutions.
- **A mechanism of cross-community cooperation** — lay Buddhist
  communities in separate polities (a Sinhalese kingdom and a Burmese
  kingdom, a Tang-dynasty Chinese court and a Heian-period Japanese
  court, a Tibetan principality and a Mongol khanate) really did
  cooperate with each other *via* shared sangha recognition,
  pilgrimage networks, text transmission, and ordination lineages.
  The sangha was the medium through which the lay communities
  cooperated.

These two modes are entangled and cannot be cleanly separated. For
the kith-covenant comparison, this has two consequences.

First, **the raw "longevity" comparison is misleading** if the sangha
is treated as the same kind of thing as Westphalia. Westphalia's ~270
years is measured as the duration of a cooperation *framework* among
polities that remained separate; the sangha's ~2500 years is measured
as the duration of a cross-polity *order* whose members were part of
the same community across those polities. The Catholic monastic
orders are the closer comparison: the Benedictines (since ca. 529 CE)
have ~1500 years; the Cistercians (since 1098) have ~900 years; the
Dominicans and Franciscans (since the 13th c.) have ~800 years.
Scaled this way, the sangha's lead narrows but remains real; and the
comparison with Westphalia becomes a comparison between unlike kinds.

Second, **the parts of the sangha record that are most directly
kith-covenant-relevant are the episodes in which the trans-polity
institution worked as an instrument of cooperation between polities
that were genuinely separate**. These episodes are real and
substantial: Sri Lankan re-ordination by Burmese and Thai monks in the
11th, 18th, and 19th centuries, each time re-establishing a higher
ordination line that had died out locally; Chinese-Japanese-Korean
monastic pilgrimage and dharma transmission across imperial
boundaries for more than a millennium; the Tibet-Mongol *yön-chö*
(patron-priest) relationship that sustained cross-polity authority
without political subjugation for several centuries. These are the
episodes on which the sangha most directly bears on kith-covenant's
design question.

A note on voluntariness. Some of the sangha's durability has rested on
royal patronage, state Buddhism, and state-enforced monastic
regulation — the Chinese state's control of ordination certificates
from Tang forward, the Tokugawa temple registration system, the
Thai *Sangharaja* and state monastic bureaucracy. Inside these
episodes the sangha is less a voluntary network than a state-licensed
institution. But its *cross-sovereign* recognition — a Japanese
Rinzai monk's ordination is recognized at a Chinese Linji temple, a
Sri Lankan Theravada monk's ordination is recognized at a Thai
*wat* — is not itself a state product. The states have recognized
what the sangha was already doing across their borders, rather than
constituting it. This is the sense in which the sangha qualifies as
voluntary cross-community cooperation, even though it has often been
bound up with state power inside any particular polity.

A further note on scope. This document treats the three regimes
(Theravada, East Asian, Tibetan Vajrayana) as genuinely distinct
rather than three faces of a single institution. After roughly the
11th century these regimes are practically separate: a Theravada monk
in Sri Lanka does not share ordination lineage with a Japanese Zen
monk, cannot serve as the tenth monk in a Tibetan ordination quorum,
recites a different Vinaya recension, and has no institutional
contact with the Tibetan hierarchy. The regimes share a common root
(the Indian sangha of the Buddha's own community, the early councils,
and the Ashokan missionary expansion) and a common lineage
ideology (unbroken transmission from the Buddha), but they are
operationally independent networks. Treating them as one institution
would overstate the longevity of any single cooperation regime; the
comparable Christian move would be to treat Orthodox, Catholic, and
Protestant Christianity as one institution to claim 2000 years of
continuous Christian cooperation. The comparison is cleaner when each
regime is evaluated on its own.

## Regime I: The Theravada monastic network

### Founding context

The Theravada tradition traces itself to the Sthaviravāda ("Teaching of
the Elders") school, one of the early divisions of the Indian sangha
that hardened after the Second Council (ca. 383 BCE, Vesali) and again
after the Third Council (ca. 250 BCE, Pataliputra under Ashoka).
Ashoka's missionary sending — recorded in the Sri Lankan *Mahavamsa*
and corroborated by the Edicts of Ashoka — dispatched nine missions to
peripheral regions, most durably Mahinda's mission to Sri Lanka
(mid-3rd c. BCE) and (in later tradition) Sona and Uttara's mission to
Suvannabhumi (probably Lower Burma / Thai-Malay peninsula).

Sri Lanka is the hinge. The *Mahavihara* at Anuradhapura preserved the
Pali canon and the Theravada monastic lineage continuously from
Mahinda's ordination of the Sinhalese king Devanampiya Tissa
(ca. 247 BCE) onward. The Pali canon was committed to writing at the
Fourth Council (ca. 29 BCE, Aluvihare, Sri Lanka) — one of the
earliest substantial scriptural corpora in any tradition to be
written down as a fixed text. From Sri Lanka, Theravada expanded to
mainland Southeast Asia — Burma, Thailand, Cambodia, Laos — in waves
over the first and second millennia CE, displacing earlier Hindu,
Mahayana, and tantric Buddhist presences in most of the region by the
15th century.

By the high medieval period the Theravada world comprised a set of
distinct Buddhist kingdoms (Sri Lankan polities, Pagan and its
successors in Burma, Sukhothai and Ayutthaya in Thailand, Angkor and
later Cambodian states, Lan Xang in Laos) united by a shared Pali
canon, a shared Vinaya recension, and mutually recognized higher
ordination. This is the Theravada cross-community cooperation regime.

### Mechanism

Several institutional elements carry the cooperation load:

- **Shared Vinaya.** The monastic rule of 227 precepts for monks
  (*bhikkhus*) is recited in Pali at the fortnightly *uposatha*
  ceremony, identically in every Theravada monastery. Transgressions
  against the rule are confessed collectively. The Vinaya's content,
  language of recitation, and procedural form are constant across the
  Theravada world.
- **The Pali canon.** The *Tipitaka* (Vinaya, Sutta, Abhidhamma) is
  preserved in Pali across all Theravada polities. Scriptural
  disputes among Theravada kingdoms are argued against a common text
  in a common liturgical language that is nobody's vernacular — an
  important neutralization mechanism.
- **Higher ordination across polities.** *Upasampada* (higher
  ordination) requires a quorum of ten fully ordained monks in a
  properly constituted *sima* (boundary). In border regions, five
  suffice. The act is performed in Pali, the rules of procedure are
  identical everywhere, and the resulting ordination is recognized
  throughout the Theravada world. A monk ordained in Thailand is a
  monk in Sri Lanka without re-ordination.
- **Re-ordination missions.** Three times in Sri Lankan history the
  local higher-ordination lineage died out (warfare, persecution,
  demographic collapse). Each time it was re-established by inviting
  a quorum of monks from another Theravada country: from Burma in the
  11th century under Vijayabahu I; from Thailand in 1753 under
  Kirti Sri Rajasinha (establishing the Siyam Nikaya, the largest
  modern Sri Lankan monastic fraternity); from Burma in 1802
  (Amarapura Nikaya) and 1864 (Ramanna Nikaya). These episodes are
  the direct empirical instances of the sangha working as a
  cross-polity cooperation mechanism — when a local community loses
  the line, the peer community across the sea supplies the
  reconstitution.
- **Pilgrimage networks.** Bodh Gaya, the Sri Lankan *Sri Pada*, the
  Burmese Shwedagon, and the Kandy *Dalada Maligawa* (Temple of the
  Sacred Tooth) are supra-polity pilgrimage sites. Royal embassies
  carrying relics between Buddhist courts — the Sri Lankan Tooth
  Relic, Burmese tooth relics, Thai Buddha images — were a durable
  channel of cross-polity diplomacy conducted through sangha
  intermediaries rather than through lay diplomatic corps.
- **Royal patron as protector-not-sovereign.** The Theravada doctrine
  of kingship frames the king as the protector of the *sasana* (the
  dispensation of the Buddha's teaching) and as a *dhammaraja*
  (righteous king) whose legitimacy is conditional on that protection.
  Kings convoke councils, enforce Vinaya among corrupt monks, and
  patronize monasteries; they do not authoritatively determine the
  Vinaya or the canon. A Burmese king cannot reform the Vinaya; he
  can only enforce it. This keeps the substrate out of the hands of
  any one sovereign.

### Output

The Theravada regime has sustained, for roughly two millennia across
separate kingdoms:

- A single transmitted canon in a single liturgical language across a
  region spanning modern Sri Lanka, Myanmar, Thailand, Cambodia,
  Laos, and (historically) parts of southern Vietnam, southern
  China (Yunnan Dai), and Indonesian trading ports;
- Mutual recognition of ordination across all these polities, making
  monk-exchange for reconstitution or training a standing
  institutional possibility;
- A shared pilgrimage geography and relic-movement network that
  functioned as an informal diplomatic channel between Buddhist
  kingdoms;
- A shared canon of commentaries (Buddhaghosa's *Visuddhimagga*,
  fifth century CE, became and remains the standard scholastic
  reference across the Theravada world);
- A repeated pattern of mutual reconstitution when a local lineage
  collapsed, as the Sri Lankan re-ordination episodes demonstrate.

The regime did not produce Westphalian political cooperation among
Theravada kingdoms — they warred with each other in the ordinary way;
Burma sacked Ayutthaya in 1767 — but it did produce a substrate of
cross-polity religious, scholastic, and scriptural cooperation that
survived those wars and that continues to function.

### Decay

The Theravada regime has not decayed in the sense Westphalia or the
Hanse decayed. It has been stressed repeatedly, and two stress
episodes deserve naming:

- **Colonial disruption** (18th–20th c.). British colonial government
  in Ceylon and Burma, French in Indochina, Dutch elsewhere — each
  reduced the pre-colonial *dhammaraja* state's support for the
  sangha, creating periods of institutional weakness. But colonial
  rule did not destroy the sangha; it damaged the state-sangha
  relationship while leaving the cross-polity mechanisms largely
  intact. Thai monastic reform (Mongkut's Dhammayutika Nikaya,
  mid-19th c.) and the Burmese revival under the *Sangharaja*
  traditions were contemporaneous responses.
- **Buddhist nationalism** (20th–21st c.). This is the more serious
  stressor and the one that speaks directly to the anti-spiral
  question below. In Sri Lanka, Sinhala-Buddhist nationalism fused
  Theravada identity with ethnic claims, producing the long civil
  war against the Tamil minority (1983–2009) and a pattern of
  anti-Muslim violence that continues. In Myanmar, the Ma Ba Tha
  and the 969 Movement have supplied the ideological framework for
  state violence against the Rohingya (the 2017 clearance operations
  being the most visible instance). These are betrayals of the
  anti-spiral features the tradition otherwise supplies, and they
  are not trivial — they are mass-violence episodes in which
  Buddhist identity functioned as an in-group marker against
  designated out-groups. The parallel with Christian colonial
  selectivity (discussed in
  [noticeable_hand.md](noticeable_hand.md#the-colonial-frontier-counterweight))
  is close: the substrate's own scriptural mandate (universal
  compassion; the Brahmaviharas including metta to enemies) was
  explicitly violated by its nominal carriers. Whether this
  is a decay of the cross-polity cooperation regime or a failure
  inside particular polities is arguable; the cross-polity
  ordination and canon mechanisms continue in parallel.

### Lesson for kith-covenant

Four lessons.

First, **a cross-polity regime that works through shared textual
canon, shared liturgical language, and shared ordination lineage can
sustain mutual recognition across sovereigns and across millennia
even when the sovereigns are at war with each other.** The Theravada
regime kept producing valid monks in Burma and Thailand while
Burmese and Thai armies were destroying each other's cities. The
analogy for kith-covenant is that the cross-compact recognition
machinery should be structurally decoupled from the operational
cooperation among member communities, so that the recognition
machinery survives operational disputes.

Second, **the re-ordination pattern is a useful precedent for
kith-covenant's reconstitution question** ([exit_and_succession.md](exit_and_succession.md)).
When a member community loses the local capacity to produce properly
credentialed successors — analogue of Sri Lanka losing the
*upasampada* lineage — a peer community across the boundary can
supply the reconstitution, provided the cross-polity recognition
machinery is kept working for exactly this purpose.

Third, **the *dhammaraja* principle is a working model of how to
keep a substrate out of any single sovereign's hands.** The king
protects the substrate; he does not own it. Reforms that purport to
come from royal authority are legitimate only if the sangha
recognizes them as consistent with the Vinaya. This is directly
parallel to the Peace of God's constitution of categories of
inviolability by ecclesiastical authority against lordly violence
([historical_cases.md](historical_cases.md#the-peace-of-god-and-truce-of-god))
— a substrate-holder independent of the sovereign is a structural
necessity for sovereign-transcending cooperation.

Fourth, **Buddhist nationalism is the anti-spiral failure mode
operative inside an otherwise robust substrate**, and kith-covenant's
compact cannot assume that supplying the anti-spiral features
guarantees their application. The features make the failure mode
avoidable; they do not make it impossible. The compact must expect
occasional betrayal and must include the anti-spiral-features
credentialing check ([recognition_protocols.md](recognition_protocols.md))
as an ongoing rather than one-time examination.

## Regime II: The East Asian Buddhist network

### Founding context

Mahayana Buddhism entered China via the Silk Road in the first
century CE, with gradual institutional consolidation through the
Three Kingdoms, Jin, Northern and Southern Dynasties, and Tang
periods. The key translation phase runs from Kumarajiva (344–413) to
Xuanzang (602–664) and Yijing (635–713), during which the Chinese
Buddhist canon was assembled from successive Sanskrit and Prakrit
translations, reworked in Chinese idiom, and stabilized into what
became the East Asian Mahayana scriptural corpus.

The East Asian regime is not a single monastic lineage but a
network of schools — Tiantai, Huayan, Pure Land, Chan (Zen in
Japanese, Seon in Korean, Thien in Vietnamese), Shingon/Mijiao, and
Vinaya schools — that share the Chinese Buddhist canon (the
*Dazangjing*), the Chinese Vinaya recension (the
*Dharmaguptaka Vinaya*, the *Sifen Lü*, the most widely used Vinaya
across China, Korea, Japan, and Vietnam), and the practice of
formal lineage transmission documented in written certificates. Over
the next millennium the network extended from China into Korea
(Goguryeo, Baekje, Silla, Goryeo, Joseon), Japan (transmission from
Korea in the 6th century, then direct Chinese transmission in
multiple waves), Vietnam (*Thien* from China), and (more
peripherally) into the Tangut and Tibetan frontiers.

What binds this into a cross-polity cooperation regime is the
practice of *pilgrimage for dharma transmission*: monks from peripheral
polities travel to China (or, in the earlier period, from China to
India) to receive transmission, to train in established monasteries,
to copy or receive scriptural authority, and to return to their home
polities with recognized credentials. This pattern runs from
Xuanzang's India pilgrimage (629–645) through Ennin's nine-year
pilgrimage to Tang China (838–847), Dogen's Song China pilgrimage
(1223–1227), and many others into the Ming and Qing periods.

### Mechanism

- **The shared East Asian Buddhist canon.** The *Dazangjing* (in
  multiple successive imperial editions — Kaibao, Qisha, Zhaocheng,
  various Korean *Tripitaka Koreana* editions, Japanese editions)
  provided a common scriptural reference across polities. The
  Goryeo Tripitaka (13th c.), carved on 81,258 wooden blocks at
  Haeinsa, remains the most complete surviving premodern East Asian
  Buddhist canon and was consulted across East Asia.
- **The *Sifen Lü* (Dharmaguptaka Vinaya) as shared monastic rule.**
  While Theravada uses the Theravada Vinaya, all major East Asian
  Buddhist schools use the *Sifen Lü* for ordination, making
  ordination lineage in principle compatible across China, Korea,
  Japan, and Vietnam.
- **Dharma transmission certificates (*yinke*, *inka*).** Chan/Zen
  transmission is explicitly documented — the disciple receives a
  written certification from the master, and the lineage chart
  identifies the unbroken transmission back through named teachers
  to Bodhidharma and ultimately to the Buddha. The certificate is
  recognized across polities; a Japanese Rinzai monk with inka from
  a Chinese Linji master is a recognized Chan teacher in China. The
  institution of documented lineage transmission is more developed in
  East Asian Buddhism than in any of the other three regimes under
  examination.
- **Pilgrimage as the cross-polity cooperation instrument.** The
  paradigmatic episodes — Xuanzang's India journey, Ennin's nine-year
  residence in Tang China, Dogen's two years in Song China, the
  serial Korean pilgrimages to China through the Goryeo period, the
  continuing Japanese Obaku transmission from Ming-Qing Fujian — are
  instances of the cross-polity cooperation regime at work. A monk
  travels, is received, is trained, is examined, receives
  transmission, returns. The receiving polity bears the training
  cost; the sending polity receives the credentialed teacher. The
  cooperation is real, routine, and sustained across sovereigns that
  were often at war (Tang China and Tibet; Ming China and Japan; and
  so on).
- **Text exchange and mutual correction of canon.** Ennin brought
  some 800 scrolls of text and ritual manuals back from Tang China;
  Uichon brought thousands of volumes of Huayan and Tiantai texts
  from Song China back to Goryeo; the later Japanese Obaku
  transmission brought substantial Ming-Qing Chan materials to
  Tokugawa Japan. The canon was, over centuries, collectively
  curated across polities through these exchanges.
- **Recognition of credentialed visiting teachers.** Ingen Ryuki's
  arrival in Japan (1654) with a group of Chinese Obaku monks led to
  the establishment of the Obaku school as a recognized Japanese
  Zen lineage, with Japanese disciples receiving transmission from
  Chinese masters on Japanese soil. This is direct instantiation of
  the cross-polity recognition machinery.

### Output

For roughly 1300 years (5th c. CE through the modern disruptions
below), the East Asian Buddhist network sustained:

- A shared scriptural canon revised collectively across polities;
- Shared liturgical Chinese (*Hanmun* in Korea, *Kanbun* in Japan)
  as a neutral scholastic language across Korean, Japanese,
  Vietnamese, and other peripheral polities;
- Mutual recognition of ordination and dharma transmission across
  sovereigns;
- Pilgrimage networks that survived dynastic transitions (Sui to
  Tang, Tang to Song, Song to Yuan, Yuan to Ming, Ming to Qing, and
  the parallel Korean and Japanese transitions), producing continuous
  cross-polity training exchange;
- A scholastic tradition (Tiantai, Huayan) whose commentarial
  apparatus was collectively developed across polities.

### Decay

Multiple major disruption episodes, but none total:

- **The Huichang persecution (845 CE).** Emperor Wuzong of Tang
  moved against Buddhism: ~4,600 monasteries destroyed, ~40,000
  smaller shrines, ~260,000 monks and nuns defrocked. The
  persecution was Tang-state-wide but did not reach Korea and Japan,
  where East Asian Buddhism continued. Chinese Buddhism recovered
  under Wuzong's successor and later dynasties, but several Chinese
  schools never fully recovered their Tang-period scope.
- **The Mongol conquest (13th c.).** Northern Chinese Buddhism was
  disrupted by the conquest and by the Yuan dynasty's preferential
  patronage of Tibetan Vajrayana over Chinese schools, shifting
  institutional weight within East Asia.
- **The Meiji *shinbutsu bunri* (1868) and subsequent state Shinto.**
  The Japanese state forcibly separated Shinto from Buddhism,
  nationalized Shinto, and marginalized Buddhism. Some 40,000
  Buddhist temples were destroyed or repurposed in the early Meiji
  period. Japanese Buddhism survived but lost its integration with
  the state and with Shinto; it has not recovered the institutional
  density of the pre-Meiji period.
- **The Joseon-dynasty suppression in Korea (14th–19th c.).**
  Neo-Confucian Joseon reduced Buddhist institutions, confined them
  mostly to mountain monasteries, and restricted monastic ordination.
  Korean Buddhism persisted but in substantially reduced
  institutional form for five centuries.
- **The Cultural Revolution (1966–76).** Most remaining Chinese
  Buddhist institutions were destroyed or repurposed, monks and
  nuns laicized or imprisoned, texts burned. Partial recovery began
  after 1979 but institutional scope is still well below
  pre-1949 levels.
- **Vietnamese 20th-century disruption.** French colonial rule,
  the wars of independence, and the post-1975 communist state all
  reduced the Vietnamese sangha; it persists and has revived but
  in reduced form.

Through all these episodes, *the cross-polity recognition machinery
did not fully collapse*. Japanese Zen lineages preserved Chinese
transmission when Chinese Chan was under persecution; Korean Seon
preserved Chinese and Japanese contacts; modern exile and
post-communist revival rely on the same cross-polity mechanisms the
network has always used. The East Asian network has sustained its
cross-polity cooperation function through multiple total political
inversions — Tang to Song, Song to Yuan, Yuan to Ming, Ming to Qing,
Qing to Republic to PRC; Goryeo to Joseon; Heian to Kamakura to
Muromachi to Edo to Meiji. This durability through political
inversion is the regime's most characteristic feature.

### Lesson for kith-covenant

Three lessons.

First, **a cross-polity cooperation regime organized around
pilgrimage-for-credentialing is a robust architectural pattern.** A
credential-issuing community of recognized authority, accessible
across boundaries, receiving candidates from peripheral polities and
sending them back credentialed — this is a pattern the East Asian
Buddhist network instantiated for over a millennium. The modern
parallel is the doctoral-degree network among universities
internationally. Kith-Covenant's recognition protocols
([recognition_protocols.md](recognition_protocols.md)) already
include a credentialing layer; the East Asian Buddhist case is an
argument for making that layer first-class and for providing
pilgrimage-style training exchange as a mechanism by which
communities can send and receive credentialed representatives.

Second, **documented lineage transmission is a structural innovation
worth adopting.** The *inka* / *yinke* certificate, combined with
the lineage chart that names every teacher back to the founder, is a
credential that carries across polities without requiring a central
authority. Kith-Covenant's members and mediators might plausibly
operate under analogous documented-transmission credentials —
recognition certificates naming the chain from the current bearer
back through named predecessors to the compact's founding.

Third, **the regime's durability through political inversion is
worth studying directly.** Every East Asian polity that hosted this
network went through at least one total political discontinuity
(dynastic overthrow, colonial imposition, revolutionary state), and
the network carried through each of them, often with institutional
damage but without losing the cross-polity recognition machinery
itself. Kith-Covenant should design for political inversion of this
kind — a member polity's state going to pieces and being replaced by
something else — as a routine expected event rather than a
catastrophic failure.

## Regime III: The Tibetan Vajrayana network

### Founding context

Buddhism entered Tibet in two waves. The *First Transmission* begins
under King Trisong Detsen (r. 755–797), with the arrival of
Shantarakshita and the tantric master Padmasambhava, and the founding
of Samye Monastery (ca. 779), Tibet's first institutional monastery.
This transmission was interrupted by King Langdarma's persecution
(ca. 841–842) — the assassination of Langdarma by a Buddhist monk,
Lhalung Palgyi Dorje, ended the persecution but also the imperial
sponsorship. The fragmentary century after Langdarma preserved
tantric lineages (what became the Nyingma, or "Old School") without
state institutional support.

The *Second Transmission* (10th–11th c.) re-established institutional
Buddhism in Tibet, largely through the efforts of the translator
Rinchen Zangpo (958–1055) in western Tibet and the Indian master
Atisha Dipankara Srijnana (982–1054) in central Tibet. From this
transmission came the three main "New Schools": Kadampa (later
absorbed into Gelug), Sakya, and Kagyu. The Gelug school was founded
by Tsongkhapa (1357–1419) as a reform of the Kadampa, and became the
dominant school from the 17th century forward under the institution
of the Dalai Lama.

Crucially for the cross-polity analysis, Tibetan Buddhism from the
13th century onward developed the *patron-priest relationship*
(*yön-chö*, "patron-priest") with non-Tibetan rulers — first with the
Mongol Yuan court (Sakya Pandita's relationship with Godan Khan, 1244;
and Phakpa's with Kublai Khan as Imperial Preceptor, from ca. 1260),
then with the later Mongol khanates (Altan Khan and the Third Dalai
Lama, 1578 — the event that conferred the title "Dalai Lama" on
Sonam Gyatso), the Qing emperors (from Güshi Khan and the Fifth
Dalai Lama forward), and the Kalmyk, Buryat, and Tuvan Mongolian
peoples into the modern period.

### Mechanism

- **Guru-disciple lineages under tantric initiation.** Vajrayana
  Buddhism is organized around initiatory lineages in which specific
  teachings, empowerments, and sadhana practices are transmitted
  master-to-disciple in a chain back to the Indian mahasiddhas and
  ultimately to the Buddha. The disciple receives named transmissions
  from named teachers; the record is preserved in lineage histories
  (*lo-rgyus*, *chö-byung*). This is the most elaborated transmission
  architecture in any of the three regimes.
- **Monastic colleges (*shedra*) and ritual colleges (*dratsang*).**
  Large Tibetan monasteries are federations of colleges specialized
  in scholastic philosophy, tantric ritual, calendrical science, and
  medicine. The three great Gelug monasteries — Ganden, Sera, and
  Drepung, all near Lhasa — became the institutional core of Tibetan
  Buddhism, with satellite institutions across Tibet, Mongolia, and
  Inner Asia.
- **The tulku (*sprul-sku*) system.** From the 13th century forward,
  senior teachers were recognized as reincarnations of their
  predecessors. The tulku institution is the most distinctive
  Tibetan innovation in monastic succession: it decouples authority
  from hereditary lineage (as in some other monastic traditions) and
  from purely political selection, and instead grounds it in a
  reincarnation claim verified by a specified procedure involving
  prophecies, signs, and formal identification by senior teachers.
  The Dalai Lama and Panchen Lama are the most famous tulku lineages;
  there are thousands of others. This institution is crucial for the
  cross-polity analysis because *tulku identification crosses
  political boundaries* — a Dalai Lama can be identified in an area
  outside the polity the previous one governed, as happened
  repeatedly.
- **The *yön-chö* relationship across polities.** Sakya Pandita's
  relationship with Godan Khan established the template. The
  Tibetan lama supplies spiritual authority, the non-Tibetan
  patron supplies political protection and material support. The
  relationship does not subordinate the lama to the patron; in
  several instances (Phakpa's *yön-chö* with Kublai Khan; the
  Fifth Dalai Lama's with the Qing Shunzhi emperor) the lama held a
  recognized authority parallel to the political authority. This
  is the clearest cross-polity instantiation of a substrate-holder
  operating independent of the sovereign while being recognized by
  the sovereign.
- **Shared scholastic curriculum.** Across the four main schools
  (Nyingma, Kagyu, Sakya, Gelug) and across the Tibetan, Mongolian,
  Bhutanese, and Himalayan Buddhist polities, a common core
  curriculum — the Prajñaparamita literature, the Madhyamaka
  treatises, the Pramana (epistemology) corpus, the Abhidharma — is
  taught in Tibetan as the liturgical language, much as Pali works
  in the Theravada world and Chinese in East Asia.
- **Cross-school tantric transmission.** The *Ris-med* (non-sectarian)
  movement of the 19th century formalized a practice that had always
  existed: senior lamas of one school receive transmissions from
  senior lamas of another school, preserving the cross-school
  lineage portfolio against school-specific decay. This is the
  clearest instance of cross-compact transmission within a single
  regime.

### Output

For roughly 1000–1100 years (counting from the Second Transmission,
ca. 11th c., to the present), the Tibetan Vajrayana network has
sustained:

- A shared scriptural canon in Tibetan (the *Kangyur* — translated
  word of the Buddha, 108 volumes; the *Tengyur* — translated
  commentaries, 225 volumes) across Tibet, Bhutan, Sikkim, Ladakh,
  Mongolia, Tuva, Buryatia, and Kalmykia;
- A shared scholastic curriculum taught in Tibetan as liturgical
  language across all these polities;
- Mutual recognition of tulku identification, monastic ordination,
  and tantric transmission across all these polities;
- The *yön-chö* cross-polity authority relationship, sustained with
  varying effectiveness across eight centuries and multiple Inner
  Asian political formations;
- A continuing exile network after 1959, in which the Tibetan
  diaspora (India, Nepal, Bhutan, and globally) has preserved the
  full institutional apparatus and extended it through contact with
  non-Buddhist host societies.

### Decay

One major disruption, still in progress:

- **The 1950 PRC occupation and subsequent destruction.** The
  Chinese occupation of Tibet (1950), the failed 1959 uprising, the
  Cultural Revolution's systematic destruction of Tibetan Buddhist
  institutions (more than 6,000 monasteries destroyed in Tibet and
  neighboring regions), and the continuing PRC policy of controlling
  tulku recognition (the competing Panchen Lama identifications
  being the most visible instance) represent the most severe attack
  on any of the three Buddhist regimes in the modern period. The
  Tibetan diaspora under the Fourteenth Dalai Lama has preserved
  the institutional apparatus in exile, rebuilding Ganden, Sera, and
  Drepung in South India and continuing the full scholastic and
  ritual curriculum. The succession question (what happens on the
  Fourteenth Dalai Lama's death, with the PRC contesting
  succession) remains open.
- **Mongolian disruption (1920s–1990s).** The Soviet-aligned
  Mongolian People's Republic conducted purges (1937–39 especially)
  that destroyed most Mongolian Buddhist institutions and killed
  thousands of monks. Buriatia, Kalmykia, and Tuva were similarly
  suppressed. Post-1990 revival is in progress but institutional
  scope remains well below pre-1920s levels.
- **The Bhutanese and Sikkimese cases** have been comparatively
  stable, as has the Tibetan diaspora in India and Nepal.

Unlike the Theravada and East Asian regimes, the Tibetan regime
faces a still-acute existential stress. The cross-polity cooperation
mechanisms are working — the exile network sustains cross-polity
ordination, tulku recognition, and scholastic transmission across
India, Nepal, Bhutan, and global diaspora communities — but the
regime's center of gravity has been displaced from Tibet itself and
its continuity is actively contested by a major power.

### Lesson for kith-covenant

Three lessons, one shared with the Theravada and East Asian cases and
two distinct.

Shared: **a substrate-holder independent of the sovereign is
structurally necessary for sovereign-transcending cooperation.** The
*yön-chö* relationship is the Tibetan instantiation of this pattern;
*dhammaraja* is the Theravada instantiation; the separation of
Buddhist authority from state authority in East Asia is the East
Asian instantiation. All three regimes depend on a substrate-holder
that is recognized by but not subordinate to the political
authority.

Distinct (1): **the *yön-chö* relationship is the clearest historical
model of cross-compact substrate cooperation.** A substrate-bearer
in one community provides recognized authority to a polity in
another community, without either community's political authority
absorbing the other's substrate. This is close to what kith-covenant
needs at the inter-community recognition layer — compact-member
communities recognizing the substrate-authority of other compact
members without requiring substrate merger. The mechanism is
patron-priest rather than creed-convergence.

Distinct (2): **the tulku system is the most elaborated succession
mechanism in the case set**, and it is specifically designed to
handle cross-polity succession where political boundaries shift
under the succession window. Kith-Covenant's member communities will
face analogous succession problems (how does the next generation of
authorized representatives get recognized across polities whose
borders have moved, whose governments have changed, whose populations
have migrated?), and the tulku architecture — prophecy and signs plus
formal identification by senior peers plus confirmation by the
receiving community — is a working model even if the
reincarnation-specific content does not transfer.

## Cross-regime comparison on the Christian-cases metrics

The Christian cases in [historical_cases.md](historical_cases.md) are
evaluated on an implicit set of metrics: voluntariness, peer-ness,
longevity, cross-sovereign scope, institutional mechanism density,
substrate, and decay mode. Here are the three Buddhist regimes on
those metrics.

### Voluntariness

- **Theravada regime.** High. Ordination is voluntary both for the
  individual and for the monastery accepting a candidate from
  another polity. Royal patronage is sought but not structurally
  required (the 11th–19th century Sri Lankan re-ordinations happened
  *because* a local line had died out, and were arranged by sangha
  leaders and requested from peer polities). The Theravada regime
  has the weakest state-dependence among the three.
- **East Asian regime.** Mixed. Ordination certificates in imperial
  China were state-regulated from Tang forward; the Tokugawa temple
  registration system (*tera-uke seido*) conscripted Japanese
  Buddhism into state census and surveillance functions. But
  *cross-polity* recognition — Japanese pilgrimage to China for
  transmission, Korean training at Chinese monasteries, Chinese
  transmission to Japanese disciples — happened largely outside
  state regulation and was recognized by the state *after* the
  sangha network had accomplished it. The cross-polity voluntariness
  is high even where intra-polity voluntariness was constrained.
- **Tibetan regime.** Mixed, complicated by the *yön-chö* structure.
  The cross-polity relationships between Tibetan lamas and
  Mongolian, Qing, and Buryat polities were voluntary on both
  sides and negotiated rather than imposed. Intra-Tibetan monastic
  succession included political elements (the Gelug's rise under
  Güshi Khan involved military conquest of Tibet's rival schools)
  that qualify pure voluntariness; and the *yön-chö* relationship
  had coercive edges on occasion. Net: the Tibetan regime is less
  voluntary than the Theravada, on a par with or somewhat more
  voluntary than the East Asian.

Verdict on voluntariness: all three regimes have enough voluntariness
in the cross-polity dimension to qualify under the
noticeable_hand.md filter, though the Theravada regime is the
cleanest instance.

### Peer-ness

The Christian cases differ on peer-ness: Westphalia is peer
cooperation among sovereigns, the Hanse is peer cooperation among
cities, lex mercatoria is peer cooperation among merchants, the
Iroquois Confederacy is peer cooperation among nations under a
federal instrument. The Peace of God is different — an ecclesiastical
authority imposing categories of inviolability — and it sits between
peer cooperation and hierarchical authority.

- **Theravada regime.** Peer-like at the sangha level — monasteries
  across Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia are peers, recognizing each
  other's ordinations and supplying each other's quorum requirements
  when needed. At the lay-polity level the peer-ness is mediated:
  the Sinhalese and Burmese kingdoms cooperated via sangha exchange
  rather than as direct political peers.
- **East Asian regime.** Peer-like at the dharma-transmission level
  — Chinese, Korean, and Japanese Chan masters recognize each other
  and exchange transmission across polity boundaries as peers. At
  the polity level, the relationship was often asymmetric (China
  as scholastic source, Korea and Japan as peripheries receiving
  transmission), but this asymmetry is functional rather than
  hierarchical — it is more like the relationship between the
  Sorbonne and Oxford in the 13th century than between a metropolis
  and a colony. After the 13th century and particularly from the
  Obaku transmission forward, the transmission flows became
  bidirectional.
- **Tibetan regime.** Peer-like at the scholastic and tantric
  transmission level across the four schools and across the Tibetan,
  Bhutanese, Sikkimese, Ladakhi, and Mongolian polities. The
  *yön-chö* relationship is structurally asymmetric (spiritual
  authority from one side, political patronage from the other) but
  is not a subordination; the Tibetan lama retains authority within
  his own sphere even when he resides at the Mongol or Qing court.

Verdict on peer-ness: all three regimes are peer-cooperation-like at
the sangha level. The lay-polity cooperation they enable is mediated
through the sangha rather than being direct sovereign-to-sovereign
cooperation in the Westphalian sense. This is the most important
structural difference from the Christian cases, and it is the one
that most directly affects the Christian-particularist reading.

### Longevity

- **Theravada regime.** Roughly 2300 years of continuous operation,
  counting from Ashoka's mission to Sri Lanka (mid-3rd c. BCE) to
  the present. Continuous means that the higher-ordination lineage
  has been transmitted without total rupture; individual kingdoms
  have lost and re-received it, but the global Theravada line has
  not.
- **East Asian regime.** Roughly 1700 years of continuous operation
  within China (counting from the 4th-century translation projects),
  and roughly 1300 years as a multi-polity regime (counting from
  Japan's reception in the 6th century). Several near-rupture
  episodes (Huichang 845, Joseon suppression, Meiji, Cultural
  Revolution) but the cross-polity network survived each.
- **Tibetan regime.** Roughly 1000–1100 years, counting from the
  Second Transmission (11th c.) to the present. One major ongoing
  disruption (1950–present) that has displaced the center of
  gravity to exile.

All three are longer than all the Christian cases individually
(Westphalia ~270 years, Hanse ~300, lex mercatoria ~600, codes
duello ~300, Peace of God ~200 as movement). The Theravada is
longer than the Roman Catholic monastic orders combined (Benedictines
~1500 years).

Verdict on longevity: the Buddhist regimes are the longest-running
cross-polity cooperation institutions in the comparison set. This is
the sharpest single piece of evidence against the strong
Christian-particularist reading.

### Cross-sovereign scope

- **Theravada regime.** Sri Lanka, Myanmar, Thailand, Cambodia,
  Laos, southern Vietnam (historically), parts of southern China
  (Dai), historical trading-port presence in the Indonesian
  archipelago. All polities shared the Pali canon and the Theravada
  Vinaya.
- **East Asian regime.** China, Korea, Japan, Vietnam, and
  historically the Tangut and Khitan frontiers. All polities shared
  the Chinese canon and the Dharmaguptaka Vinaya.
- **Tibetan regime.** Tibet, Bhutan, Sikkim, Ladakh, Mongolia,
  Buryatia, Tuva, Kalmykia, and historically parts of the Qing Inner
  Asian frontier. All polities shared the Tibetan Kangyur/Tengyur
  and Tibetan liturgical language.

Verdict: all three regimes have comparable cross-sovereign scope to
the Westphalian system. None matches the global reach of the
post-1945 UN system, but none of the Christian cases does either
under the voluntary-peer filter.

### Institutional mechanism density

All three regimes have dense institutional mechanisms: shared canon
in a shared liturgical language, shared Vinaya or equivalent,
mutual recognition of ordination, documented transmission (highest
in East Asian), cross-polity pilgrimage and exchange, reconstitution
procedures, monastic colleges. This matches the Christian cases'
institutional density.

### Substrate

- **All three regimes.** The substrate is Buddhist — specifically
  the Dharma (Sanskrit *dharma*, Pali *dhamma*). The *structural
  feature* of the Buddhist substrate is contested relative to the
  "transcendent moral authority pointing to a single ultimate"
  formulation of
  [historical_cases.md](historical_cases.md#the-transcendent-moral-authority-pattern).
  Buddhism does not have a Creator God. What it has is the Dharma
  as *the nature of reality* that the Buddha *awakened to* (not
  invented, not given by a Creator — discovered). The Dharma is
  non-personal but is a single ultimate in the sense required for
  transcendent-moral-authority analysis: it is above any individual,
  above any community, above any polity, and it is what ethical
  commitments are ultimately answerable to. The Buddhist substrate
  is thus structurally analogous to the "single ultimate" feature
  without being theistically equivalent.
- The [transcendent moral authority pattern section](historical_cases.md#the-transcendent-moral-authority-pattern)
  claims that Buddhist Dharma "confirms the pattern" as a
  single ultimate; the present analysis supports that claim with
  the caveat that the single-ultimate feature is formally rather
  than theologically supplied. The Dharma is not "a god" in any
  recognizable sense. But it *does* perform the structural role
  that the substrate analysis requires.

### Decay mode

- **Theravada regime.** No terminal decay. Stress episodes (colonial
  disruption, Buddhist nationalism) are real but the regime
  continues.
- **East Asian regime.** Multiple severe stress episodes (Huichang,
  Meiji, Joseon, Cultural Revolution). No terminal decay — the
  cross-polity recognition machinery has survived every episode,
  often in reduced form.
- **Tibetan regime.** One major ongoing stressor (PRC occupation
  and continuing disruption). Exile network has preserved the
  apparatus. Succession of the Dalai Lama post-Fourteenth is the
  open question.

Verdict: no Buddhist regime has fully decayed. The Christian cases
Westphalia, Hanse, codes duello, and Peace of God have all
substantially decayed; lex mercatoria partially decayed and partially
revived; the Iroquois regime continues in altered form. The Buddhist
regimes' sustained operation through modern disruptions is a
distinctive feature.

## The five anti-spiral features applied to the sangha

The operative formulation from
[justus_hostis.md](justus_hostis.md#the-anti-spiral-features-why-anchor-above-self-is-necessary-but-not-sufficient)
specifies that a substrate must supply five features whose combination
blocks the purity-spiral failure mode: universal fallen-actor
anthropology; grace over merit; scapegoat-revelation or functional
equivalent; enemy-love as operational norm; non-ultimacy of the
political community.

### (1) Universal fallen-actor anthropology

**Verdict: strongly supplied.**

Buddhist anthropology holds that every sentient being is subject to
*dukkha* (unsatisfactoriness, suffering) rooted in *kleshas* (mental
afflictions — ignorance, craving, aversion, conceit, wrong view,
doubt, in the standard enumeration). Ignorance (*avidya*) is the root
klesha; it conditions craving; craving conditions becoming; becoming
conditions suffering. No sentient being escapes this condition until
full awakening, and even arhats in the Theravada reading — and more
pointedly, bodhisattvas on the long path in Mahayana — have only
partially overcome the kleshas.

The doctrine of anatta (no-self) deepens the feature: because there
is no substantial self to be "good" or "evil," the line between
wholesome and unwholesome action runs through the present act rather
than between persons. This is Solzhenitsyn's line ("the line dividing
good and evil cuts through the heart of every human being") in a
non-theistic register.

Crucially, Buddhist ethics explicitly rejects the tribal / in-group
anthropology that the anti-spiral feature is designed to foreclose.
The Brahmaviharas (metta, karuna, mudita, upekkha) are trained to
extend universally to all sentient beings without distinction.
Classical metta practice explicitly includes the exercise of
extending loving-kindness to enemies in sequence with friends and
strangers, the point being that the practice fails if one's metta
stops at the in-group boundary.

Buddhism therefore supplies the universal fallen-actor feature in a
different theoretical register (kleshas and anatta rather than
original sin and imago Dei) with the same structural function. The
feature is as robust in Buddhism as in Christianity, though the
anthropological framing is quite different.

### (2) Grace over merit

**Verdict: supplied, with significant variation across schools.**

This is the feature where Buddhism's internal diversity matters most.

- **Theravada** has the strongest merit-accumulation element. *Punna*
  (merit) is generated by generosity, ethical conduct, and meditation,
  and is explicitly transferable (to deceased relatives, for
  instance) and accumulative. A purely merit-driven reading of
  Theravada would fail the "grace over merit" feature. But that is
  not the operative Theravada reading. The doctrine of anatta
  undercuts any ultimately substantial attachment to "my merit."
  The Dhammapada is explicit: attachment even to one's own virtue is
  a defilement. Theravada's path is consummated by *nibbana*, which
  is not achieved by merit accumulation but by the cessation of
  craving — including craving for good outcomes.
- **Mahayana** extends the move substantially. *Bodhicitta* — the
  aspiration to awaken for the benefit of all sentient beings — is
  explicitly gratuitous in the sense that it is not earnable by
  prior merit; it arises as a response to the recognition of the
  suffering of others, and is cultivated through practices that
  diminish rather than increase self-referential merit-claims. The
  Mahayana doctrine of *sunyata* (emptiness) applied to merit itself
  empties merit of any self-standing reality; merit is empty of
  "my merit."
- **Pure Land** traditions (particularly Shin Buddhism in Japan)
  develop the feature in a form closest to Christian grace. Shinran
  (1173–1263) teaches *tariki* (other-power, specifically the power
  of Amida Buddha's vow) against *jiriki* (self-power, the path of
  merit accumulation). Awakening in the Pure Land is received
  through Amida's vow, not earned. Shin's *akunin shoki* ("evil
  person, proper object") teaching — that the evildoer is the
  proper object of Amida's saving vow before the good person —
  reads almost exactly as a Buddhist Pauline theology of grace. This
  is the closest structural analogue to the Christian grace doctrine
  in any non-Christian tradition.
- **Zen** and **Vajrayana** handle the feature through different
  registers. Zen's *shikantaza* or *kensho* is explicitly not a
  merit-accumulation achievement — it cannot be earned by effort
  alone; masters routinely dismiss students who approach practice
  as merit accumulation. Vajrayana's deity-yoga and guru-devotion
  practices are framed as receptive rather than generative: the
  practitioner receives the *blessing* (*adhishthana*) of the
  deity or the guru, which is not produced by the practitioner's
  effort but transmitted through it.

Across all forms the Buddhist handling of merit includes the
anti-competitive-virtue move that the feature requires. Virtue is
not a competitive status marker; attachment to one's own virtue is a
defilement; the path consummates in a release from the self that
could own the virtue.

The feature is supplied, differently from Christianity, at comparable
depth.

### (3) Scapegoat-revelation or functional equivalent

**Verdict: functional equivalent supplied, structurally different
from the Christian form.**

This is the feature where Christianity's form is most
distinctive — Girard's argument that the Cross reveals the scapegoat
mechanism *as* scapegoating by presenting the innocent victim as
innocent from God's point of view is a revelation story with specific
historical content. Buddhism does not have this story.

What Buddhism has is a structurally different disarmament of the
sacrificial mechanism. The mechanism Girard identifies depends on:
(a) mimetic rivalry producing social tension that (b) discharges
through (c) the selection of a scapegoat believed to be responsible
for the crisis, whose (d) destruction restores the community to
unanimity. The Christian revelation defuses (c)-(d) by making the
scapegoat's innocence explicit and theologically central.

Buddhism defuses the same mechanism at an earlier stage. The doctrine
of anatta — no substantial self, therefore no ultimately substantial
other — removes the self/other boundary that the scapegoat mechanism
requires for its target designation. If the apparently guilty
scapegoat is, from the awakened point of view, not substantially
different from the community expelling them, then the
expulsion-as-purification logic has no ontological purchase. The
universal-compassion practices (metta, karuna, and the bodhisattva
commitments) operationalize this: the practitioner trains the mind
not to generate the self-other separation that the scapegoat
mechanism requires.

Operationally, the disarming includes:

- **Metta to enemies.** The explicit practice of extending
  loving-kindness to those who have harmed oneself or one's
  community, trained as a formal meditation with the direct goal of
  undermining the in-group/out-group boundary.
- **The bodhisattva vow.** In Mahayana, the bodhisattva takes a vow
  to save all sentient beings without exception — explicitly
  including hell beings, explicitly including those who have
  committed the worst acts. The vow structurally forecloses any
  categorization of persons as "beyond compassion."
- **No eternal hell.** Buddhist cosmology has hells, but even the
  worst hells are not eternal — rebirth out of them is part of the
  structure of the cosmos. This denies the sacrificial mechanism
  its final move (the permanent designation of the scapegoat as
  beyond the community).
- **The Jataka literature.** The previous-lives stories of the
  Buddha routinely feature the bodhisattva sacrificing himself for
  others — including for enemies. The structure is different from
  the Cross (these are many voluntary sacrifices by the
  enlightenment-bound bodhisattva, not a single scapegoat
  revelation), but the moral content is close: the innocent one
  yields his life, and the story's telling elevates rather than
  conceals the innocent yielding.

Girard himself was ambivalent about the Buddhist case in his later
work, arguing that only the Christian Gospel fully exposes the
scapegoat mechanism because it presents an actual historical
scapegoat narrative revealed *as* scapegoating, rather than an
ontology that cuts below the mechanism. The ontological cut is
structurally different from the historical revelation, and reasonable
analysts differ on whether it is functionally equivalent or merely a
partial analogue.

The honest reading is: Buddhism supplies a real anti-sacrificial
structure that operates through different means than Christianity,
at meaningful depth, and the question of whether it is as
structurally robust as the Christian form remains live. The
justus_hostis.md formulation accepts Buddhist anatta + compassion as
a partial functional equivalent, and the present analysis supports
that position. The Buddhist Pure Land and Shin traditions are the
closest to the Christian form on this feature; Theravada is the
furthest.

### (4) Enemy-love as operational norm

**Verdict: strongly supplied.**

The practice of metta (loving-kindness) to enemies is one of the most
formally developed practices in the Buddhist tradition. The
*Metta Sutta* and the *Visuddhimagga*'s exposition of metta
meditation are explicit: the practice extends loving-kindness in
sequence — to oneself, to a benefactor, to a friend, to a neutral
person, to an enemy — and the practice is held to *fail* if the
practitioner cannot extend metta to the enemy. The enemy category
is load-bearing in the practice.

The Dhammapada's famous verse (5): *"Hatred does not cease by hatred,
but only by love. This is the eternal law."*

Mahayana extends the feature through the bodhisattva commitments
enumerated above: the bodhisattva has explicitly vowed to save all
beings including enemies, and the vow is not sentimental but
structural — the practitioner is to train the mind until the vow
becomes operational.

Tibetan *lojong* ("mind training") practices are organized around
converting apparent enemies into the primary teachers of the path,
on the view that an enemy who causes suffering is providing the
most direct opportunity to practice patience and compassion.
Chekhawa's *Seven Points of Mind Training* (12th c.) and its
commentaries are the most developed Buddhist corpus on this feature.

The feature is as strong in Buddhism as in Christianity, and on the
specific "enemy as teacher" framing, arguably more developed.

### (5) Non-ultimacy of the political community

**Verdict: strongly supplied.**

Buddhist renunciation tradition is structurally built on the
non-ultimacy of ordinary political and social life. The monk or nun
*leaves* the household (*pabbajja* — "going forth") in order to
pursue the Dharma; the act of renunciation is a declaration that the
household, the polity, and the ordinary social order are not the
ultimate.

The two-truths doctrine (*sammuti-sacca* / *paramattha-sacca* in
Theravada; *samvrti-satya* / *paramartha-satya* in Mahayana) codifies
the feature at the epistemic level: conventional reality — including
political reality — is not the ultimate truth. This is structurally
parallel to Augustine's two-cities distinction, reached through
different theoretical apparatus.

The Buddhist tradition has been operationally reminded of this
feature through the *dhammaraja* doctrine in Theravada and the
*yön-chö* relationship in Tibetan Vajrayana: the king or patron is
not the ultimate, he is the *servant* of the ultimate. When kings
overreach — when they attempt to absorb the substrate — the
tradition resists, sometimes at severe cost. Burmese and Thai
conflicts between kings and senior monks over Vinaya interpretation
are recurring, and the sangha has generally preserved its
independence even at cost. Tibetan resistance to the Qing and to the
PRC's attempts to control tulku recognition is the same feature
operating at a higher-stakes register.

The feature is strongly supplied.

### Overall verdict on the anti-spiral features

The sangha supplies the five anti-spiral features. The mapping is
not identical to the Christian form; features (1), (3), (4), and (5)
are supplied through different theoretical apparatus, and feature
(2) is supplied in a form that varies substantially across Buddhist
schools. But the load-bearing function of the features — blocking the
purity-spiral failure mode by foreclosing the psychological and
ontological moves the spiral requires — is performed.

The one feature where Christianity has a distinctive structural
advantage is (3), scapegoat-revelation. Buddhism supplies an
ontological disarming of the scapegoat mechanism; Christianity
supplies a historical-narrative revelation of it. These are not the
same thing, and the Christian form may be more robust against the
specific failure mode of weaponized scapegoating, because it
provides a direct counter-narrative ("behold the lamb of God, who
takes away the sin of the world") rather than an abstraction. But
this is a live question rather than a settled result.

The Buddhist nationalism failure mode in modern Sri Lanka and Myanmar
demonstrates that the features can be *bypassed* when the tradition
is weaponized as ethnic or political identity rather than received
as its own scriptural mandate. This is parallel to the Christian
colonial selectivity counterweight in
[noticeable_hand.md](noticeable_hand.md#the-colonial-frontier-counterweight).
Neither tradition's supplying of the features immunizes its nominal
carriers from betraying them. The features describe what the
substrate *makes available*; they do not describe what the substrate
guarantees.

## Verdict on the Christian-particularist reading

[noticeable_hand.md](noticeable_hand.md) distinguished three readings
of the cross-community cooperation evidence: Christian-particularist,
catholic/perennialist, and naturalist. The sangha evidence bears
directly on the choice among them.

### What the sangha evidence does to the strong Christian-particularist reading

The strong Christian-particularist reading holds that *only
Christianity* supplies the substrate for long-running voluntary
cross-community cooperation, that other traditions' apparent
successes are either (a) imperial arrangements misidentified as
voluntary, (b) short-lived, or (c) partial participation in the
same truth that Christianity articulates more completely.

**The sangha weakens this reading substantially.** All three Buddhist
regimes are real, voluntary (at the cross-polity level),
long-running (all longer than any Christian case individually), and
built on a substrate that supplies the anti-spiral features through
non-Christian theoretical apparatus. Move (a) fails: the regimes are
not primarily imperial. Move (b) fails: the regimes outlast the
Christian cases. Move (c) is available but requires a theological
move — the claim that Buddhism's supplying of the features indicates
its (partial) participation in a truth Christianity articulates more
completely — which is coherent within Christian theology (Justin
Martyr's *logos spermatikos*; *Lumen Gentium*'s treatment of
non-Christian religions) but is not derivable from the historical
evidence alone.

### What the sangha evidence does to the moderate Christian-particularist reading

The moderate Christian-particularist reading holds that
*Christianity supplies the most complete single combination of the
anti-spiral features, and produces the most numerous successful
voluntary cross-community cooperation regimes in the Western
record, while allowing that other traditions supply real partial
versions of the features.*

**The sangha is consistent with this reading and may even support
it.** On the five anti-spiral features, the sangha supplies (1),
(2), (4), and (5) at depth comparable to Christianity; it supplies
(3) at lesser depth through a structurally different mechanism. If
the Christian argument is that the *specific combination* of
features is most fully supplied by Christianity — not that
Christianity is the only source — the sangha's handling of (3) is
the evidence most relevant to that claim. The anti-sacrificial
disarmament in Buddhism operates ontologically; the Christian
disarmament operates historically-narratively; and the latter may
be more robust against specific failure modes.

### What the sangha evidence does to the "different kind of cooperation" claim

A sharper Christian-particularist move is to claim that the sangha
and the Christian cases are *different kinds of cooperation regimes*:
the sangha is a trans-polity monastic order that sustains itself
across polities that remain separate; the Christian cases
(Westphalia, Hanse, lex mercatoria, codes duello) are frameworks
through which polities cooperate with each other while remaining
separate. On this view the sangha is structurally more like the
Roman Catholic monastic orders than like Westphalia, and the
comparison with Westphalia is category-confused.

**This move is substantially correct and is the strongest form of
the Christian-particularist argument.** The sangha's mechanism is a
trans-polity religious community doing scholastic, ritual, and
credentialing work across polities, which is structurally closer to
the Benedictines than to the Hanse. The Christian tradition has
produced both kinds of cooperation regime — the trans-polity
religious orders (Benedictines, Cistercians, Jesuits, Society of
Jesus, and the papacy as an ecclesiastical authority) *and* the
cross-polity cooperation frameworks (Westphalia, Hanse, lex
mercatoria). The question for the Christian-particularist is
whether Buddhism has produced the *second kind* of regime, not just
the first.

The honest answer is that Buddhism has produced the first kind in
greater abundance and longer duration than Christianity, and the
second kind in weaker forms. The *yön-chö* relationship is the
closest Buddhist analogue to Westphalia; it is real but is narrower
in scope (Tibet and its Inner Asian patrons rather than the full
Christendom). There is no Buddhist Hanseatic League, no Buddhist
lex mercatoria, no Buddhist codes duello.

### Net verdict

The sangha is the sharpest counterweight available to the strong
Christian-particularist reading. It weakens that reading
substantially but does not refute it. It is consistent with the
moderate Christian-particularist reading and with the catholic /
perennialist reading. It does not support the naturalist reading,
because the Buddhist regimes rest on a substrate that meets the
"single ultimate" structural requirement through the Dharma, not
on the absence of such a substrate.

The strongest Christian-particularist formulation consistent with
the sangha evidence is:

> Christianity supplies the most complete combination of the
> anti-spiral features, with feature (3) (scapegoat-revelation) in
> its most structurally robust form. Christianity has produced the
> greatest density of voluntary cross-polity cooperation *frameworks*
> (Westphalia, Hanse, lex mercatoria, codes duello, Peace of God,
> papal jurisdiction) among separate sovereigns; Buddhism has
> produced the longest-running voluntary cross-polity *communities*
> (Theravada, East Asian, Tibetan Vajrayana). These are different
> kinds of achievement. Christianity's cross-polity-framework
> achievement is what kith-covenant most directly needs to learn
> from; Buddhism's cross-polity-community achievement is a real and
> durable alternative form that kith-covenant should also learn
> from.

This is a narrower but more defensible Christian-particularist
position than the one
[noticeable_hand.md](noticeable_hand.md#the-hypothesis-stated-in-its-strongest-form)
initially stated. The present document's empirical work makes this
narrowing honest rather than concessive.

## Lessons for kith-covenant

Four lessons specific to this document (in addition to the
regime-specific lessons above):

First, **the substrate requirement for kith-covenant members should
remain anti-spiral-feature-based rather than theology-based.** The
sangha evidence confirms that the features can be supplied from
multiple theological sources, and the features rather than the
sources are what the cooperation regime requires. This is the
formulation already operative in
[justus_hostis.md](justus_hostis.md#the-anti-spiral-features-why-anchor-above-self-is-necessary-but-not-sufficient);
the present document supplies additional evidence for it.

Second, **kith-covenant should recognize that it is designing a
cross-polity cooperation framework (closer to Westphalia than to
the Benedictines) and should therefore draw most directly from the
Christian-frameworks record for its design**. The Buddhist regimes'
achievement is a trans-polity community form that is less directly
applicable to kith-covenant's goal. This is not a dismissal of the
Buddhist evidence; it is recognition that the Buddhist evidence's
greatest relevance is to a different compact type than kith-covenant
is.

Third, **kith-covenant should incorporate the pilgrimage-for-
credentialing pattern from the East Asian Buddhist case and the
*yön-chö* substrate-holder-across-polities pattern from the Tibetan
case**. Both are direct architectural contributions to the
recognition protocols. The former operationalizes cross-compact
training exchange; the latter operationalizes substrate authority
that crosses political boundaries without merging substrates.

Fourth, **kith-covenant should expect the anti-spiral features to
fail in practice even when they are supplied by the substrate in
principle**. The Buddhist nationalism cases, like the Christian
colonial selectivity cases, demonstrate that the features can be
bypassed by nominal carriers. The compact's credentialing and
recognition protocols
([recognition_protocols.md](recognition_protocols.md)) should
therefore check for ongoing operation of the features, not just
for the substrate's in-principle supply of them. This is already
operative in the two-layer recognition architecture; the present
document supplies additional evidence that the ongoing check is
load-bearing.

## Open questions

- The scapegoat-revelation feature is the hardest to evaluate
  cross-traditionally. Girard's late position that only Christianity
  fully reveals the mechanism is contested; the Buddhist ontological
  disarming is structurally different. What additional evidence
  (comparative historical analysis of sacrificial-crisis moments
  across Buddhist and Christian societies) would distinguish the
  claims more cleanly?
- The Pure Land (Shin) tradition appears to supply grace-over-merit
  in the form most directly analogous to Christian grace. Does it
  also supply a scapegoat-revelation analogue — Amida's vow as a
  structural revelation of the unworthiness-of-merit dynamic that
  drives the scapegoat mechanism? This is a non-trivial
  theological-comparative question that would repay direct treatment.
- The *yön-chö* relationship is the clearest cross-polity substrate
  authority model in the case set. Are there Christian historical
  analogues (the medieval papacy's relationship with Christian
  kings; specific abbot-king relationships) that would repay direct
  comparison with *yön-chö* on the cross-polity-authority
  dimension?
- The Buddhist nationalism failure mode (Sinhala, Burmese, elements
  of Thai and Mongolian nationalism) is structurally parallel to the
  Christian colonial selectivity failure mode. What is the
  mechanism by which an anti-spiral-feature-supplying substrate
  produces these failure modes in practice, and what does that
  mechanism tell kith-covenant about the ongoing anti-spiral-features
  check?
- The three Buddhist regimes (Theravada, East Asian, Tibetan) are
  operationally separate after roughly the 11th century. Do they
  *recognize* each other as analogous regimes — does a Theravada
  monastic leader recognize a Tibetan tulku as playing a comparable
  role in his tradition? If not, what does that say about
  cross-compact recognition generally?
- Is the Roman Catholic monastic orders' record (Benedictines
  ~1500 years; papal authority over monastic orders across
  Christendom ~1500 years) better analogized to the Buddhist
  regimes than to the Christian cross-polity cooperation
  frameworks? A direct comparison of the Benedictines with the
  Theravada monastic network would sharpen the trans-polity-community
  vs. cross-polity-framework distinction.

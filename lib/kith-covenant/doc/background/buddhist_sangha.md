# The Buddhist Sangha as Voluntary Cross-Community Cooperation

## Why this document exists

[noticeable_hand.md](noticeable_hand.md) applied a voluntary-peer +
longevity filter to the case set in
[historical_cases.md](historical_cases.md) and identified the Buddhist
*sangha* as "the single sharpest challenge to the strong Christian
reading": the longest-running voluntary cross-community network in
the dataset, non-theistic in its philosophical core, and obviously not
Christian. The open question at the end of that document asked for a
serious analysis of the sangha on the same metrics the Christian cases
are evaluated on. This document is that analysis.

The document reaches four principal findings, stated up front so the
detailed treatment can be read against them:

1. **Although the sangha is a real case of voluntary
   cross-community cooperation, it is a different *kind* of case
   than Westphalia or the Hanse.** It is a trans-polity
   religious order as much as it is a cooperation regime among
   polities that stay separate. The closer Christian analogue is
   not Westphalia but the Roman Catholic monastic orders, or the
   medieval papacy as a cross-sovereign ecclesiastical
   authority.
2. **Treated at this properly specified scale, the sangha splits into
   three distinct regimes with distinct durations, mechanisms, and
   decay patterns**: the Theravada monastic network across Sri Lanka
   and mainland Southeast Asia, the East Asian Buddhist network
   across China, Korea, and Japan, and the Tibetan Vajrayana network
   across the Tibetan plateau and the Inner Asian steppe. Each has
   lasted between ~1100 and ~2300 years. Each has worked through
   different mechanisms. Each has a different relationship to
   political power.
3. **The sangha supplies the five anti-spiral features
   ([justus_hostis.md](justus_hostis.md#the-anti-spiral-features-why-anchor-above-self-is-necessary-but-not-sufficient)),
   substantially on (1), (2), (4), and (5), and in
   functional-but-not-identical form on (3)**. This is a genuine
   result. Buddhism is not a partial or degraded substrate on
   the anti-spiral features. It supplies them, in recognizably
   different form, with comparable depth to Christianity on most
   of them and narrower scope on the scapegoat-revelation
   feature where the form of Christianity is structurally
   distinctive.
4. **The verdict on the Christian-particularist reading is mixed
   and specific**. The strong form ("only Christianity can do
   the load-bearing work") is weakened by the sangha case. The
   weaker form ("Christianity supplies the most complete single
   combination of the features while other traditions supply
   real partial versions") survives and is consistent with the
   sangha evidence. The strongest move of the
   Christian-particularist reading, the claim that the *specific
   kind* of cross-polity cooperation Christendom produced
   (Westphalia, Hanse, lex mercatoria) has no close Buddhist
   analogue, also survives, but at the cost of conceding that
   the kind of cross-polity cooperation is a real and durable
   achievement of a different form.

The document proceeds through a methodological note; three regime
analyses on the Christian-cases template (founding context, mechanism,
output, decay, lesson); a cross-regime comparison on the metrics used
for the Christian cases; the five anti-spiral features applied to the
sangha; a verdict on the Christian-particularist and catholic readings;
lessons for kith-covenant; open questions; and references.

## Methodological note. What is being compared

The Christian cases in [historical_cases.md](historical_cases.md) share
a specific structural feature. They are regimes in which *separate
polities*, each with its own sovereign and its own institutions,
cooperate across their boundaries on a framework they jointly
recognize. Westphalia is peer cooperation among sovereigns.
The Hanse is peer cooperation among commercial cities. Lex mercatoria
is cross-polity law among merchants. The Iroquois Confederacy is peer
cooperation among nations under a shared federal instrument.

The sangha does not straightforwardly fit this shape. It is
simultaneously:

- **A trans-polity institution.** A single religious community whose
  members (monks and nuns under the Vinaya) share an identity that
  persists across sovereign boundaries. Analogously, one does not
  describe the Benedictine order or the Society of Jesus as
  "cross-polity cooperation among the communities they inhabit".
  They are themselves cross-polity institutions.
- **A mechanism of cross-community cooperation.** Lay Buddhist
  communities in separate polities (a Sinhalese kingdom and a Burmese
  kingdom, a Tang-dynasty Chinese court and a Heian-period Japanese
  court, a Tibetan principality and a Mongol khanate) really did
  cooperate with each other *via* shared sangha recognition,
  pilgrimage networks, text transmission, and ordination lineages.
  The sangha was the medium through which the lay communities
  cooperated.

These two modes are entangled and cannot be cleanly separated. For
the kith-covenant comparison, this has two consequences.

First, **the raw "longevity" comparison is misleading** if the
sangha is treated as the same kind of thing as Westphalia.
Westphalia's ~270 years is measured as the duration of a
cooperation *framework* among polities that remained separate.
The sangha's ~2500 years is measured as the duration of a
cross-polity *order* whose members were part of the same
community across those polities. The Catholic monastic orders
are the closer comparison: the Benedictines (since ca. 529 CE)
have ~1500 years. The Cistercians (since 1098) have ~900 years.
The Dominicans and Franciscans (since the 13th c.) have ~800
years. Scaled this way, the lead of the sangha narrows but
remains real. And the comparison with Westphalia becomes a
comparison between unlike kinds.

Second, **the parts of the sangha record that are most directly
kith-covenant-relevant are the episodes in which the
trans-polity institution worked as an instrument of cooperation
between polities that were genuinely separate**. These episodes
are real and substantial: Sri Lankan re-ordination by Burmese
and Thai monks in the 11th, 18th, and 19th centuries, each time
re-establishing a higher ordination line that had died out
locally; Chinese-Japanese-Korean monastic pilgrimage and dharma
transmission across imperial boundaries for more than a
millennium. The Tibet-Mongol *yön-chö* (patron-priest)
relationship that sustained cross-polity authority without
political subjugation for several centuries. These are the
episodes on which the sangha most directly bears on the design
question of kith-covenant.

One note on voluntariness is due. Some of the durability of the sangha
has rested on royal patronage, state Buddhism, and
state-enforced monastic regulation. Chinese state control of
ordination certificates from Tang forward, the Tokugawa temple
registration system, the Thai *Sangharaja* and state monastic
bureaucracy. Inside these episodes the sangha is less a
voluntary network than a state-licensed institution. But its
*cross-sovereign* recognition (the ordination of a Japanese
Rinzai monk is recognized at a Chinese Linji temple, the
ordination of a Sri Lankan Theravada monk is recognized at a
Thai *wat*) is not itself a state product. The states have
recognized what the sangha was already doing across their
borders, rather than constituting it. This is the sense in which
the sangha qualifies as voluntary cross-community cooperation,
even though it has often been bound up with state power inside
any particular polity.

A further note on scope. This document treats the three regimes
(Theravada, East Asian, Tibetan Vajrayana) as genuinely distinct
rather than three faces of a single institution. After roughly
the 11th century these regimes are practically separate. A
Theravada monk in Sri Lanka does not share ordination lineage
with a Japanese Zen monk, cannot serve as the tenth monk in a
Tibetan ordination quorum, recites a different Vinaya recension,
and has no institutional contact with the Tibetan hierarchy. The
regimes share a common root (the Indian sangha of the Buddha
himself, the early councils, and the Ashokan missionary
expansion) and a common lineage ideology (unbroken transmission
from the Buddha), though they are operationally independent
networks. Treating them as one institution would overstate the
longevity of any single cooperation regime. The comparable
Christian move would be to treat Orthodox, Catholic, and
Protestant Christianity as one institution to claim 2000 years
of continuous Christian cooperation. The comparison is cleaner
when each regime is evaluated on its own.

## Regime I: The Theravada monastic network

### Founding context

The Theravada tradition traces itself to the Sthaviravāda
("Teaching of the Elders") school, one of the early divisions of
the Indian sangha that hardened after the Second Council (ca.
383 BCE, Vesali) and again after the Third Council (ca. 250 BCE,
Pataliputra under Ashoka). The missionary sending by Ashoka
(recorded in the Sri Lankan *Mahavamsa* and corroborated by the
Edicts of Ashoka) dispatched nine missions to peripheral
regions, most durably the mission of Mahinda to Sri Lanka
(mid-3rd c. BCE) and (in later tradition) the mission of Sona
and Uttara to Suvannabhumi (probably Lower Burma / Thai-Malay
peninsula).

Sri Lanka is the hinge. The *Mahavihara* at Anuradhapura
preserved the Pali canon and the Theravada monastic lineage
continuously from the ordination by Mahinda of the Sinhalese
king Devanampiya Tissa (ca. 247 BCE) onward. The Pali canon was
committed to writing at the Fourth Council (ca. 29 BCE,
Aluvihare, Sri Lanka), one of the earliest substantial
scriptural corpora in any tradition to be written down as a
fixed text. From Sri Lanka, Theravada expanded to mainland
Southeast Asia (Burma, Thailand, Cambodia, Laos) in waves over
the first and second millennia CE, displacing earlier Hindu,
Mahayana, and tantric Buddhist presences in most of the region
by the 15th century.

By the high medieval period the Theravada world comprised a set
of distinct Buddhist kingdoms: Sri Lankan polities, Pagan and
its successors in Burma, Sukhothai and Ayutthaya in Thailand,
Angkor and later Cambodian states, and Lan Xang in Laos. They
were united by a shared Pali canon, a shared Vinaya recension,
and mutually recognized higher ordination. This is the Theravada
cross-community cooperation regime.

### Mechanism

Several institutional elements carry the cooperation load:

- **Shared Vinaya.** The monastic rule of 227 precepts for monks
  (*bhikkhus*) is recited in Pali at the fortnightly *uposatha*
  ceremony, identically in every Theravada monastery.
  Transgressions against the rule are confessed collectively.
  The content of the Vinaya, its language of recitation, and
  procedural form are constant across the Theravada world.
- **The Pali canon.** The *Tipitaka* (Vinaya, Sutta, Abhidhamma)
  is preserved in Pali across all Theravada polities. Scriptural
  disputes among Theravada kingdoms are argued against a common
  text in a common liturgical language that is the vernacular of
  nobody, an important neutralization mechanism.
- **Higher ordination across polities.** *Upasampada* (higher
  ordination) requires a quorum of ten fully ordained monks in a
  properly constituted *sima* (boundary). In border regions, five
  suffice. The act is performed in Pali, the rules of procedure are
  identical everywhere, and the resulting ordination is recognized
  throughout the Theravada world. A monk ordained in Thailand is a
  monk in Sri Lanka without re-ordination.
- **Re-ordination missions.** Three times in Sri Lankan history the
  local higher-ordination lineage died out (warfare, persecution,
  demographic collapse). Each time it was re-established by inviting
  a quorum of monks from another Theravada country: from Burma in the
  11th century under Vijayabahu I. From Thailand in 1753 under
  Kirti Sri Rajasinha (establishing the Siyam Nikaya, the largest
  modern Sri Lankan monastic fraternity). From Burma in 1802
  (Amarapura Nikaya) and 1864 (Ramanna Nikaya). These episodes are
  the direct empirical instances of the sangha working as a
  cross-polity cooperation mechanism: when a local community loses
  the line, the peer community across the sea supplies the
  reconstitution.
- **Pilgrimage networks.** Bodh Gaya, the Sri Lankan *Sri Pada*, the
  Burmese Shwedagon, and the Kandy *Dalada Maligawa* (Temple of the
  Sacred Tooth) are supra-polity pilgrimage sites. Royal embassies
  carrying relics between Buddhist courts (the Sri Lankan Tooth
  Relic, Burmese tooth relics, Thai Buddha images) were a durable
  channel of cross-polity diplomacy conducted through sangha
  intermediaries rather than through lay diplomatic corps.
- **Royal patron as protector-not-sovereign.** The Theravada
  doctrine of kingship frames the king as the protector of the
  *sasana* (the dispensation of Buddhist teaching) and as a
  *dhammaraja* (righteous king) whose legitimacy is conditional
  on that protection. Kings convoke councils, enforce Vinaya
  among corrupt monks, and patronize monasteries. They do not
  authoritatively determine the Vinaya or the canon. A Burmese
  king cannot reform the Vinaya. He can only enforce it. This
  keeps the substrate out of the hands of any one sovereign.

### Output

The Theravada regime has sustained, for roughly two millennia across
separate kingdoms:

- A single transmitted canon in a single liturgical language across a
  region spanning modern Sri Lanka, Myanmar, Thailand, Cambodia,
  Laos, and (historically) parts of southern Vietnam, southern
  China (Yunnan Dai), and Indonesian trading ports;
- Mutual recognition of ordination across all these polities, making
  monk-exchange for reconstitution or training a standing
  institutional possibility;
- A shared pilgrimage geography and relic-movement network that
  functioned as an informal diplomatic channel between Buddhist
  kingdoms;
- A shared canon of commentaries (Buddhaghosa's *Visuddhimagga*,
  fifth century CE, became and remains the standard scholastic
  reference across the Theravada world);
- A repeated pattern of mutual reconstitution when a local lineage
  collapsed, as the Sri Lankan re-ordination episodes demonstrate.

The regime did not produce Westphalian political cooperation among
Theravada kingdoms (they warred with each other in the ordinary way;
Burma sacked Ayutthaya in 1767) but it did produce a substrate of
cross-polity religious, scholastic, and scriptural cooperation that
survived those wars and that continues to function.

### Decay

The Theravada regime has not decayed in the sense Westphalia or the
Hanse decayed. It has been stressed repeatedly, and two stress
episodes deserve naming:

- **Colonial disruption** (18th-20th c.). British colonial
  government in Ceylon and Burma, French in Indochina, and Dutch
  elsewhere each reduced the pre-colonial *dhammaraja* state
  support for the sangha, creating periods of institutional
  weakness. But colonial rule did not destroy the sangha. While
  it damaged the state-sangha relationship, it left the
  cross-polity mechanisms largely intact. Thai monastic reform
  (Mongkut's Dhammayutika Nikaya, mid-19th c.) and the Burmese
  revival under the *Sangharaja* traditions were contemporaneous
  responses.
- **Buddhist nationalism** (20th-21st c.). This is the more
  serious stressor and the one that speaks directly to the
  anti-spiral question below. In Sri Lanka, Sinhala-Buddhist
  nationalism fused Theravada identity with ethnic claims,
  producing the long civil war against the Tamil minority
  (1983-2009) and a pattern of anti-Muslim violence that
  continues. In Myanmar, the Ma Ba Tha and the 969 Movement have
  supplied the ideological framework for state violence against
  the Rohingya (the 2017 clearance operations being the most
  visible instance). These are betrayals of the anti-spiral
  features the tradition otherwise supplies, and they are not
  trivial. They are mass-violence episodes in which Buddhist
  identity functioned as an in-group marker against designated
  out-groups. The parallel with Christian colonial selectivity
  (discussed in
  [noticeable_hand.md](noticeable_hand.md#counterweights)) is
  close. The scriptural mandate of the substrate itself
  (universal compassion. The Brahmaviharas including metta to
  enemies) was explicitly violated by its nominal carriers.
  Whether this is a decay of the cross-polity cooperation regime
  or a failure inside particular polities is arguable. The
  cross-polity ordination and canon mechanisms continue in
  parallel.

### Lesson for kith-covenant

Four lessons.

First, **a cross-polity regime that works through shared textual
canon, shared liturgical language, and shared ordination lineage
can sustain mutual recognition across sovereigns and across
millennia even when the sovereigns are at war with each other**.
Even while Burmese and Thai armies were destroying the cities of
the other, the Theravada regime kept producing valid monks in
both. The analogy for kith-covenant is that the cross-compact
recognition machinery should be structurally decoupled from the
operational cooperation among member communities, so that the
recognition machinery survives operational disputes.

Second, **the re-ordination pattern is a useful precedent for
the reconstitution question of kith-covenant**
([exit_and_succession.md](exit_and_succession.md)). When a
member community loses the local capacity to produce properly
credentialed successors (analogue of Sri Lanka losing the
*upasampada* lineage), a peer community across the boundary can
supply the reconstitution, provided the cross-polity recognition
machinery is kept working for exactly this purpose.

Third, **the *dhammaraja* principle is a working model of how to
keep a substrate out of the hands of any single sovereign.** The
king protects the substrate. He does not own it. If reforms
purport to come from royal authority, they are legitimate only
where the sangha recognizes them as consistent with the Vinaya.
This is directly parallel to the constitution of categories in
the Peace of God of inviolability by ecclesiastical authority
against lordly violence
([historical_cases.md](historical_cases.md#the-peace-of-god-and-truce-of-god)).
A substrate-holder independent of the sovereign is a structural
necessity for sovereign-transcending cooperation.

Fourth, **Buddhist nationalism is the anti-spiral failure mode
operative inside an otherwise robust substrate**, and the
kith-covenant compact cannot assume that supplying the
anti-spiral features guarantees their application. The features
make the failure mode avoidable. They do not make it impossible.
The compact must expect occasional betrayal and must include the
anti-spiral-features credentialing check
([recognition_protocols.md](recognition_protocols.md)) as an
ongoing rather than one-time examination.

## Regime II: The East Asian Buddhist network

### Founding context

Mahayana Buddhism entered China via the Silk Road in the first
century CE, with gradual institutional consolidation through the
Three Kingdoms, Jin, Northern and Southern Dynasties, and Tang
periods. The key translation phase runs from Kumarajiva (344-413) to
Xuanzang (602-664) and Yijing (635-713), during which the Chinese
Buddhist canon was assembled from successive Sanskrit and Prakrit
translations, reworked in Chinese idiom, and stabilized into what
became the East Asian Mahayana scriptural corpus.

The East Asian regime is a network of schools rather than a single
monastic lineage: Tiantai, Huayan, Pure Land, Chan (Zen in
Japanese, Seon in Korean, Thien in Vietnamese), Shingon/Mijiao, and
Vinaya schools. These schools share the Chinese Buddhist canon (the
*Dazangjing*), the Chinese Vinaya recension (the
*Dharmaguptaka Vinaya*, the *Sifen Lü*, the most widely used Vinaya
across China, Korea, Japan, and Vietnam), and the practice of
formal lineage transmission documented in written certificates. Over
the next millennium the network extended from China into Korea
(Goguryeo, Baekje, Silla, Goryeo, Joseon), Japan (transmission from
Korea in the 6th century, then direct Chinese transmission in
multiple waves), Vietnam (*Thien* from China), and (more
peripherally) into the Tangut and Tibetan frontiers.

What binds this into a cross-polity cooperation regime is the
practice of *pilgrimage for dharma transmission.* Monks from
peripheral polities travel to China (or, in the earlier period,
from China to India) to receive transmission, to train in
established monasteries, to copy or receive scriptural
authority, and to return to their home polities with recognized
credentials. This pattern runs from the India pilgrimage of
Xuanzang (629-645) through the nine-year pilgrimage of Ennin to
Tang China (838-847), the Song China pilgrimage of Dogen
(1223-1227), and many others into the Ming and Qing periods.

### Mechanism

- **The shared East Asian Buddhist canon.** The *Dazangjing* (in
  multiple successive imperial editions: Kaibao, Qisha, Zhaocheng,
  various Korean *Tripitaka Koreana* editions, Japanese editions)
  provided a common scriptural reference across polities. The
  Goryeo Tripitaka (13th c.), carved on 81,258 wooden blocks at
  Haeinsa, remains the most complete surviving premodern East Asian
  Buddhist canon and was consulted across East Asia.
- **The *Sifen Lü* (Dharmaguptaka Vinaya) as shared monastic rule.**
  While Theravada uses the Theravada Vinaya, all major East Asian
  Buddhist schools use the *Sifen Lü* for ordination, making
  ordination lineage in principle compatible across China, Korea,
  Japan, and Vietnam.
- **Dharma transmission certificates (*yinke*, *inka*).** Chan/Zen
  transmission is explicitly documented. The disciple receives a
  written certification from the master, and the lineage chart
  identifies the unbroken transmission back through named teachers
  to Bodhidharma and ultimately to the Buddha. The certificate is
  recognized across polities. A Japanese Rinzai monk with inka from
  a Chinese Linji master is a recognized Chan teacher in China. The
  institution of documented lineage transmission is more developed in
  East Asian Buddhism than in any of the other three regimes under
  examination.
- **Pilgrimage as the cross-polity cooperation instrument.** The
  paradigmatic episodes (the India journey of Xuanzang, the
  nine-year residence of Ennin in Tang China, the two years of
  Dogen in Song China, the serial Korean pilgrimages to China
  through the Goryeo period, the continuing Japanese Obaku
  transmission from Ming-Qing Fujian) are instances of the
  cross-polity cooperation regime at work. A monk travels, is
  received, is trained, is examined, receives transmission,
  returns. The receiving polity bears the training cost. The
  sending polity receives the credentialed teacher. The
  cooperation is real, routine, and sustained across sovereigns
  that were often at war (Tang China and Tibet; Ming China and
  Japan. And so on).
- **Text exchange and mutual correction of canon.** Ennin brought
  some 800 scrolls of text and ritual manuals back from Tang China;
  Uichon brought thousands of volumes of Huayan and Tiantai texts
  from Song China back to Goryeo. The later Japanese Obaku
  transmission brought substantial Ming-Qing Chan materials to
  Tokugawa Japan. The canon was, over centuries, collectively
  curated across polities through these exchanges.
- **Recognition of credentialed visiting teachers.** the arrival
  of Ingen Ryuki in Japan (1654) with a group of Chinese Obaku
  monks led to the establishment of the Obaku school as a
  recognized Japanese Zen lineage, with Japanese disciples
  receiving transmission from Chinese masters on Japanese soil.
  This is direct instantiation of the cross-polity recognition
  machinery.

### Output

For roughly 1300 years (5th c. CE through the modern disruptions
below), the East Asian Buddhist network sustained:

- A shared scriptural canon revised collectively across polities;
- Shared liturgical Chinese (*Hanmun* in Korea, *Kanbun* in Japan)
  as a neutral scholastic language across Korean, Japanese,
  Vietnamese, and other peripheral polities;
- Mutual recognition of ordination and dharma transmission across
  sovereigns;
- Pilgrimage networks that survived dynastic transitions (Sui to
  Tang, Tang to Song, Song to Yuan, Yuan to Ming, Ming to Qing, and
  the parallel Korean and Japanese transitions), producing continuous
  cross-polity training exchange;
- A scholastic tradition (Tiantai, Huayan) whose commentarial
  apparatus was collectively developed across polities.

### Decay

Multiple major disruption episodes, but none total:

- **The Huichang persecution (845 CE).** Emperor Wuzong of Tang
  moved against Buddhism: ~4,600 monasteries destroyed, ~40,000
  smaller shrines, ~260,000 monks and nuns defrocked. The
  persecution was Tang-state-wide but did not reach Korea and
  Japan, where East Asian Buddhism continued. Though Chinese
  Buddhism recovered under the successor to Wuzong and later
  dynasties, several Chinese schools never fully recovered their
  Tang-period scope.
- **The Mongol conquest (13th c.).** Northern Chinese Buddhism
  was disrupted by the conquest and by the preferential
  patronage of the Yuan dynasty of Tibetan Vajrayana over
  Chinese schools, shifting institutional weight within East
  Asia.
- **The Meiji *shinbutsu bunri* (1868) and subsequent state Shinto.**
  The Japanese state forcibly separated Shinto from Buddhism,
  nationalized Shinto, and marginalized Buddhism. Some 40,000
  Buddhist temples were destroyed or repurposed in the early Meiji
  period. Japanese Buddhism survived but lost its integration with
  the state and with Shinto. It has not recovered the institutional
  density of the pre-Meiji period.
- **The Joseon-dynasty suppression in Korea (14th-19th c.).**
  Neo-Confucian Joseon reduced Buddhist institutions, confined them
  mostly to mountain monasteries, and restricted monastic ordination.
  Korean Buddhism persisted but in substantially reduced
  institutional form for five centuries.
- **The Cultural Revolution (1966-76).** Most remaining Chinese
  Buddhist institutions were destroyed or repurposed, monks and
  nuns laicized or imprisoned, texts burned. Partial recovery began
  after 1979 but institutional scope is still well below
  pre-1949 levels.
- **Vietnamese 20th-century disruption.** French colonial rule,
  the wars of independence, and the post-1975 communist state all
  reduced the Vietnamese sangha. It persists and has revived but
  in reduced form.

Through all these episodes, *the cross-polity recognition
machinery did not fully collapse*. Japanese Zen lineages
preserved Chinese transmission when Chinese Chan was under
persecution; Korean Seon preserved Chinese and Japanese
contacts. Modern exile and post-communist revival rely on the
same cross-polity mechanisms the network has always used. The
East Asian network has sustained its cross-polity cooperation
function through multiple total political inversions: Tang to
Song, Song to Yuan, Yuan to Ming, Ming to Qing, Qing to Republic
to PRC; Goryeo to Joseon; Heian to Kamakura to Muromachi to Edo
to Meiji. This durability through political inversion is the
most characteristic feature of the regime.

### Lesson for kith-covenant

Three lessons.

First, **a cross-polity cooperation regime organized around
pilgrimage-for-credentialing is a robust architectural
pattern**. A credential-issuing community of recognized
authority, accessible across boundaries, that receives
candidates from peripheral polities and sends them back
credentialed, is a pattern the East Asian Buddhist network
instantiated for over a millennium. The modern parallel is the
doctoral-degree network among universities internationally. The
recognition protocols of Kith-Covenant
([recognition_protocols.md](recognition_protocols.md)) already
include a credentialing layer. The East Asian Buddhist case is
an argument for making that layer first-class and for providing
pilgrimage-style training exchange as a mechanism by which
communities can send and receive credentialed representatives.

Second, **documented lineage transmission is a structural
innovation worth adopting**. The *inka* / *yinke* certificate,
combined with the lineage chart that names every teacher back to
the founder, is a credential that carries across polities
without requiring a central authority. The members and mediators
of Kith-Covenant might plausibly operate under analogous
documented-transmission credentials. Recognition certificates
naming the chain from the current bearer back through named
predecessors to the founding of the compact.

Third, **the durability of the regime through political
inversion is worth studying directly**. Every East Asian polity
that hosted this network went through at least one total
political discontinuity (dynastic overthrow, colonial
imposition, revolutionary state), and the network carried
through each of them, often with institutional damage but
without losing the cross-polity recognition machinery itself.
Kith-Covenant should design for political inversion of this kind
(the state of a member polity going to pieces and being replaced
by something else) as a routine expected event rather than a
catastrophic failure.

## Regime III: The Tibetan Vajrayana network

### Founding context

Buddhism entered Tibet in two waves. The *First Transmission*
begins under King Trisong Detsen (r. 755-797), with the arrival
of Shantarakshita and the tantric master Padmasambhava, and the
founding of Samye Monastery (ca. 779), the first institutional
monastery in Tibet. This transmission was interrupted by the
persecution under King Langdarma (ca. 841-842). The
assassination of Langdarma by a Buddhist monk, Lhalung Palgyi
Dorje, ended the persecution but also the imperial sponsorship.
The fragmentary century after Langdarma preserved tantric
lineages (what became the Nyingma, or "Old School") without
state institutional support.

The *Second Transmission* (10th-11th c.) re-established institutional
Buddhism in Tibet, largely through the efforts of the translator
Rinchen Zangpo (958-1055) in western Tibet and the Indian master
Atisha Dipankara Srijnana (982-1054) in central Tibet. From this
transmission came the three main "New Schools": Kadampa (later
absorbed into Gelug), Sakya, and Kagyu. The Gelug school was founded
by Tsongkhapa (1357-1419) as a reform of the Kadampa, and became the
dominant school from the 17th century forward under the institution
of the Dalai Lama.

For the cross-polity analysis, Tibetan Buddhism from the 13th
century onward developed the *patron-priest relationship*
(*yön-chö*, "patron-priest") with non-Tibetan rulers: first with
the Mongol Yuan court (the relationship of Sakya Pandita with
Godan Khan, 1244. And that of Phakpa with Kublai Khan as
Imperial Preceptor, from ca. 1260), then with the later Mongol
khanates (Altan Khan and the Third Dalai Lama, 1578. The event
that conferred the title "Dalai Lama" on Sonam Gyatso), the Qing
emperors (from Güshi Khan and the Fifth Dalai Lama forward), and
the Kalmyk, Buryat, and Tuvan Mongolian peoples into the modern
period.

### Mechanism

- **Guru-disciple lineages under tantric initiation.** Vajrayana
  Buddhism is organized around initiatory lineages in which specific
  teachings, empowerments, and sadhana practices are transmitted
  master-to-disciple in a chain back to the Indian mahasiddhas and
  ultimately to the Buddha. The disciple receives named transmissions
  from named teachers. The record is preserved in lineage histories
  (*lo-rgyus*, *chö-byung*). This is the most elaborated transmission
  architecture in any of the three regimes.
- **Monastic colleges (*shedra*) and ritual colleges (*dratsang*).**
  Large Tibetan monasteries are federations of colleges specialized
  in scholastic philosophy, tantric ritual, calendrical science, and
  medicine. The three great Gelug monasteries (Ganden, Sera, and
  Drepung, all near Lhasa) became the institutional core of Tibetan
  Buddhism, with satellite institutions across Tibet, Mongolia, and
  Inner Asia.
- **The tulku (*sprul-sku*) system.** From the 13th century forward,
  senior teachers were recognized as reincarnations of their
  predecessors. The tulku institution is the most distinctive
  Tibetan innovation in monastic succession. It decouples authority
  from hereditary lineage (as in some other monastic traditions) and
  from purely political selection, and instead grounds it in a
  reincarnation claim verified by a specified procedure involving
  prophecies, signs, and formal identification by senior teachers.
  The Dalai Lama and Panchen Lama are the most famous tulku lineages.
  There are thousands of others. This institution matters to the
  cross-polity analysis because *tulku identification crosses
  political boundaries*: a Dalai Lama can be identified in an area
  outside the polity the previous one governed, as happened
  repeatedly.
- **The *yön-chö* relationship across polities.** Sakya
  Pandita's relationship with Godan Khan established the
  template. The Tibetan lama supplies spiritual authority, the
  non-Tibetan patron supplies political protection and material
  support. The relationship does not subordinate the lama to the
  patron. In several instances (Phakpa's *yön-chö* with Kublai
  Khan. That of the Fifth Dalai Lama with the Qing Shunzhi
  emperor) the lama held a recognized authority parallel to the
  political authority. This is the clearest cross-polity
  instantiation of a substrate-holder operating independent of
  the sovereign while being recognized by the sovereign.
- **Shared scholastic curriculum.** Across the four main schools
  (Nyingma, Kagyu, Sakya, Gelug) and across the Tibetan, Mongolian,
  Bhutanese, and Himalayan Buddhist polities, a common core
  curriculum is taught in Tibetan as the liturgical language, much as
  Pali works in the Theravada world and Chinese in East Asia. That
  curriculum comprises the Prajñaparamita literature, the Madhyamaka
  treatises, the Pramana (epistemology) corpus, and the Abhidharma.
- **Cross-school tantric transmission.** The *Ris-med* (non-sectarian)
  movement of the 19th century formalized a practice that had always
  existed: senior lamas of one school receive transmissions from
  senior lamas of another school, preserving the cross-school
  lineage portfolio against school-specific decay. This is the
  clearest instance of cross-compact transmission within a single
  regime.

### Output

For roughly 1000-1100 years (counting from the Second Transmission,
ca. 11th c., to the present), the Tibetan Vajrayana network has
sustained:

- A shared scriptural canon in Tibetan (the *Kangyur*, translated
  word of the Buddha, 108 volumes. The *Tengyur*, translated
  commentaries, 225 volumes) across Tibet, Bhutan, Sikkim, Ladakh,
  Mongolia, Tuva, Buryatia, and Kalmykia;
- A shared scholastic curriculum taught in Tibetan as liturgical
  language across all these polities;
- Mutual recognition of tulku identification, monastic ordination,
  and tantric transmission across all these polities;
- The *yön-chö* cross-polity authority relationship, sustained with
  varying effectiveness across eight centuries and multiple Inner
  Asian political formations;
- A continuing exile network after 1959, in which the Tibetan
  diaspora (India, Nepal, Bhutan, and globally) has preserved the
  full institutional apparatus and extended it through contact with
  non-Buddhist host societies.

### Decay

One major disruption, still in progress:

- **The 1950 PRC occupation and subsequent destruction.** The
  Chinese occupation of Tibet (1950), the failed 1959 uprising,
  and the systematic destruction in the Cultural Revolution of
  Tibetan Buddhist institutions destroyed more than 6,000
  monasteries in Tibet and neighboring regions. That, with the
  continuing PRC policy of controlling tulku recognition (the
  competing Panchen Lama identifications being the most visible
  instance), is the most severe attack on any of the three
  Buddhist regimes in the modern period. The Tibetan diaspora
  under the Fourteenth Dalai Lama has preserved the
  institutional apparatus in exile, rebuilding Ganden, Sera, and
  Drepung in South India and continuing the full scholastic and
  ritual curriculum. The succession question (what happens on
  the death of the Fourteenth Dalai Lama, with the PRC
  contesting succession) remains open.
- **Mongolian disruption (1920s-1990s).** The Soviet-aligned
  Mongolian People's Republic conducted purges (1937-39 especially)
  that destroyed most Mongolian Buddhist institutions and killed
  thousands of monks. Buriatia, Kalmykia, and Tuva were similarly
  suppressed. Post-1990 revival is in progress but institutional
  scope remains well below pre-1920s levels.
- **The Bhutanese and Sikkimese cases** have been comparatively
  stable, as has the Tibetan diaspora in India and Nepal.

Unlike the Theravada and East Asian regimes, the Tibetan regime
faces a still-acute existential stress. The cross-polity
cooperation mechanisms are working (the exile network sustains
cross-polity ordination, tulku recognition, and scholastic
transmission across India, Nepal, Bhutan, and global diaspora
communities), but the center of gravity of the regime has been
displaced from Tibet itself and its continuity is actively
contested by a major power.

### Lesson for kith-covenant

Three lessons, one shared with the Theravada and East Asian cases and
two distinct.

Shared: **a substrate-holder independent of the sovereign is
structurally necessary for sovereign-transcending cooperation**. The
*yön-chö* relationship is the Tibetan instantiation of this pattern;
*dhammaraja* is the Theravada instantiation. The separation of
Buddhist authority from state authority in East Asia is the East
Asian instantiation. All three regimes depend on a substrate-holder
that is recognized by but not subordinate to the political
authority.

Distinct (1): **the *yön-chö* relationship is the clearest
historical model of cross-compact substrate cooperation.** A
substrate-bearer in one community provides recognized authority
to a polity in another community, without the political
authority of either community absorbing the substrate of the
other. This is close to what kith-covenant needs at the
inter-community recognition layer. Compact-member communities
recognizing the substrate-authority of other compact members
without requiring substrate merger. The mechanism is
patron-priest rather than creed-convergence.

Distinct (2): **the tulku system is the most elaborated
succession mechanism in the case set**, and it is specifically
designed to handle cross-polity succession where political
boundaries shift under the succession window. The member
communities of Kith-Covenant will face analogous succession
problems. How does the next generation of authorized
representatives get recognized across polities whose borders
have moved, whose governments have changed, whose populations
have migrated? The tulku architecture (prophecy and signs plus
formal identification by senior peers plus confirmation by the
receiving community) is a working model even if the
reincarnation-specific content does not transfer.

## Cross-regime comparison on the Christian-cases metrics

The Christian cases in [historical_cases.md](historical_cases.md) are
evaluated on an implicit set of metrics: voluntariness, peer-ness,
longevity, cross-sovereign scope, institutional mechanism density,
substrate, and decay mode. Here are the three Buddhist regimes on
those metrics.

### Voluntariness

- **Theravada regime.** High. Ordination is voluntary both for the
  individual and for the monastery accepting a candidate from
  another polity. Royal patronage is sought but not structurally
  required (the 11th-19th century Sri Lankan re-ordinations happened
  *because* a local line had died out, and were arranged by sangha
  leaders and requested from peer polities). The Theravada regime
  has the weakest state-dependence among the three.
- **East Asian regime.** Mixed. Ordination certificates in imperial
  China were state-regulated from Tang forward. The Tokugawa temple
  registration system (*tera-uke seido*) conscripted Japanese
  Buddhism into state census and surveillance functions. But
  *cross-polity* recognition (Japanese pilgrimage to China for
  transmission, Korean training at Chinese monasteries, Chinese
  transmission to Japanese disciples) happened largely outside
  state regulation and was recognized by the state *after* the
  sangha network had accomplished it. The cross-polity voluntariness
  is high even where intra-polity voluntariness was constrained.
- **Tibetan regime.** Mixed, complicated by the *yön-chö*
  structure. The cross-polity relationships between Tibetan
  lamas and Mongolian, Qing, and Buryat polities were voluntary
  on both sides and negotiated rather than imposed.
  Intra-Tibetan monastic succession included political elements
  (the rise of the Gelug under Güshi Khan involved military
  conquest of the rival schools in Tibet) that qualify pure
  voluntariness. And the *yön-chö* relationship had coercive
  edges on occasion. Net. The Tibetan regime is less voluntary
  than the Theravada, on a par with or somewhat more voluntary
  than the East Asian.

The verdict on voluntariness is that all three regimes have enough voluntariness
in the cross-polity dimension to qualify under the
noticeable_hand.md filter, though the Theravada regime is the
cleanest instance.

### Peer-ness

The Christian cases differ on peer-ness: Westphalia is peer
cooperation among sovereigns, the Hanse is peer cooperation among
cities, lex mercatoria is peer cooperation among merchants, the
Iroquois Confederacy is peer cooperation among nations under a
federal instrument. The Peace of God is different (an ecclesiastical
authority imposing categories of inviolability) and it sits between
peer cooperation and hierarchical authority.

- **Theravada regime.** Peer-like at the sangha level.
  Monasteries across Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia are peers,
  recognizing the ordinations of the others and supplying quorum
  requirements for each other when needed. At the lay-polity
  level the peer-ness is mediated. The Sinhalese and Burmese
  kingdoms cooperated via sangha exchange rather than as direct
  political peers.
- **East Asian regime.** Peer-like at the dharma-transmission level:
  Chinese, Korean, and Japanese Chan masters recognize each other
  and exchange transmission across polity boundaries as peers. At
  the polity level, the relationship was often asymmetric (China
  as scholastic source, Korea and Japan as peripheries receiving
  transmission), but this asymmetry is functional rather than
  hierarchical. It is more like the relationship between the
  Sorbonne and Oxford in the 13th century than between a metropolis
  and a colony. After the 13th century and particularly from the
  Obaku transmission forward, the transmission flows became
  bidirectional.
- **Tibetan regime.** Peer-like at the scholastic and tantric
  transmission level across the four schools and across the Tibetan,
  Bhutanese, Sikkimese, Ladakhi, and Mongolian polities. The
  *yön-chö* relationship is structurally asymmetric (spiritual
  authority from one side, political patronage from the other) but
  is not a subordination. The Tibetan lama retains authority within
  his own sphere even when he resides at the Mongol or Qing court.

The verdict on peer-ness is that all three regimes are peer-cooperation-like at
the sangha level. The lay-polity cooperation they enable is mediated
through the sangha rather than being direct sovereign-to-sovereign
cooperation in the Westphalian sense. This is the most important
structural difference from the Christian cases, and it is the one
that most directly affects the Christian-particularist reading.

### Longevity

- **Theravada regime.** Roughly 2300 years of continuous
  operation, counting from the mission of Ashoka to Sri Lanka
  (mid-3rd c. BCE) to the present. Continuous means that the
  higher-ordination lineage has been transmitted without total
  rupture. While individual kingdoms have lost and re-received
  it, the global Theravada line has not.
- **East Asian regime.** Roughly 1700 years of continuous
  operation within China (counting from the 4th-century
  translation projects), and roughly 1300 years as a
  multi-polity regime (counting from the reception in Japan in
  the 6th century). Several near-rupture episodes (Huichang 845,
  Joseon suppression, Meiji, Cultural Revolution) but the
  cross-polity network survived each.
- **Tibetan regime.** Roughly 1000-1100 years, counting from the
  Second Transmission (11th c.) to the present. One major ongoing
  disruption (1950-present) that has displaced the center of
  gravity to exile.

All three are longer than all the Christian cases individually
(Westphalia ~270 years, Hanse ~300, lex mercatoria ~600, codes
duello ~300, Peace of God ~200 as movement). The Theravada is
longer than the Roman Catholic monastic orders combined (Benedictines
~1500 years).

The verdict on longevity is that the Buddhist regimes are the longest-running
cross-polity cooperation institutions in the comparison set. This is
the sharpest single piece of evidence against the strong
Christian-particularist reading.

### Cross-sovereign scope

- **Theravada regime.** Sri Lanka, Myanmar, Thailand, Cambodia,
  Laos, southern Vietnam (historically), parts of southern China
  (Dai), historical trading-port presence in the Indonesian
  archipelago. All polities shared the Pali canon and the Theravada
  Vinaya.
- **East Asian regime.** China, Korea, Japan, Vietnam, and
  historically the Tangut and Khitan frontiers. All polities shared
  the Chinese canon and the Dharmaguptaka Vinaya.
- **Tibetan regime.** Tibet, Bhutan, Sikkim, Ladakh, Mongolia,
  Buryatia, Tuva, Kalmykia, and historically parts of the Qing Inner
  Asian frontier. All polities shared the Tibetan Kangyur/Tengyur
  and Tibetan liturgical language.

Verdict. All three regimes have comparable cross-sovereign scope
to the Westphalian system. Although none matches the global
reach of the post-1945 UN system, none of the Christian cases
does either under the voluntary-peer filter.

### Institutional mechanism density

All three regimes have dense institutional mechanisms. Shared
canon in a shared liturgical language, shared Vinaya or
equivalent, mutual recognition of ordination, documented
transmission (highest in East Asian), cross-polity pilgrimage
and exchange, reconstitution procedures, monastic colleges. This
matches the institutional density of the Christian cases.

### Substrate

- **All three regimes.** The substrate is Buddhist, specifically
  the Dharma (Sanskrit *dharma*, Pali *dhamma*). The *structural
  feature* of the Buddhist substrate is contested relative to the
  "transcendent moral authority pointing to a single ultimate"
  formulation of
  [historical_cases.md](historical_cases.md#the-transcendent-moral-authority-pattern).
  Buddhism does not have a Creator God. What it has is the Dharma
  as *the nature of reality* that the Buddha *awakened to* (not
  invented, not given by a Creator, but discovered). The Dharma is
  non-personal but is a single ultimate in the sense required for
  transcendent-moral-authority analysis. It is above any individual,
  above any community, above any polity, and it is what ethical
  commitments are ultimately answerable to. The Buddhist substrate
  is thus structurally analogous to the "single ultimate" feature
  without being theistically equivalent.
- The [transcendent moral authority pattern section](historical_cases.md#the-transcendent-moral-authority-pattern)
  claims that Buddhist Dharma "confirms the pattern" as a
  single ultimate. The present analysis supports that claim with
  the caveat that the single-ultimate feature is formally rather
  than theologically supplied. The Dharma is not "a god" in any
  recognizable sense. But it *does* perform the structural role
  that the substrate analysis requires.

### Decay mode

- **Theravada regime.** No terminal decay. Stress episodes (colonial
  disruption, Buddhist nationalism) are real but the regime
  continues.
- **East Asian regime.** Multiple severe stress episodes (Huichang,
  Meiji, Joseon, Cultural Revolution). No terminal decay. The
  cross-polity recognition machinery has survived every episode,
  often in reduced form.
- **Tibetan regime.** One major ongoing stressor (PRC occupation
  and continuing disruption). Exile network has preserved the
  apparatus. Succession of the Dalai Lama post-Fourteenth is the
  open question.

Verdict. No Buddhist regime has fully decayed. The Christian
cases Westphalia, Hanse, codes duello, and Peace of God have all
substantially decayed. Lex mercatoria partially decayed and
partially revived. The Iroquois regime continues in altered
form. The sustained operation of the Buddhist regimes through
modern disruptions is a distinctive feature.

## The five anti-spiral features applied to the sangha

The operative formulation from
[justus_hostis.md](justus_hostis.md#the-anti-spiral-features-why-anchor-above-self-is-necessary-but-not-sufficient)
specifies that a substrate must supply five features whose combination
blocks the purity-spiral failure mode: universal fallen-actor
anthropology, grace over merit, scapegoat-revelation or a functional
equivalent, enemy-love as an operational norm, and non-ultimacy of the
political community.

### (1) Universal fallen-actor anthropology

**Verdict. Strongly supplied.**

Buddhist anthropology holds that every sentient being is subject to
*dukkha* (unsatisfactoriness, suffering) rooted in *kleshas* (mental
afflictions: ignorance, craving, aversion, conceit, wrong view,
doubt, in the standard enumeration). Ignorance (*avidya*) is the root
klesha. It conditions craving. Craving conditions becoming. Becoming
conditions suffering. No sentient being escapes this condition until
full awakening, and even arhats in the Theravada reading (and more
pointedly, bodhisattvas on the long path in Mahayana) have only
partially overcome the kleshas.

The doctrine of anatta (no-self) deepens the feature. Because
there is no substantial self to be "good" or "evil," the line
between wholesome and unwholesome action runs through the
present act rather than between persons. This is the line of
Solzhenitsyn ("the line dividing good and evil cuts through the
heart of every human being") in a non-theistic register.

Buddhist ethics explicitly rejects the tribal / in-group
anthropology that the anti-spiral feature is designed to
foreclose. The Brahmaviharas (metta, karuna, mudita, upekkha)
are trained to extend universally to all sentient beings without
distinction. Classical metta practice explicitly includes the
exercise of extending loving-kindness to enemies in sequence
with friends and strangers, the point being that the practice
fails if metta stops at the in-group boundary.

Buddhism therefore supplies the universal fallen-actor feature
in a different theoretical register (kleshas and anatta rather
than original sin and imago Dei) with the same structural
function. Though the anthropological framing is quite different,
the feature is as robust in Buddhism as in Christianity.

### (2) Grace over merit

**Verdict. Supplied, with significant variation across schools.**

This is the feature where the internal diversity of Buddhism
matters most.

- **Theravada** has the strongest merit-accumulation element.
  *Punna* (merit) is generated by generosity, ethical conduct,
  and meditation, and is explicitly transferable (to deceased
  relatives, for instance) and accumulative. A purely
  merit-driven reading of Theravada would fail the "grace over
  merit" feature. But that is not the operative Theravada
  reading. The doctrine of anatta undercuts any ultimately
  substantial attachment to "my merit." The Dhammapada is
  explicit. Attachment even to one's own virtue is a defilement.
  The Theravada path is consummated by *nibbana*, which is not
  achieved by merit accumulation but by the cessation of
  craving, including craving for good outcomes.
- **Mahayana** extends the move substantially. *Bodhicitta* (the
  aspiration to awaken for the benefit of all sentient beings) is
  explicitly gratuitous in the sense that it is not earnable by
  prior merit. It arises as a response to the recognition of the
  suffering of others, and is cultivated through practices that
  diminish rather than increase self-referential merit-claims. The
  Mahayana doctrine of *sunyata* (emptiness) applied to merit itself
  empties merit of any self-standing reality. Merit is empty of
  "my merit."
- **Pure Land** traditions (particularly Shin Buddhism in Japan)
  develop the feature in a form closest to Christian grace.
  Shinran (1173-1263) teaches *tariki* (other-power,
  specifically the power of the vow of Amida Buddha) against
  *jiriki* (self-power, the path of merit accumulation).
  Awakening in the Pure Land is received through the vow of
  Amida, not earned. Shin's *akunin shoki* ("evil person, proper
  object") teaching (that the evildoer is the proper object of
  the saving vow of Amida before the good person) reads almost
  exactly as a Buddhist Pauline theology of grace. This is the
  closest structural analogue to the Christian grace doctrine in
  any non-Christian tradition.
- **Zen** and **Vajrayana** handle the feature through different
  registers. Zen's *shikantaza* or *kensho* is explicitly not a
  merit-accumulation achievement. It cannot be earned by effort
  alone. Masters routinely dismiss students who approach
  practice as merit accumulation. The deity-yoga and
  guru-devotion of Vajrayana practices are framed as receptive
  rather than generative. The practitioner receives the
  *blessing* (*adhishthana*) of the deity or the guru, which is
  not produced by the effort of the practitioner but transmitted
  through it.

Across all forms the Buddhist handling of merit includes the
anti-competitive-virtue move that the feature requires. Virtue is
not a competitive status marker. Attachment to one's own virtue is a
defilement. The path consummates in a release from the self that
could own the virtue.

The feature is supplied, differently from Christianity, at comparable
depth.

### (3) Scapegoat-revelation or functional equivalent

**Verdict. Functional equivalent supplied, structurally different
from the Christian form**.

This is the feature where the form of Christianity is most
distinctive: the argument of Girard that the Cross reveals the
scapegoat mechanism *as* scapegoating by presenting the innocent
victim as innocent from the point of view of God is a revelation
story with specific historical content. Buddhism does not have
this story.

What Buddhism has is a structurally different disarmament of the
sacrificial mechanism. The mechanism Girard identifies depends
on: (a) mimetic rivalry producing social tension that (b)
discharges through (c) the selection of a scapegoat believed to
be responsible for the crisis, whose (d) destruction restores
the community to unanimity. The Christian revelation defuses
(c)-(d) by making the innocence of the scapegoat explicit and
theologically central.

Buddhism defuses the same mechanism at an earlier stage. The doctrine
of anatta (no substantial self, therefore no ultimately substantial
other) removes the self/other boundary that the scapegoat mechanism
requires for its target designation. If the apparently guilty
scapegoat is, from the awakened point of view, not substantially
different from the community expelling them, then the
expulsion-as-purification logic has no ontological purchase. The
universal-compassion practices (metta, karuna, and the bodhisattva
commitments) operationalize this. The practitioner trains the mind
not to generate the self-other separation that the scapegoat
mechanism requires.

Operationally, the disarming includes:

- **Metta to enemies.** The explicit practice of extending
  loving-kindness to those who have harmed oneself or the
  community, trained as a formal meditation with the direct goal
  of undermining the in-group/out-group boundary.
- **The bodhisattva vow.** In Mahayana, the bodhisattva takes a vow
  to save all sentient beings without exception, explicitly
  including hell beings, explicitly including those who have
  committed the worst acts. The vow structurally forecloses any
  categorization of persons as "beyond compassion."
- **No eternal hell.** While Buddhist cosmology has hells, even
  the worst of them are not eternal. Rebirth out of them is part
  of the structure of the cosmos. This denies the sacrificial
  mechanism its final move (the permanent designation of the
  scapegoat as beyond the community).
- **The Jataka literature.** The previous-lives stories of the
  Buddha routinely feature the bodhisattva sacrificing himself
  for others, including for enemies. The structure is different
  from the Cross (these are many voluntary sacrifices by the
  enlightenment-bound bodhisattva, not a single scapegoat
  revelation), but the moral content is close. The innocent one
  yields his life, and the telling of the story elevates rather
  than conceals the innocent yielding.

Girard himself was ambivalent about the Buddhist case in his later
work, arguing that only the Christian Gospel fully exposes the
scapegoat mechanism because it presents an actual historical
scapegoat narrative revealed *as* scapegoating, rather than an
ontology that cuts below the mechanism. The ontological cut is
structurally different from the historical revelation, and reasonable
analysts differ on whether it is functionally equivalent or merely a
partial analogue.

The reading the evidence supports: Buddhism supplies a real
anti-sacrificial
structure that operates through different means than Christianity,
at meaningful depth, and the question of whether it is as
structurally robust as the Christian form remains live. The
justus_hostis.md formulation accepts Buddhist anatta + compassion as
a partial functional equivalent, and the present analysis supports
that position. The Buddhist Pure Land and Shin traditions are the
closest to the Christian form on this feature; Theravada is the
furthest.

### (4) Enemy-love as operational norm

**Verdict. Strongly supplied.**

The practice of metta (loving-kindness) to enemies is one of the most
formally developed practices in the Buddhist tradition. The
*Metta Sutta* and the *Visuddhimagga*'s exposition of metta
meditation are explicit: the practice extends loving-kindness in
sequence (to oneself, to a benefactor, to a friend, to a neutral
person, to an enemy) and the practice is held to *fail* if the
practitioner cannot extend metta to the enemy. The enemy category
is load-bearing in the practice.

The famous verse of the Dhammapada (5): *"Hatred does not cease
by hatred, but only by love. This is the eternal law."*

Mahayana extends the feature through the bodhisattva commitments
enumerated above. The bodhisattva has explicitly vowed to save all
beings including enemies, and the vow is structural rather than
sentimental. The practitioner is to train the mind until the vow
becomes operational.

Tibetan *lojong* ("mind training") practices are organized around
converting apparent enemies into the primary teachers of the path,
on the view that an enemy who causes suffering is providing the
most direct opportunity to practice patience and compassion.
Chekhawa's *Seven Points of Mind Training* (12th c.) and its
commentaries are the most developed Buddhist corpus on this feature.

The feature is as strong in Buddhism as in Christianity, and on the
specific "enemy as teacher" framing, arguably more developed.

### (5) Non-ultimacy of the political community

**Verdict. Strongly supplied.**

Buddhist renunciation tradition is structurally built on the
non-ultimacy of ordinary political and social life. The monk or nun
*leaves* the household (*pabbajja*, "going forth") to
pursue the Dharma. The act of renunciation is a declaration that the
household, the polity, and the ordinary social order are not the
ultimate.

The two-truths doctrine (*sammuti-sacca* / *paramattha-sacca* in
Theravada; *samvrti-satya* / *paramartha-satya* in Mahayana)
codifies the feature at the epistemic level. Conventional
reality (including political reality) is not the ultimate truth.
This is structurally parallel to the two-cities distinction of
Augustine, reached through different theoretical apparatus.

The Buddhist tradition has been operationally reminded of this
feature through the *dhammaraja* doctrine in Theravada and the
*yön-chö* relationship in Tibetan Vajrayana. The king or patron
is not the ultimate, he is the *servant* of the ultimate. When
kings overreach (when they attempt to absorb the substrate), the
tradition resists, sometimes at severe cost. Burmese and Thai
conflicts between kings and senior monks over Vinaya
interpretation are recurring, and the sangha has generally
preserved its independence even at cost. Tibetan resistance to
the Qing and to PRC attempts to control tulku recognition is the
same feature operating at a higher-stakes register.

The feature is strongly supplied.

### Overall verdict on the anti-spiral features

The sangha supplies the five anti-spiral features. The mapping is
not identical to the Christian form. Features (1), (3), (4), and (5)
are supplied through different theoretical apparatus, and feature
(2) is supplied in a form that varies substantially across Buddhist
schools. But the load-bearing function of the features (blocking the
purity-spiral failure mode by foreclosing the psychological and
ontological moves the spiral requires) is performed.

The one feature where Christianity has a distinctive structural
advantage is (3), scapegoat-revelation. Buddhism supplies an
ontological disarming of the scapegoat mechanism; Christianity
supplies a historical-narrative revelation of it. These are not the
same thing, and the Christian form may be more robust against the
specific failure mode of weaponized scapegoating, because it
provides a direct counter-narrative ("behold the lamb of God, who
takes away the sin of the world") rather than an abstraction. But
this is a live question rather than a settled result.

The Buddhist nationalism failure mode in modern Sri Lanka and
Myanmar demonstrates that the features can be *bypassed* when
the tradition is weaponized as ethnic or political identity
rather than received as its own scriptural mandate. This is
parallel to the Christian colonial selectivity counterweight in
[noticeable_hand.md](noticeable_hand.md#counterweights).
Supplying the feature immunizes the nominal carriers of neither
tradition from betraying them. The features describe what the
substrate *makes available*. They do not describe what the
substrate guarantees.

## Verdict on the Christian-particularist reading

[noticeable_hand.md](noticeable_hand.md) distinguished three readings
of the cross-community cooperation evidence: Christian-particularist,
catholic/perennialist, and naturalist. The sangha evidence bears
directly on the choice among them.

### What the sangha evidence does to the strong Christian-particularist reading

The strong Christian-particularist reading holds that *only
Christianity* supplies the substrate for long-running voluntary
cross-community cooperation, that the apparent successes of
other traditions are either (a) imperial arrangements
misidentified as voluntary, (b) short-lived, or (c) partial
participation in the same truth that Christianity articulates
more completely.

**The sangha weakens this reading substantially.** All three
Buddhist regimes are real, voluntary (at the cross-polity
level), long-running (all longer than any Christian case
individually), and built on a substrate that supplies the
anti-spiral features through non-Christian theoretical
apparatus. Move (a) fails. The regimes are not primarily
imperial. Move (b) fails: the regimes outlast the Christian
cases. Move (c) is available, though it requires a theological
move. The claim is that the supplying of the features by
Buddhism indicates its (partial) participation in a truth
Christianity articulates more completely, which is coherent
within Christian theology (Justin Martyr's *logos spermatikos*;
*Lumen Gentium*'s treatment of non-Christian religions) but is
not derivable from the historical evidence alone.

### What the sangha evidence does to the moderate Christian-particularist reading

The moderate Christian-particularist reading holds that
*Christianity supplies the most complete single combination of the
anti-spiral features, and produces the most numerous successful
voluntary cross-community cooperation regimes in the Western
record, while allowing that other traditions supply real partial
versions of the features.*

**The sangha is consistent with this reading and may even
support it**. On the five anti-spiral features, the sangha
supplies (1), (2), (4), and (5) at depth comparable to
Christianity. It supplies (3) at lesser depth through a
structurally different mechanism. If the Christian argument is
that the *specific combination* of features is most fully
supplied by Christianity (not that Christianity is the only
source), the handling of (3) by the sangha is the evidence most
relevant to that claim. The anti-sacrificial disarmament in
Buddhism operates ontologically. The Christian disarmament
operates historically-narratively. And the latter may be more
robust against specific failure modes.

### What the sangha evidence does to the "different kind of cooperation" claim

A sharper Christian-particularist move is to claim that the sangha
and the Christian cases are *different kinds of cooperation regimes.* The sangha is a trans-polity monastic order that sustains itself
across polities that remain separate. The Christian cases
(Westphalia, Hanse, lex mercatoria, codes duello) are frameworks
through which polities cooperate with each other while remaining
separate. On this view the sangha is structurally more like the
Roman Catholic monastic orders than like Westphalia, and the
comparison with Westphalia is category-confused.

**This move is substantially correct and is the strongest form
of the Christian-particularist argument**. The mechanism of the
sangha is a trans-polity religious community doing scholastic,
ritual, and credentialing work across polities, which is
structurally closer to the Benedictines than to the Hanse. The
Christian tradition has produced both kinds of cooperation
regime: the trans-polity religious orders (Benedictines,
Cistercians, Jesuits, Society of Jesus, and the papacy as an
ecclesiastical authority) *and* the cross-polity cooperation
frameworks (Westphalia, Hanse, lex mercatoria). The question for
the Christian-particularist is whether Buddhism has produced the
*second kind* of regime, not just the first.

The answer is that Buddhism has produced the first kind in
greater abundance and longer duration than Christianity, and the
second kind in weaker forms. The *yön-chö* relationship is the
closest Buddhist analogue to Westphalia. It is real but is narrower
in scope (Tibet and its Inner Asian patrons rather than the full
Christendom). There is no Buddhist Hanseatic League, no Buddhist
lex mercatoria, no Buddhist codes duello.

### Net verdict

The sangha is the sharpest counterweight available to the strong
Christian-particularist reading. It weakens that reading
substantially but does not refute it. It is consistent with the
moderate Christian-particularist reading and with the catholic /
perennialist reading. It does not support the naturalist reading,
because the Buddhist regimes rest on a substrate that meets the
"single ultimate" structural requirement through the Dharma, not
on the absence of such a substrate.

The strongest Christian-particularist formulation consistent with
the sangha evidence is:

> Christianity supplies the most complete combination of the >
anti-spiral features, with feature (3) (scapegoat-revelation) in
> its most structurally robust form. Christianity has produced
the > greatest density of voluntary cross-polity cooperation
*frameworks* > (Westphalia, Hanse, lex mercatoria, codes duello,
Peace of God, > papal jurisdiction) among separate sovereigns;
Buddhism has > produced the longest-running voluntary
cross-polity *communities* > (Theravada, East Asian, Tibetan
Vajrayana). These are different > kinds of achievement. The
cross-polity-framework achievement of Christianity > is what
kith-covenant most directly needs to learn > from; the
cross-polity-community achievement of Buddhism is a real and >
durable alternative form that kith-covenant should also learn >
from.

This is a narrower but more defensible Christian-particularist
position than
[noticeable_hand.md's strongest form](noticeable_hand.md#the-hypothesis-stated-in-its-strongest-form).
The narrowing is evidence-driven, not concessive.

## Lessons for kith-covenant

Four lessons specific to this document (in addition to the
regime-specific lessons above):

First, **the substrate requirement for kith-covenant members should
remain anti-spiral-feature-based rather than theology-based**. The
sangha evidence confirms that the features can be supplied from
multiple theological sources, and the features rather than the
sources are what the cooperation regime requires. This is the
formulation already operative in
[justus_hostis.md](justus_hostis.md#the-anti-spiral-features-why-anchor-above-self-is-necessary-but-not-sufficient).
The present document supplies additional evidence for it.

Second, **kith-covenant should recognize that it is designing a
cross-polity cooperation framework (closer to Westphalia than to
the Benedictines) and should therefore draw most directly from
the Christian-frameworks record for its design**. The
achievement of the Buddhist regimes is a trans-polity community
form that is less directly applicable to the goal of
kith-covenant. This is not a dismissal of the Buddhist evidence.
It is recognition that the greatest relevance of the Buddhist
evidence is to a different compact type than kith-covenant is.

Third, **kith-covenant should incorporate the pilgrimage-for-
credentialing pattern from the East Asian Buddhist case and the
*yön-chö* substrate-holder-across-polities pattern from the Tibetan
case**. Both are direct architectural contributions to the
recognition protocols. The former operationalizes cross-compact
training exchange. The latter operationalizes substrate authority
that crosses political boundaries without merging substrates.

Fourth, **kith-covenant should expect the anti-spiral features
to fail in practice even when they are supplied by the substrate
in principle**. The Buddhist nationalism cases, like the
Christian colonial selectivity cases, demonstrate that the
features can be bypassed by nominal carriers. The credentialing
and recognition of the compact protocols
([recognition_protocols.md](recognition_protocols.md)) should
therefore check for ongoing operation of the features, not just
for the in-principle supply by the substrate of them. This is
already operative in the two-layer recognition architecture. The
present document supplies additional evidence that the ongoing
check is load-bearing.

## Open questions

- The scapegoat-revelation feature is the hardest to evaluate
  cross-traditionally. The late position of Girard that only
  Christianity fully reveals the mechanism is contested. The
  Buddhist ontological disarming is structurally different. What
  additional evidence (comparative historical analysis of
  sacrificial-crisis moments across Buddhist and Christian
  societies) would distinguish the claims more cleanly?
- The Pure Land (Shin) tradition appears to supply
  grace-over-merit in the form most directly analogous to
  Christian grace. Does it also supply a scapegoat-revelation
  analogue, the vow of Amida as a structural revelation of the
  unworthiness-of-merit dynamic that drives the scapegoat
  mechanism? This is a non-trivial theological-comparative
  question that would repay direct treatment.
- The *yön-chö* relationship is the clearest cross-polity
  substrate authority model in the case set. Are there Christian
  historical analogues (the relationship of the medieval papacy
  with Christian kings. Specific abbot-king relationships) that
  would repay direct comparison with *yön-chö* on the
  cross-polity-authority dimension?
- The Buddhist nationalism failure mode (Sinhala, Burmese, elements
  of Thai and Mongolian nationalism) is structurally parallel to the
  Christian colonial selectivity failure mode. What is the
  mechanism by which an anti-spiral-feature-supplying substrate
  produces these failure modes in practice, and what does that
  mechanism tell kith-covenant about the ongoing anti-spiral-features
  check?
- The three Buddhist regimes (Theravada, East Asian, Tibetan) are
  operationally separate after roughly the 11th century. Do they
  *recognize* each other as analogous regimes? Does a Theravada
  monastic leader recognize a Tibetan tulku as playing a comparable
  role in his tradition? If not, what does that say about
  cross-compact recognition generally?
- Is the record of the Roman Catholic monastic orders
  (Benedictines ~1500 years. Papal authority over monastic
  orders across Christendom ~1500 years) better analogized to
  the Buddhist regimes than to the Christian cross-polity
  cooperation frameworks? A direct comparison of the
  Benedictines with the Theravada monastic network would sharpen
  the trans-polity-community vs. cross-polity-framework
  distinction.

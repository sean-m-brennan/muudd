# Commons federation: Ostrom's nested enterprises applied to habitats

## Thesis

Space habitats make the commons problem inescapable. Life support
(atmosphere, water, thermal regulation) is a commons that cannot be
privatized without producing the very coercion vectors the common-virtue
framework exists to prevent. Communication spectrum, navigational data,
the knowledge archive, and rescue infrastructure are all commons of the
same structural kind, non-excludable at the margin, rivalrous under
stress, and weaponizable by withholding or pollution. In "Tragedy of the
Commons" (1968) Garrett Hardin proposed that commons governance is
impossible without either privatization or central authority. The
empirical work of Elinor Ostrom (*Governing the Commons*, 1990) demonstrated
that Hardin was wrong. Communities have governed commons successfully
for centuries without either solution, provided certain design
principles are met.

Her eight design principles for long-enduring commons institutions
map onto the kith-covenant architecture with surprising precision: clear
boundaries (the four categories), proportional equivalence of benefits
and costs (symmetric rules), collective-choice arrangements (compact
deliberation), monitoring (the mediator pool), graduated sanctions (the
escalation ladder), conflict-resolution mechanisms (liminal institutions),
minimal recognition of rights to organize (sovereignty of member
communities), and nested enterprises (habitat-level to compact-level to
inter-compact). The mapping is not coincidental. Those design
principles were derived empirically from the same class of historical
cases (commons, fisheries, and irrigation systems) that the
kith-covenant theoretical framework draws on.

The two frameworks diverge on the deepest question.
**The commons work assumes rational actors, and the kith-covenant
virtue framework assumes fallen ones.** The divergence matters
operationally. A rational actor defects when the expected payoff from
defection exceeds the expected cost of punishment. A fallen actor may
defect even when it does not pay, out of resentment, pride, and appetite,
or from the *libido dominandi* that Augustine named as the deepest
human corruption. Commons governance designed only for rational defectors
fails against fallen ones, and the virtue framework provides
the deeper layer.

The synthesis produces a commons architecture that is empirically
grounded (the evidence base is formidable) and theologically
sober (the fallen-actor anthropology ensures the design does not
assume more about human nature than the evidence warrants).

## Ostrom's eight design principles

Ostrom distilled her findings into eight design principles present in
all long-enduring commons governance regimes. Each is stated below
with its original formulation, the empirical evidence she marshaled,
and the kith-covenant structural element it maps to.

### 1. Clearly defined boundaries

*"Individuals or households who have rights to withdraw resource units
from the [common-pool resource] must be clearly defined, as must the
boundaries of the [resource] itself."*

Without clear boundaries, free-riding is undetectable. The commons
must know who its appropriators are and what the resource is.

**Kith-covenant mapping.** The four categories
([Recognition protocols](recognition_protocols.md)) define who is
inside the compact (members), who is in structured conflict with it
(*justus hostis*), who has been designated outside the cooperative
framework (anathema), and who is encountered but not a
party (non-members encountered). The recognition ceremony is the
boundary-drawing mechanism. You are a member of the commons if and only
if you have made and maintain the five-movement recognition statement.

### 2. Proportional equivalence of benefits and costs

*"Appropriation rules restricting time, place, technology, and/or
quantity of resource units are related to local conditions and to
provision rules requiring labor, material, and/or money."*

Those who benefit more contribute more, and those who contribute more are
entitled to benefit more. Asymmetric rules (where one party bears
costs while another captures benefits) corrode cooperation.

**Kith-covenant mapping.** The symmetric-rules precondition from
[Justus hostis](justus_hostis.md) reads, "Whatever is permitted to one side
is permitted to the other, and whatever is forbidden is forbidden to
both." In the commons context, this means that a habitat that consumes
more of a shared resource (e.g., communication bandwidth) contributes
proportionally more to its maintenance.

### 3. Collective-choice arrangements

*"Most individuals affected by the operational rules can participate in
modifying the operational rules."*

Rules imposed from outside are less stable than rules developed by the
appropriators themselves. That is her empirical finding against
top-down governance.

**Kith-covenant mapping:** the deliberative process of the
compact. The recognition ceremony is non-amendable (the five
movements are structural), but operational rules (resource
allocation, scheduling, emergency procedures) are set by the
member communities collectively, with each community having
standing in the deliberation.

### 4. Monitoring

*"Monitors, who actively audit [common-pool resource] conditions and
appropriator behavior, are accountable to the appropriators or are the
appropriators themselves."*

While commons without monitoring decay, monitors who are not accountable
to the commons community become a surveillance apparatus.

**Kith-covenant mapping.** The mediator pool
([Liminal institutions](liminal_institutions.md)) serves the
monitoring function. Mediators are drawn from member communities, serve
terms, and return home, accountable to the compact rather than to
a permanent monitoring bureaucracy. The "open-carry" principle (from
the founding concept, where participants are visibly heavily
armed, not as threat but to signal preparedness, determination, and
seriousness) provides the philosophical grounding. Enforcement
capability is displayed, not concealed, making monitoring distributed
by design rather than imposed by a central authority.

### 5. Graduated sanctions

*"Appropriators who violate operational rules are likely to be assessed
graduated sanctions (depending on the seriousness and context of the
offense) by other appropriators, by officials accountable to these
appropriators, or by both."*

Although binary enforcement (comply or be expelled) is simple, it is
brittle. Graduated sanctions allow correction without destruction.

**Kith-covenant mapping.** The escalation ladder in
[Bounded conflict](bounded_conflict.md) is a graduated-sanctions
system. From private communication through formal mediation through
compact-wide deliberation through anathema designation,
each rung is more severe and more costly to invoke. Her empirical
finding that graduated sanctions are *necessary* for commons
durability strengthens the case for how the ladder is designed.

### 6. Conflict-resolution mechanisms

*"Appropriators and their officials have rapid access to low-cost local
arenas to resolve conflicts among appropriators or between appropriators
and officials."*

Conflicts that cannot be resolved cheaply and quickly escalate,
damaging the commons in the process.

**Kith-covenant mapping:** the liminal institutions
([Liminal institutions](liminal_institutions.md)), especially
the pie-powder-court model for rapid, low-cost adjudication of
specific disputes, and the interim-injunction mechanism for
emergency cases.

### 7. Minimal recognition of rights to organize

*"The rights of appropriators to devise their own institutions are not
challenged by external governmental authorities."*

Commons governance works when the commons community is autonomous. If an
external sovereign can override its rules or dismantle its institutions,
the governance does not hold.

**Kith-covenant mapping:** the sovereignty of member communities.
Internal governance in each community is the autonomous_trust
scope, not the kith-covenant one. The compact does not override
the internal arrangements of member communities, and governs only
the inter-community commons. This is the design principle that
prevents kith-covenant from becoming a universal sovereign.

### 8. Nested enterprises

*"Appropriation, provision, monitoring, enforcement, conflict
resolution, and governance activities are organized in multiple layers
of nested enterprises."*

Large-scale commons are governed by nesting: local rules at the
local level, regional rules at the regional level, and so on. Each
level has its own governance appropriate to its scale.

**Kith-covenant mapping:** three nesting levels.
- **Habitat level** (autonomous_trust scope). Intra-community commons
  (internal life support, internal communications, internal
  governance) governed by the institutions each community holds.
- **Compact level** (kith-covenant scope). Inter-community commons
  (shared communication relays, navigational data, knowledge archive,
  emergency infrastructure) governed by the liminal
  institutions of the compact.
- **Inter-compact level** (not yet developed). Commons shared between
  multiple compacts, if compacts federate. This is a forward pointer,
  and the mechanisms would be structurally analogous to kith-covenant
  itself, but at a higher nesting level.

## The five space commons

Space habitats produce five commons that cannot be privatized without
producing coercion.

### 1. Life support

Atmosphere generation, water recycling, and thermal regulation are the
most fundamental commons. In a sealed habitat, no individual or sub-group
can be excluded from the atmosphere without killing them, and no use of
the atmosphere by one person can be prevented without doing the
same. Although no one can be shut out of it, the atmosphere is
*rivalrous under stress*. A habitat whose life-support capacity is
exceeded by its population faces a genuine tragedy-of-the-commons
scenario in which each additional person degrades the resource for all.

**Coercion vulnerability.** A community that controls life-support
infrastructure can coerce other communities by threatening to restrict
it. This is why life support must be a commons governed by the compact
rather than owned by any single community. The "reverence for life"
virtue from the common-virtue framework applies directly. Attacks on
life-support systems are the most severe virtue violation, and
withholding life support for political leverage is structurally
equivalent to an attack.

### 2. Communication spectrum

Electromagnetic spectrum for intra-system communication is finite and
rivalrous. A habitat that monopolizes the available bandwidth degrades
communication for all others. The "protection of communicative commons"
virtue from [Bounded conflict](bounded_conflict.md) (no attacks on
relays, no jamming of emergency frequencies, no corruption of timing
services, no interdiction of diplomatic traffic) is the commons-
governance rule for this resource.

**Coercion vulnerability.** A community that controls communication
relays can intercept, delay, or block communications between other
communities. This is the information-warfare vector that makes
communication infrastructure a commons requiring compact governance.

### 3. Navigational data

Orbital mechanics data (ephemerides, conjunctions, and debris) is a
commons that every habitat needs and no single habitat can maintain
alone. The data is non-excludable (you cannot prevent a
habitat from using publicly broadcast orbital data) but rivalrous in
production (the telescopes and computation required to maintain
accurate debris-tracking catalogs are expensive).

**Coercion vulnerability.** A community that controls navigational data
can endanger other communities by withholding conjunction warnings or
providing false orbital data. This is the space analog of poisoning
a well, corrupting a commons that everyone depends on.

### 4. The knowledge archive

The accumulated knowledge of the compact (scientific data, engineering
records, cultural artifacts, historical documents) is the commons
protected by the "inheritance of the future" virtue. The knowledge
archive is non-rivalrous (use by one person does not degrade it) but
depletable by *destruction* (deliberate archive erasure, corruption of
records, suppression of inconvenient data).

**Coercion vulnerability.** The knowledge archive is vulnerable to
selective destruction, the Orwellian threat of controlling the past
to control the future. The "inheritance of the future" virtue treats
archive destruction as equivalent to genocide delayed by one
generation.

### 5. Rescue infrastructure

Emergency response capability (distress beacons, rescue vessels,
medical facilities available to all compact members) is a commons
governed by the "sanctuary of the rescuer" virtue. It is non-rivalrous
in normal conditions (maintaining rescue capacity does not reduce it)
but rivalrous in crisis (rescue capacity deployed to one emergency is
not available for another).

**Coercion vulnerability.** A community that controls rescue
infrastructure can deny emergency aid as political leverage. The
"sanctuary of the rescuer" virtue prohibits this, and the commons-
governance framework provides the institutional structure to ensure
the prohibition is not merely aspirational.

## Monitoring without panopticon

The fourth principle (monitoring accountable to the
appropriators) faces a hard physical constraint in the space context.
AU-distance light lag makes centralized, real-time monitoring
impossible.

A monitoring signal from Jupiter to the inner system takes 35 to 52
minutes one way. A report from the outer system to a central monitoring
authority and back takes over an hour, during which time the situation
may have changed completely. Centralized monitoring is not merely
undesirable, as Ostrom argues on governance grounds. It is physically
impossible at interplanetary scales.

The alternative is **distributed monitoring through the mediator
pool**. Each liminal institution monitors the commons in its local
area (within light-lag bounds that allow meaningful real-time
observation) and reports to the compact on a delayed basis. The
reports are aggregated, not in real time but on a cadence that
accounts for light-lag, in weekly or monthly summaries that capture
trends rather than moment-by-moment snapshots.

This is structurally analogous to the autonomous_trust gossip protocol
for intra-community reputation. AT propagates reputation updates
through peer-to-peer gossip, reaching eventual consistency rather than
instantaneous consistency. Commons monitoring at the compact level
works the same way. Each monitoring node observes its local area and
propagates observations through the shared communication network, so
the compact as a whole achieves eventually-consistent knowledge of
commons health.

The "open-carry" principle supports this architecture. Communities
that visibly maintain their enforcement capability (not as threat but
as signal of seriousness) make commons monitoring a natural
byproduct of visible preparedness. If the capacity and usage of each
community are legible to all the others (because concealment is
neither expected nor rewarded), then monitoring is distributed by
default, and every member is a monitor.
The role of the liminal institutions is not to *do* all the monitoring but
to *aggregate and adjudicate* the distributed observations.

The panopticon risk arises when monitoring is centralized, invisible,
and unaccountable. Distributed monitoring that is visible, aggregated
by accountable liminal institutions, and subject to deliberative
oversight avoids this risk, not perfectly but structurally.

## Graduated sanctions and the escalation ladder

The fifth principle (graduated sanctions) maps directly onto
the escalation ladder in [Bounded conflict](bounded_conflict.md).
The commons-governance literature strengthens the case for that
design in several ways.

**First-offense tolerance.** Ostrom found that long-enduring commons
regimes tolerate first offenses. They do not punish harshly on the
first violation, because harsh first-offense punishment discourages
self-reporting and encourages concealment. This maps onto the lowest
rungs of the escalation ladder, which are private communication,
expression of concern, and diplomatic inquiry, all designed to permit
correction without humiliation.

**Proportional escalation.** Subsequent offenses receive proportionally
harsher sanctions, but the proportionality matters. Sanctions too light
fail to deter, and sanctions too harsh are perceived as unjust and
provoke defiance rather than compliance. The graduated structure of the
ladder (from private communication through formal mediation through
compact-wide deliberation) embodies this principle.

**Community-applied sanctions.** Ostrom found that sanctions applied by
the commons community (rather than by an external authority) are more
effective, because the judgment of that community carries moral weight
an external enforcer cannot. This maps onto the deliberative process.
Anathema designation requires the concurrence of multiple member
communities, not the decision of a central authority.

**The virtuous-actor discount.** An observation not in Ostrom but
consistent with the framework here. A community known for
virtue receives the benefit of the doubt in ambiguous cases. A
community with a history of commons violations receives heightened
scrutiny. The virtue framework adds a dimension the
rational-actor model misses. The act is not the only thing that matters,
the *character* of the actor matters too, and character is legible over
time through the common-virtue refusals.

## Light-lag and eventually-consistent commons

The physics of interplanetary distance creates a governance constraint
that has no terrestrial analog. **Information about the commons is
always stale at any non-local point.**

### The delay problem

At one AU (Earth-Sun distance), light takes approximately 8 minutes to
travel. At the distance of Jupiter (~5 AU), one-way light time is 35 to 52
minutes. At Saturn (~10 AU), 70 to 90 minutes. A compact spanning the
inner and outer solar system faces communication delays of hours for
round-trip deliberation.

This delay does not merely slow governance, it structurally changes it.
A centralized deliberative body cannot adjudicate a real-time commons
dispute between communities separated by AU distances. By the time the
central body receives the report, deliberates, and transmits a decision,
the situation on the ground may have changed completely.

### Eventually-consistent governance

The solution is borrowed from distributed-systems engineering, and is
**eventual consistency**. Each local node of the compact governance
network makes decisions based on the most recent information available
to it, and those decisions are propagated to the rest of the network.
Over time, the network converges on a consistent state, but at any
given moment, different nodes may have different information and
therefore different views of how the commons stands.

This is not anarchy, but structured autonomy within a shared
framework. The shared framework (the common virtues, the escalation
ladder, the mediator pool) provides the rules by which local
decisions are made. The propagation mechanism (periodic reports from
liminal institutions, gossip-protocol-style information sharing)
provides the convergence. The deliberative process (which
necessarily operates on a slower cadence at interplanetary scales)
provides the course correction when local decisions diverge.

### Split-brain scenarios

The worst case is that two parts of the compact lose communication with
each other for an extended period (equipment failure, conjunction,
hostile interdiction). Each part continues to govern its local commons
based on its most recent information, but the two parts may make
incompatible decisions. When communication is restored, the decisions
must be reconciled.

The commons-governance literature does not address this scenario
(terrestrial commons do not face multi-hour communication delays). The
distributed-systems literature addresses it extensively under the
rubric of "partition tolerance" (the CAP theorem, where consistency,
availability, and partition tolerance are pick two). The
commons governance here must choose partition tolerance and availability
(local governance continues during the partition) at the expense of
consistency (decisions may diverge). Reconciliation upon partition
healing follows the established deliberative process, with the
mediator pool adjudicating incompatible decisions.

### The shadow of the future, delayed

The folk theorem from [Tacit coordination](tacit_coordination.md)
shows that cooperation is sustainable when the shadow of the future is
long, when actors expect to interact repeatedly and indefinitely. In
the space context, the shadow of the future is very long (habitats
cannot easily relocate, and the interaction is effectively permanent) but
the shadow is also **delayed**, since the consequences of defection take
time to propagate. A community that defects against the commons may
enjoy the benefits of defection for the duration of the light-lag
delay before other communities can even detect the defection, let
alone respond.

This delay advantage makes commons violation more tempting at
interplanetary scales than at terrestrial scales. The commons-
governance response has three parts. Automated monitoring systems detect
and report violations without human delay. Pre-committed response
protocols activate upon detection (the interim-injunction
mechanism from [Liminal institutions](liminal_institutions.md)).
Reputation consequences persist beyond the immediate
violation, making the long-term cost of defection exceed the
short-term benefit even after accounting for the delay advantage.

### Light-lag-honest decision classes

The eventual-consistency framing above treats light-lag as a
**governance latency** problem. The wider compact will *eventually*
recognize what has happened locally, and disputes are reconciled
under the deliberative process when communication is restored.
[capture_resistance.md "Light-lag-honest decision classes"](capture_resistance.md#5-light-lag-honest-decision-classes)
reframes the same physical fact as a **capture vulnerability**. A
faction can complete a capture before the wider compact has the
information needed to recognize it, so eventual consistency means the
network will eventually disapprove of the capture but cannot
*prevent* it within the operational window the captor has.

The strengthening move is to formalize **which classes of decision
can be made within light-lag and which structurally cannot**, with
**cryptographic enforcement** of the distinction.

**First-class decisions** (local operational choices, commons
drawdowns within pre-agreed thresholds, mediator-pool internal
procedure, life-critical-commons response, intra-community
governance) are valid when made by an authorized local body
acting within its envelope. These are the decisions eventual
consistency was always intended to handle, and nothing about the
present analysis changes their treatment.

**Second-class decisions** produce **protocol artifacts
that fail to validate without inputs from the wider network**. This
class covers new mediations begun, sanctions
imposed, designations made (anathema, *hostis humani generis*),
compact-level amendments, modification of the compact-incapable
list ([Justus hostis](justus_hostis.md#the-anti-spiral-features-why-anchor-above-self-is-necessary-but-not-sufficient),
[capture_resistance.md "Substrate-deference test"](capture_resistance.md#2-the-substrate-deference-test-operationalizing-non-ultimacy)),
amendment of the recognition-statement five movements (which the
recognition protocol forbids in any case), and reclassification of
decisions between the two classes. A
faction acting unilaterally in the second class is not merely
producing an outcome that the wider network will later disapprove
of, but an outcome that the protocol itself flags as
illegitimate the moment validation is attempted, and the audit
commons surfaces the attempted-but-invalid action.

The cryptographic mechanism is straightforward. Second-class
decisions produce signed records whose validation requires
multi-party signature from a quorum spanning multiple communities
at multiple distances, with the quorum requirement tuned so that
no single AU-region's faction can satisfy it alone. The signatures
must arrive within a defined latency window, and signatures arriving
later are accepted only if the decision has been re-issued within
the window. This makes second-class decisions *impossible to
fait-accompli* without faking signatures from communities the
captor does not control, which is the protocol-level violation
the audit commons surfaces immediately.

The classification of decision types (which class which decision
falls into) is a **constitutional-layer commitment**, set at
compact-formation, modifiable only through the same heavyweight
procedure that the recognition statement itself requires. A faction
attempting to capture the compact by reclassifying decisions from
second to first class is attempting an obvious protocol amendment,
which is itself a second-class decision. The classification is
self-protecting in one specific structural sense. The only path to
weakening the protection is itself protected by the protection.

This refinement of the eventual-consistency model preserves
local autonomy for decisions that genuinely require it (where
light-lag would make centralized adjudication useless) while
foreclosing the capture vector for decisions that are not local
in their consequences (where unilateral action by a faction can
inflict damage the wider compact cannot reverse). The CAP-theorem
trade in §"Split-brain scenarios" above is preserved for first-class
decisions and **inverted** for second-class decisions. The protocol
chooses consistency over availability for the latter category,
because the cost of inconsistent second-class decisions
(unrepudiable but illegitimate sanctions, faked anathema
designations, and amendments the wider compact never authorized)
exceeds the cost of unavailability during partition.

## The tragedy-of-the-virtues problem

While a commons governed purely by rules is gamed (the law Goodhart
states, as developed in [Bounded conflict](bounded_conflict.md)), a
commons governed purely by virtue degrades when virtue erodes. The
empirical finding was that *both* internalized norms and external
monitoring are necessary for commons durability. Neither alone is
sufficient.

The **tragedy of the virtues** is the specific failure mode where the
commons is maintained by a shrinking population of virtuous actors
while free-riders consume it. The virtuous actors bear the full cost
of commons maintenance (because their virtue prevents them from
defecting) while the free-riders enjoy the full benefit (because the
monitoring system designed for rational actors does not detect
actors who technically comply with the rules while violating their
spirit). Over time, the burden on the virtuous becomes unsustainable,
and the commons collapses, not because the rules failed but because
the virtue that made the rules work eroded beneath them.

This is the deepest point of contact between the commons framework and
the anthropology here. Ostrom observed the phenomenon
empirically (commons that decayed despite intact governance structures)
but did not theorize the *fallen actor* as a category. The
fallen-actor anthropology (drawn from Augustine, Niebuhr, Madison,
and the Christian tradition developed in
[Justus hostis](justus_hostis.md)) provides the theoretical
account. Humans are not merely rational calculators who defect when
it pays. They are fallen beings who may defect out of pride,
resentment, or appetite even when it does *not* pay.

The commons-governance response is not to assume virtue (that produces
the tragedy of the virtues) or to assume only self-interest (that
produces the tragedy of the commons), but to **design for the full
range of human motivation**, with rules that deter rational defectors,
monitoring that detects sophisticated defectors, sanctions that
correct recoverable defectors, and the virtue framework that cultivates
the dispositions that make the rules and monitoring work. The
escalation ladder, the mediator pool, and the common-virtue refusals
are all part of this multi-layered governance.

## Historical commons cases

### The Swiss alpine commons

The primary case study. Swiss alpine villages have governed
shared pastureland (*Allmenden*) for over five centuries, with some
governance institutions documented back to the 13th century. The
commons are genuine commons. No single farmer owns the pasture, but
each farmer has defined rights to graze a specified number of cattle.

**Substrate:** local custom reinforced by Swiss confederal
recognition of cantonal autonomy. The Protestant and Catholic
cantons maintained the commons governance across the Reformation
divide, suggesting that the commons-governance mechanism is
resilient to substrate variation (both Christian confessions
furnished the oath-binding function).

**Mechanism.** Annual village assemblies set grazing limits, designated
Alps-masters (rotating, not hereditary) monitored compliance, sanctions
ran graduated from fines to exclusion, and governance nested from
village to cantonal level.

**Decay mode.** Remarkably, many Swiss alpine commons survive to the
present day. The primary erosion vector is not governance failure but
economic irrelevance. As agriculture declines in economic importance,
the commons governance structures that served it lose their
constituency. The lesson is that commons governance endures as long as
the commons matters. When the commons becomes marginal, governance
structures hollow out even if they are not formally abolished.

### Japanese village commons (*iriai*)

Japanese villages maintained common forests, fisheries, and water
sources through *iriai* (common-use) institutions for centuries. The
governance was village-level, with clear boundaries (who could use the
commons), proportional rules (contribution requirements tied to
usage rights), monitoring (by village members themselves), and
graduated sanctions (from warnings to fines to exclusion from the
commons).

**Substrate.** Confucian and Buddhist social norms reinforced the
institutional rules. The village *mura* was simultaneously a
governance unit and a moral community.

**Mechanism.** Village assemblies, rotating stewardship, and
reputation-based enforcement.

**Decay mode.** The centralization program of the Meiji Restoration
(1868 onward) deliberately dismantled village commons governance to
bring local resources under state control. The destruction was not
governance failure but *external sovereign absorption*, the same
mechanism that killed the pie-powder courts and the Hanseatic Kontore.
The lesson reinforces the Kontore lesson. Commons governance without
sovereign protection is absorbed by rising states.

### The Balinese subak water temple system

Balinese rice farmers coordinate irrigation across the entire island
through a network of *subak* (irrigation societies) governed by water
temples. The system manages a complex commons (irrigation water that
must be shared across thousands of rice paddies on different
schedules) without central authority.

**Substrate:** Hindu-Balinese temple religion. The water temples
are simultaneously irrigation-governance institutions and sacred
spaces. Decisions about water allocation are made at temples,
under the authority of the gods, and violations are sacral
offenses as well as practical harms.

**Mechanism.** The water temple hierarchy mirrors the watershed
hierarchy. Small temples govern local canals, larger temples govern
rivers, and the supreme water temple (Pura Ulun Danu Batur) governs
the island-wide system. Ostrom and Lansing (*Priests and Programmers*,
1991) showed that this decentralized system outperforms centralized
government irrigation management.

**Decay mode.** The centralized government irrigation management the
Green Revolution introduced in the 1970s nearly destroyed the
subak system. When the centralized system produced worse outcomes
(pest outbreaks, water shortages), the government reverted to the
temple-based system. The lesson is that the sacred substrate is not an
optional decoration. It is the governance mechanism. Removing it and
replacing it with "rational" management produced *worse* outcomes,
because the sacred system embedded centuries of accumulated local
knowledge that the centralized system could not replicate.

### Maritime commons: freedom of the seas

In *Mare Liberum* (1609) Grotius established the principle that the
open seas are a commons, and that no state can claim sovereignty over
the high seas. The principle survived for centuries as a genuine
commons-governance regime. All nations had the right to navigate, fish,
and trade on the high seas, and attacks on neutral shipping were (in
theory) universally condemned.

**Substrate:** the *jus publicum Europaeum*, the shared framework
of European international law, itself resting on the Christendom
substrate that [Historical cases](historical_cases.md)
develops.

**Mechanism.** Naval custom, reciprocal enforcement (an attack on the
shipping of one nation provoked collective response), and the doctrine of
*hostis humani generis* applied to pirates, who were, by definition,
enemies of all mankind and subject to universal jurisdiction.

**Decay mode.** The freedom of the seas eroded as states extended
territorial waters, established exclusive economic zones (EEZs), and
began to treat the oceans as partitionable resources rather than
commons. The UN Convention on the Law of the Sea (1982) codified this
partition. The commons was not destroyed by defection. It was
**enclosed**, the maritime analog of the English enclosure movement
that destroyed the common fields. The lesson is that commons governance
is vulnerable to defection from within and also to enclosure from
without.

## Open questions

- What is the **commons equivalent of the anathema designation**? When
  does abuse of a shared commons by a member community
  become grounds for escalation beyond graduated sanctions? The
  escalation ladder provides the mechanism, but the trigger
  threshold for commons-specific violations needs specification.

- Can the **knowledge commons** (the "inheritance of the future"
  virtue) be governed by the same mechanisms as physical commons, or
  does the non-rivalrous character of information require a fundamentally
  different architecture? A rivalrous commons (grazing land, bandwidth)
  is depleted by overuse, and a knowledge commons is depleted by
  *destruction*. The threat models are different. Are the governance
  mechanisms?

- How does the **"minimal recognition of rights to organize"**
  principle interact with the kith-covenant requirement that member
  communities anchor to a transcendent moral authority above
  themselves and supply the five anti-spiral features (per
  [justus_hostis.md "The anti-spiral features"](justus_hostis.md#the-anti-spiral-features-why-anchor-above-self-is-necessary-but-not-sufficient))?
  Communities that lack full-compact substrate may still be parties
  to commons governance at the non-member-encountered level or in
  thin-functional-domain frameworks (the Red Crescent / Rhodian Sea
  Law / lex mercatoria pattern documented in
  [historical_cases.md §"Convergence without shared culture"](historical_cases.md#convergence-without-shared-culture-the-thin-functional-domain-cases)).
  How are they governed by the commons rules without the full
  covenant that makes the rules trustworthy at the compact scale?
  The thin-functional-domain pattern suggests an answer. Functional
  convergence on specific commons (life support, rescue, orbital
  debris, communications) is available without shared substrate.
  But the boundary between what that pattern can and cannot carry
  is itself a design question.

- What happens to **commons governance when the compact itself
  fractures**? If two successor compacts both depend on the same
  communication relay or knowledge archive, which governs it? This
  is the material dimension of the problem
  [Exit and succession](exit_and_succession.md) addresses.

- The **Balinese subak** case suggests that sacred-substrate commons
  governance outperforms secular centralized management. Is this
  finding generalizable? Are there cases where secular commons
  governance outperforms sacred-substrate governance, and if so,
  what structural features distinguish those cases?

- The **tragedy-of-the-virtues** problem suggests that commons
  governance decays from within even when the institutional structure
  is intact. What are the early-warning indicators? Can the shared
  monitoring infrastructure detect virtue-erosion before the commons
  itself is visibly degraded?

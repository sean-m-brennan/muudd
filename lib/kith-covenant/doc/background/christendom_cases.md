# Christendom cases: voluntary cross-polity cooperation frameworks

## Why this document exists

The Christian cases in
[Historical cases](historical_cases.md) (Westphalia and the
*jus publicum Europaeum*, the Peace of God and Truce of God, the
Hanseatic League, the *lex mercatoria*, and the codes duello)
are the most directly relevant historical precedents for
kith-covenant because they share the structural feature
kith-covenant is trying to instantiate: *voluntary peer
cooperation among polities that do not share a sovereign*. The
treatment of each in historical_cases.md is compact, which suits
a document that surveys a broad case set across many traditions.
Several of these cases warrant more detailed treatment, and
Westphalia in particular carries an outsized share of the
argumentative load because the *jus publicum Europaeum* is the
most developed historical instance of exactly the institutional
form kith-covenant aspires to.

This document supplies that increased detail. It covers the same
five Christendom cases that appear in
[Historical cases](historical_cases.md), but with
substantially fuller treatment of the founding negotiations, the
institutional mechanisms, and the actual texts with their
specific provisions, the decay vectors and how they actually
played out, and the anti-spiral features
([Justus hostis](justus_hostis.md#the-anti-spiral-features-why-anchor-above-self-is-necessary-but-not-sufficient))
in their specifically Christendom-produced forms. It complements
[The Buddhist sangha](buddhist_sangha.md), which provides
comparable depth on the sangha evidence. The two documents
should be read as a pair, since the Christian-particularist
verdict in
[The noticeable hand](noticeable_hand.md#counterweights) rests on
the comparison between them.

Two framing points structure the treatment.

**First, "Christendom" is not "Christianity."** The Christendom
cases are products of a specific historical civilization,
late-medieval and early-modern Latin Christian Europe. Its
features go beyond doctrinal Christianity to include the
institutional legacy of the Western Roman Church, the
distinctive Western European integration of Roman law with canon
law, the patrimonial-feudal political organization inherited
from the Carolingian settlement, and the tradition of
Aristotelian-Thomistic rational theology. When this document
refers to the Christian substrate, it means Christianity as it
functioned in this civilizational matrix, not Christianity
considered abstractly.

**Second, the cases are not reducible to one story**. When no
central state existed, the Peace of God grew out of
ecclesiastical authority organizing itself against lordly
violence. The Hanse and lex mercatoria grew out of merchant
networks operating across royal and ducal jurisdictions. The
codes duello regulated personal honor violence within a specific
aristocratic class; Westphalia ended a war of confessional and
dynastic conflict and established a framework among sovereign
states. Each case is its own regime with its own institutional
logic, and treating them as a single "Christian cooperation
achievement" obscures what each actually did. The cross-case
patterns recover only at the level of the substrate
(transcendent-moral-authority structure with the anti-spiral
features) and at the level of certain recurring institutional
devices (oath-binding before a recognized sacred ultimate. Named
paths to restitution. Recognition of the adversary as a peer
worthy of full standing).

## Westphalia and the jus publicum Europaeum. Extended treatment

### The war being ended

The Thirty Years' War (1618-1648) began as a Bohemian revolt
against Habsburg Catholic consolidation, escalated through four
largely distinct phases, Bohemian (1618-1625), Danish
(1625-1629), Swedish (1630-1635), and French (1635-1648), and
ended as a general European war whose participants had largely
lost sight of the original confessional dispute. The Imperial
Diet could not meet in Regensburg as a normal political
instrument by 1630. The authority of the Emperor in the Empire
was contested by both Protestant and Catholic Estates; Spain,
France, Sweden, and the Dutch Republic were all operating as
external belligerents with complex alliance structures.
Estimated mortality for the German-speaking lands runs from 15%
to 35% of the population in the worst-affected regions;
Magdeburg (1631) was sacked by Imperial forces with ~20,000
killed, an episode that defined the character of the war in
European memory.

By the mid-1630s the belligerents were exhausted but unable to
disengage because no party held a position strong enough to dictate
terms and no mechanism existed to negotiate a general settlement.
The Peace of Prague (May 1635) between the Emperor and the Saxons
was a partial settlement that Sweden and France declined to join.
Its failure demonstrated that any durable peace would have to
include all major belligerents. Preliminary negotiations for a
general peace began in 1636, and the formal congresses at Münster
(Catholic and secular powers) and Osnabrück (Protestant powers and
the Emperor) opened in 1644.

### The negotiating congress

The Westphalian congress was a new kind of diplomatic event. The
parties did not assemble in a single location. The Imperial
negotiators worked between the two cities, Catholic envoys from
France, Spain, the representative of the Pope, and various
Catholic Imperial Estates met at Münster, Protestant envoys from
Sweden and various Protestant Imperial Estates met at Osnabrück.
Dutch and Spanish representatives negotiated their separate
peace at Münster.

Approximately 194 officially accredited delegations
participated: Empire (both Emperor and Imperial Estates in their
own right), France, Spain, Sweden, Dutch Republic, Venice (as
mediator), the Papacy (as mediator), Denmark (which had formally
exited the war in 1629 but maintained an observer role), and
dozens of Imperial Estates sending their own representatives.
The scale was unprecedented. So was the fact that smaller
Imperial Estates (dukes, counts, free cities) negotiated
directly rather than as parties to the position of the Emperor.
This produced two institutional innovations whose effects
outlasted the specific settlement:

- **Formal congress diplomacy.** A set of procedural rules for
  multi-party peace negotiations, including credentials review,
  rotating chair at sub-conferences, written positions with formal
  replies, and the production of a single consolidated text.
  Subsequent European congresses, Nijmegen (1678-79), Rijswijk
  (1697), Utrecht (1713), Aachen (1748), and eventually Vienna
  (1815), inherited this form.
- **Protocol and precedence.** Rules about who takes the floor
  first, who is seated where, which party signs which document,
  and how rank is signaled in forms of address. The Westphalian
  protocol rules of the congress, further refined in Nijmegen
  and Vienna, became the ground of the modern diplomatic
  precedence system. Precedence was not merely ceremonial. In a
  regime where formal equality among sovereigns was the
  operative fiction, the ordering of names in a treaty and of
  delegations in a congress was the visible instantiation of the
  political order.

The representative of the Pope at Münster, Fabio Chigi (later
Pope Alexander VII), formally protested the religious provisions
of the settlement (most notably in the 1648 bull *Zelo domus
Dei*), but the Catholic signatories proceeded without papal
endorsement. The Catholic willingness of the powers to conclude
a settlement over the objections of the Pope is itself
diagnostic of the new order: *cujus regio, eius religio* and its
Westphalian extension created a political order that was
theologically Christian but not subject to ecclesiastical
supervision.

### The texts: IPM and IPO

The Peace of Westphalia consists of two treaties, signed
simultaneously on 24 October 1648:

- The **Instrumentum Pacis Osnabrugensis (IPO)**, between the
  Emperor Ferdinand III and Queen Christina of Sweden, with the
  Protestant Imperial Estates as co-parties. 17 articles, much of
  the text in German and Latin.
- The **Instrumentum Pacis Monasteriensis (IPM)**, between the
  Emperor and King Louis XIV of France, with the Catholic Imperial
  Estates as co-parties. 120 paragraphs, primarily in Latin.

The two texts are largely parallel, with differences reflecting the
different confessional composition of the signatory groups. Together
they constitute "the Peace of Westphalia" as the historical term is
used. The Spanish-Dutch Treaty of Münster (30 January 1648) is
sometimes included under the rubric. It ended the Eighty Years' War
and formally recognized Dutch independence from Spain.

The principal provisions:

**Religious settlement (IPO Article V; IPM equivalents).** The
confessional status of each territory was fixed to what it had
been in the *Normaljahr* of 1 January 1624, a compromise between
Protestant demands for a later *Normaljahr* (which would have
reflected Protestant advances) and Catholic demands for an
earlier one (which would have reflected pre-war Catholic
consolidation). *Ius reformandi* (the right of the ruler to
determine his confession of the territory) was confirmed from
Augsburg 1555. Subjects of a territory whose ruler changed
confession retained the *ius emigrandi* (right to emigrate,
carrying their property, within a defined period) and were
guaranteed the right of domestic private practice of the *other*
confession in specified circumstances. Calvinism was added to
Lutheranism and Catholicism as a recognized confession,
correcting the Augsburg restriction. *Ius emigrandi* formalized
the limitation on the prior ability of the system to convert
populations by force.

**Territorial provisions (IPM).** France received Alsace (with
complex carve-outs for Strasbourg and other free cities that kept
their imperial status), the *Trois-Évêchés* of Metz, Toul, and
Verdun (confirmed from earlier French possession), and Breisach and
Pinerolo. Sweden received Western Pomerania, Stettin, the port of
Wismar, Bremen and Verden as secularized bishoprics, and substantial
indemnity payments. The Swiss Confederation and the Dutch Republic
had their independence from the Empire explicitly confirmed.

**Constitutional provisions (IPO Article VIII).** The Imperial
Estates received the *ius foederis* (the right to conclude
alliances with foreign powers and among themselves), provided
these alliances were not directed against the Emperor, the
Empire, the public peace, or the Peace of Westphalia itself.
This provision, more than any other, established the
quasi-sovereignty of the Imperial Estates. Combined with the
constitutional guarantees of the Imperial three colleges of the
Diet (Electors, Princes, and Free Cities) and the *itio in
partes* (separate deliberation by confession) on religious
questions, this made the Empire a hybrid entity. Neither a
unitary state nor a purely contractual federation, but a complex
layered structure whose internal order was constitutionally
fixed against the ability of the Emperor to modify it.

**Guarantor provisions.** France and Sweden became explicit
guarantors of the Peace, meaning they had treaty-rooted standing
to protest and ultimately to intervene if the Emperor attempted
to override the settlement. This was a distinct institutional
innovation. The peace was not merely a bilateral compromise with
the Emperor but a multi-party settlement whose observance could
be enforced by external guarantors invited to do so by the text
itself. The guarantor role was exercised in the 1680s (the
annexations by France under the *Réunions* policy were grounded
in claimed interpretations of Westphalian provisions), in 1700
(Spanish succession), and into the eighteenth century before
gradually atrophying as the Empire became a
Franco-Prussian-Austrian triangle rather than a Westphalian
subject.

**Amnesty provisions.** Paragraph 2 of the IPM and equivalent IPO
provisions established a general amnesty. No party was to prosecute
any other for acts committed during the war, and territories that
had changed confession or political allegiance under duress were to
be restored to the 1624 baseline without punishment. This
encoded at the text level the architecture of confession-and-return
([Bounded conflict](bounded_conflict.md#protocol-sketch)). A
named path back to standing after violation, structurally rather
than discretionarily granted.

**Procedural provisions for future disputes.** Disputes among
signatories were to be resolved by negotiation, with escalation to
armed enforcement only after three years of failed negotiation.
This is an early version of compulsory negotiation-before-force,
similar to dispute-resolution clauses in later treaties, and a
deliberate attempt to prevent fast re-escalation.

### The "Westphalian system" as distinct from the treaty text

A distinction careful historiography has pressed. The *jus publicum
Europaeum* as a developed system of international law and practice
is not identical to what the 1648 texts actually agreed. Andreas
Osiander (1994, 2001), Stephen Krasner (1999), and others have
argued that the "Westphalian myth" (reading territorial
sovereignty, non-intervention, and the modern state system directly
out of the 1648 text) substantially misrepresents both what the
treaties said and what they accomplished. The actual 1648 settlement
was deeply entangled with the specific Imperial constitutional
order, with the confessional question, and with the dynastic claims
of the participating houses. The emergence of a *general* European
system of sovereign equality and non-intervention took another ~150
years of institutional development. Through the resident-embassy
consolidation of the late 17th century, the Utrecht settlement
(1713), the elaboration of international law in Grotius and his
successors (Pufendorf, Vattel, Wolff), the Congress of Vienna
(1814-15), and the institutional practices of the Concert of Europe.

The precise formulation: **Westphalia initiated the institutional
trajectory whose mature form was the nineteenth-century European
state system**, and the specific 1648 provisions encoded several of
the load-bearing features of that system (formal equality among
sovereigns in the Imperial-Estates system, the right of alliance,
the amnesty-and-restoration architecture, the guarantor mechanism,
and the compulsory-negotiation provision), but the 1648 texts themselves
did not fully articulate the principles the 19th century retroactively
attributed to them. The trajectory is real. The myth of an
instantaneous 1648 revolution in international order is not.

This matters for kith-covenant. The lesson is that a mature
cross-polity cooperation framework is not produced by a single
foundational document, however important. It emerges through
iterative institutional development (congresses, diplomatic
practice, treaty elaboration, jurisprudential consolidation) over
generations, with the foundational document setting direction rather
than delivering the finished system.

### How the system actually worked

For approximately 270 years (1648-1914), the European state system
exhibited several characteristic features that are not trivial to
produce and that kith-covenant needs to understand in specificity.

**Sovereign equality in formal procedure, disparity in real
capacity**. The Diet of the Empire treated the Electorate of
Mainz (a small archbishopric) and the Electorate of Brandenburg
(becoming Prussia, a major power) as equal parties in their
respective college. On the European stage, the Treaty of Utrecht
(1713) treated the Prince-Bishopric of Liège and the Kingdom of
France as equal signatories despite utterly different material
weights. Real great- power disparities continued to operate. The
smaller powers were dependent on great-power protection, the
Great Powers met in separate side-conferences, and the Concert
of Europe after 1815 formalized the great-power oversight role.
But the formal-equality fiction did work. Small sovereigns
retained recognition, territory, and standing: real recognition,
real territorial integrity most of the time, and real diplomatic
standing. That was not trivial compared to alternative orders
(the Mongol khaganate, the Chinese tributary system,
colonial-era direct rule over non-European polities) in which
small polities were simply absorbed or subordinated.

**Resident embassies with codified immunities.** Before
Westphalia, embassies were ad-hoc missions dispatched for
specific negotiations. The institution of *resident* embassies
(permanent diplomatic representation at major capitals)
consolidated in the late 17th and 18th centuries. By 1750 every
major European capital hosted resident embassies from the other
European courts, with a codified corpus of diplomatic immunities
(person, residence, communications) that had achieved
effectively universal recognition. The institutional innovation
was substantial. A resident ambassador functioned as an
advance-posted standing listener whose reporting shaped the
policy of the home-court in real time, collapsing the
information-latency that had previously made pre-Westphalian
diplomacy responsive only to crisis.

**Treaty law as the operative idiom.** Disputes ended in written
treaty texts, drafted by professionals trained in international
law, signed with formal ratification procedures, registered, and
circulated in print. The 1648 IPM/IPO were among the earliest
treaties printed as official texts immediately after signing. By
the 18th century the *Corps universel diplomatique du droit des
gens* (Dumont, 1726-1739) compiled treaties as a reference
corpus for practitioners. Treaty public character of the law
(the availability of the text to all parties, the formal
ratification record, the clarity of the provisions) was
load-bearing. A sovereign could be held to account against the
specific language of the treaty she had ratified, and departures
from treaty obligations were publicly identifiable rather than
contested as mere political assertions.

**A working *jus in bello* distinct from *jus ad bellum*.** The
great achievement of the *jus publicum Europaeum* was the
separation of *cause* of war from *conduct* of war. Wars could
be fought lawfully (under recognized procedural rules: formal
declaration, combatant identification, prisoner exchange,
combatant-noncombatant distinction, neutrality respected, treaty
termination) even when their causes were dubious. Because it did
not depend on anyone agreeing about the cause, the procedure
could be preserved across cause-shifts. This is the *justus
hostis* move at institutional scale
([Justus hostis](justus_hostis.md)). The opponent in a lawful
war is a legitimate peer in combat, whose rights to surrender,
to ransom, to return of captured officers under parole, to
protected status for medical personnel, and (eventually) to
Geneva-Convention treatment for wounded and prisoners were
recognized without reference to the justice of her cause. Even
when the cause of her sovereign was theologically mistaken, the
*jus in bello* worked, because the substrate recognized every
combatant as an *imago Dei*.

**Dispute-resolution short of war.** Congresses (Nijmegen 1678,
Rijswijk 1697, Utrecht 1713, Aachen 1748, Paris 1763, Paris 1783,
Vienna 1814) ended wars and adjudicated disputes that might
otherwise have escalated. By the 19th century, non-congress
mediation (e.g., Wilhelm I of Germany arbitrating the San Juan
Islands dispute between Britain and the US in 1872) was part of the
standard repertoire. This was a real achievement. The *jus publicum*
provided multiple paths short of war for resolving disputes that in
other orders (pre-Westphalian, Mongol-imperial, tributary) would
have been resolved by unilateral action or by war.

### The decay vectors in detail

The *jus publicum Europaeum* did not die in a single collapse. It
was eroded by a series of specific structural shocks across ~200
years, each of which exposed a latent vulnerability the 1648
settlement had not addressed.

**The partitions of Poland (1772, 1793, 1795).** Three collusive
acts by Russia, Prussia, and Austria (the three Great Powers
surrounding the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth) progressively
divided and eventually eliminated Poland as a sovereign state.
Each partition was negotiated among the three powers without
Polish consent, justified by internal-Polish political "anarchy"
that the partitioning powers had themselves engineered. The
fundamental violation. Three formal-equal members of the system
eliminated a fourth formal-equal member by collusion. The
Westphalian system had no institutional response to this. No
standing mechanism by which the other European powers could
protect a formal-equal sovereign against combined great-power
action. The partitions are therefore not a "failure" of the
system in the sense of falling below its own standards. They are
a demonstration that the system had an institutional gap its
formal equality principle could not close. Kith-Covenant must
design against this specific failure mode. The protection of the
compact for members against combined action by a subset of other
members must be structurally stronger than the Westphalian
equivalent was.

**The Napoleonic disruption (1796-1815).** Revolutionary and
Napoleonic France operated as a state whose foreign policy was
shaped by an ideological project (spreading the Revolution) that
was explicitly universalist and explicitly incompatible with
Westphalian formal-equality-among-existing-sovereigns. The
reordering by Napoleon of central Europe (the Confederation of
the Rhine, 1806; the dissolution of the Holy Roman Empire, 1806;
the Duchy of Warsaw, 1807; successive territorial
reorganizations in Germany, Italy, and Iberia) treated existing
sovereignties as interchangeable instruments rather than as
formal equals whose standing was the basis of the order. Although the Congress of Vienna (1814-15) restored a version of the Westphalian order, the restoration was imperfect. The Holy Roman Empire was not
    re-constituted, the German Confederation (1815-1866)
    replaced it with a looser structure whose coherence was
    undermined by Prussian-Austrian competition, and the Concert
    of the explicit European  Great Power condominium overrode
    the formal equality of smaller sovereigns even more
    explicitly than the pre-Napoleonic practice had.

**The colonial frontier.** From the 16th century onward, the
European powers systematically excluded non-European polities
from the *jus publicum*. The colonial frontier is the single
largest counter-evidence against any reading of the *jus
publicum* as universal. Within Europe, sovereigns recognized
each other as peers. Outside Europe, European states treated
non-European political communities as subjects, territories, or
sources of extraction, with occasional exceptions for polities
recognized as sufficiently "civilized" (the Ottoman Empire,
Japan after Meiji). This is the failure mode treated at length
in [The noticeable hand](noticeable_hand.md#counterweights). The
scriptural mandate of the Christian substrate, universal
neighbor-love (Luke 10), was not operationally applied to
non-European neighbors encountered face-to-face. The Westphalian
internal coherence of the system depended on having an outside
where its rules did not apply, and that outside was most of
humanity.

**The nationalist principle.** From the mid-19th century onward,
the basis of political legitimacy shifted from recognition of
sovereigns to recognition of peoples. The unifications of Italy
(1861) and Germany (1871), the recognition of Balkan states at the
Congress of Berlin (1878), and eventually the Wilsonian
self-determination principle at Versailles (1919) replaced the
sovereign-to-sovereign model with a people-to-people model. This
had genuine benefits (the formal equality of nations replaced the
formal equality of dynasties as the operative fiction) but three
consequences that weakened the *jus publicum.* It made domestic
ethnic composition an international-policy matter (creating the
minority-protection problem that devoured interwar European
diplomacy); it fragmented previously-unified imperial territories
into many smaller and less-viable states. And it introduced an
explicit metric (national self-realization) that could be
asserted against the existing order rather than adjudicated within
it.

**Universalist ideologies after 1914.** The twentieth-century
universalisms (Wilsonian liberalism, Leninist communism,
fascist/Nazi race-ideology, and, later, neoconservative
democratism) each treated the opposing carrier states of the
ideology as criminal or heretical rather than as legitimate
peers. The post-1945 explicit framing of the order of Nazi
Germany and Imperial Japan as criminal enterprises to be tried
(Nuremberg, Tokyo) restored something like the pre-Westphalian
confessional model. The opponent is not a legitimate peer in
lawful war, she is a criminal whose cause is itself outside the
bounds of legitimacy. This move has real moral justification
(the specific atrocities of Nazi rule warrant the specific
judgment), but its structural effect is to evacuate the *jus in
bello / jus ad bellum* distinction that had previously allowed
wars to end in settlements between continuing peers. War-crimes
prosecution, the humanitarian-intervention doctrine, and the
responsibility-to-protect framework all inherit this structure.

**1914 and after.** The First World War did not destroy the *jus
publicum* at its outset: the declarations of war, the initial
diplomatic exchanges, even the early conduct of combat operated
within the inherited forms. What the war did was destroy the
*substrate* on which the forms rested. The shared European
upper- class Christian civilization whose members recognized
each other as peers across borders had been eroded by
mass-politics, industrial warfare, and the radicalization
produced by the duration of the war itself. By 1918 the
personnel and the culture that had operated the *jus publicum*
were largely dead or discredited. The Versailles Treaty (1919)
treated Germany as a criminal defendant rather than as a
defeated peer whose standing was to be restored. And the
interwar institutional experiments of the period (League of
Nations, Kellogg-Briand, disarmament conferences) operated on
principles that were substantively different from those of the
*jus publicum*.

### Lesson for kith-covenant

The *jus publicum Europaeum* is the most directly relevant
historical precedent for kith-covenant. Several lessons, of
varying specificity, follow.

**Formal equality among members, regardless of material weight,
is structurally load-bearing and must be designed against both
insider and outsider threats**. The Westphalian formal-equality
principle worked, within its operative scope, to protect smaller
sovereigns against casual absorption. It failed in the specific
case of combined great-power action (the Polish partitions) and
in the specific case of ideological-state challenge (Napoleon).
Kith- Covenant commitment to formal equality must include
explicit procedural protection against combined action by a
subset of members against another member, and a credible answer
to ideologically-driven external pressure.

**A mature cross-polity cooperation framework is produced by
iterative institutional development, not by a founding document
alone**. Westphalia initiated the trajectory. The *jus publicum*
in its mature form was a product of another 150 years of
elaboration. Kith-Covenant should expect that its founding
documents set direction rather than deliver the finished system,
and should plan for iterative institutional development as a
routine activity of the first several years of the compact, its
generations.

**The *jus in bello / jus ad bellum* distinction is the *justus
hostis* move at system scale and must be preserved.** When the
distinction collapsed (after 1914, and again in the post-1945
humanitarian-intervention framework), the ability of the regime
to terminate conflict in settlements among continuing peers
substantially degraded. The anathema procedure of Kith-Covenant
([Justus hostis](justus_hostis.md#hostis-humani-generis-limits-of-the-justus-hostis-frame))
reserves criminal-status treatment for specific categories of
violation. The default for disputes between members and between
members and neighbors is peer-recognition, and the anathema
narrowness of the procedure is itself a deliberate structural
commitment against the late-modern slide.

**Compulsory negotiation before force, amnesty-and-restoration,
and guarantor mechanisms are real Westphalian institutional
innovations that kith-covenant can adopt**. They are the three-year
negotiation window before armed escalation, the amnesty clauses
automatically restoring territorial status absent prosecution, and
the explicit guarantor role for external parties invited into the
treaty text. These are mechanisms whose effectiveness in the
*jus publicum* is well-attested, and they are transferrable to
inter-community protocol of kith-covenant.

**Resident representation with codified immunities is the
working precedent for continuous cross-community presence**. The
inter-community recognition of kith-covenant and cooperation
work benefits from continuous, informed, protected
cross-community representation. The resident-embassy institution
is the proven mechanism for this in the Westphalian tradition,
and kith-covenant should study its mature form (late-17th-
through 19th-century practice) as the template for its own
continuous-presence provisions.

**Kith-Covenant must design against the colonial-frontier failure
mode**. The *jus publicum* worked within Europe and failed
catastrophically at the frontier with the non-European world. The
Samaritan-parable correction (discernment within actual encounter,
regardless of community of origin) is the substrate-level answer.
The compact-level answer is to ensure that the inter-community
recognition machinery applies symmetrically to all encountered
parties, with the member/neighbor/anathema/non-member distinctions
operating on conduct rather than on community origin. This is
already operative in
[Recognition protocols](recognition_protocols.md#members-neighbors-enemies-the-four-categories);
the historical record is why.

## The Peace of God and Truce of God. Extended treatment

### The collapse that produced it

The Peace of God (*Pax Dei*) emerged in late-10th- and
early-11th- century Aquitaine, the Midi, and Burgundy, under
conditions of Carolingian political collapse. The Carolingian
unified authority of the Empire, which had structured central
and western Europe from the coronation of Charlemagne (800)
through the mid-9th century, had by ~950 fragmented into a
multiplicity of local lordships whose holders commanded private
armies and extracted revenue by violence. The the property of
bishops and monasteries, the peasantry, smallholders, merchants
in transit, and religious pilgrims were systematically subject
to predation (not by organized state authority but by private
armed entrepreneurship), and neither the remnant royal authority
nor the feudal-hierarchical order could provide effective
protection.

Into this gap the episcopate stepped. The 989 Synod of Charroux
(Poitou, Aquitaine), convoked by Archbishop Gombaud of Bordeaux and
attended by 5 bishops of the southwest, is the conventional
starting point of the movement. The Synod declared three
anathemas: against those who broke into churches, those who
violently took the property of the poor, and those who struck
clerics. This was an unusual institutional move. A regional
ecclesiastical assembly declaring universal moral law against
specific categories of violent predation, enforceable not by the
royal power (which was absent or ineffective) but by the sacred
authority of the assembled bishops.

### The development of the movement

Subsequent synods at Le Puy (990), Anse (994), Narbonne (1054),
Elne (1027), and others expanded both the protected categories
and the enforcement mechanism. By the mid-11th century the
movement included:

- **The Peace of God proper.** Categories of person and place
  protected from violence at all times: clergy, monks, women
  (all women, not only specifically religious women), peasants,
  merchants, pilgrims, Jews (in many synods); churches,
  cemeteries, mills, and agricultural tools. Violators incurred
  automatic excommunication.
- **The Truce of God** (*Treuga Dei*). Periods during which all
  violence was forbidden: initially Sunday, then extending
  through Wednesday evening to Monday morning (to preserve the
  Triduum), and eventually through the liturgical major seasons
  of the year (Lent, Advent, the Octaves of major feasts). The
  rationale was explicitly liturgical. These were the times of
  Christ's passion, resurrection, and the major mysteries, and
  violence during them was an offense against the cosmic
  calendar.
- **Peace leagues** (*paix*). Sworn associations of laymen
  pledged to enforce the Peace by collective action against
  violators. The bishops administered the oaths and convened the
  leagues. The laymen supplied the enforcement. This was the
  concrete mechanism by which the Peace was operationalized against
  violators who did not fear excommunication alone.
- **Relic veneration at synods.** The assembled bishops brought
  major relics (the body of St. Martial at Limoges, the relics of
  St. Hilary at Poitiers) to the synod, and oaths were sworn on the
  relics. The physical presence of the saints at the synod was held
  to make the oaths directly binding before the Saint and ultimately
  before God.

By 1027 the movement had spread from Aquitaine and Burgundy into
the rest of France. By 1081 Pope Urban II endorsed the Peace at
the Council of Reims. By the 12th century the Peace was
absorbed into the canonical tradition (Gratian's *Decretum*, 1140s).

### How it actually worked

The enforcement mechanism of the Peace deserves specific
attention. In a period when no central state authority existed,
the movement had to rely on sacred-substrate authority alone:

- **Excommunication** was the primary canonical weapon. An
  excommunicate could not receive the sacraments, could not be
  buried in consecrated ground, could not hold office, and lost
  the oaths of his vassals (in the mature theory). This was a
  substantial social and (in the cosmology of the period)
  eschatological penalty.
- **Relic-witnessed oaths** were the vernacular supplement. A
  lord sworn on St. Martial to keep the Peace had put his
  salvation and his welfare of the lineage at stake in the
  accounts of the saint. Oath- breaking was held to invite not
  only eschatological consequence but immediate saintly
  vengeance.
- **Peace leagues** provided the physical enforcement against
  violators who did not respect the sacred sanctions. When the
  Archbishop of Bourges organized a peace league in 1038, the
  league fielded armed laymen against recalcitrant lords. The
  forces of the archbishop itself were defeated by the lords at
  La Châtre in 1038 (an instance of the limits of the
  institutional capacity of the movement against well-resourced
  violators), but the mechanism was real.
- **Penances** for violators included pilgrimage (often to distant
  shrines), fines paid to monasteries, restoration of plundered
  property, and formal reconciliation ceremonies with the
  offended parties. The confession-restitution-return architecture
  is explicit in the mature Peace tradition.

### What the Peace accomplished and did not

Where the movement functioned, it substantially reduced
predation on the protected categories. Peasants, merchants, and
pilgrims in 11th-century Aquitaine and Burgundy experienced a
measurable decline in lordly violence. Monastic economic
activity flourished within the protection; Jewish communities
(protected in several synods) had more reliable access to legal
protection than before or after. While the movement did not
eliminate lordly violence, it regulated and restricted it, and
the restriction was substantial.

The most important achievement of the movement for purposes of
kith-covenant is what it *demonstrated in principle.* That
categories of inviolability can be sustained across polities
without state authority, by sacred substrate alone, in a period
when no state was strong enough to do the work. The protected
categories were not the subjects of the king or the subjects of
the lord. They were categories of person defined by
ecclesiastical authority and recognized across political
jurisdictions. A peasant under the Peace was protected in Poitou
and in Burgundy alike. A pilgrim was protected through many
different lordly jurisdictions on the route to Compostela or
Rome.

### The absorption of the movement

The Peace of God did not collapse so much as get overtaken by
stronger institutions that performed overlapping functions:

- **The rising territorial monarchies** (Capetian France from
  ~1150; Angevin England; the Empire under Hohenstaufen from
  ~1150) developed royal peace (*pax regis*) traditions that
  absorbed and generalized the ecclesiastical Peace. By the 13th
  century the royal peace was the operative institution in most
  of western Europe. The ecclesiastical Peace continued but as a
  supplement rather than the primary enforcement mechanism.
- **The Crusading movement** (from 1095) redirected aristocratic
  violence outward. Urban II's call at Clermont specifically
  invoked the Peace argument. The knights should not fight each
  other but should fight the infidel in defense of Christendom.
  The Crusading redirection relieved the domestic-violence
  pressure the Peace was addressing, at the cost of
  externalizing it onto non-Christian populations. This is the
  most sobering aspect of the history of the Peace for
  kith-covenant. The mechanism that succeeded in bounding
  domestic violence *also* supplied the moral grammar for its
  redirection against an external enemy, and the record of
  atrocity in the Crusades demonstrates that the redirection was
  not bounded in the same way.
- **Codification in canon law** (Gratian's *Decretum*, 1140s;
  further in the Extravagantes) preserved the substantive
  content of the Peace as part of the universal canon law of
  Latin Christendom. The doctrinal contribution of the Peace to
  *jus in bello* (protected categories of non-combatants) became
  the direct ancestor of the Geneva-Convention tradition of
  combatant/non-combatant distinction.

### Lesson for kith-covenant

Three sharp lessons follow:

First, **a substrate-only enforcement regime can work at scale
across polities that share no common government, provided the
substrate has real bite**. The Peace of God is the historical
existence-proof: categories, sanctions, and oaths, with no state
behind any of them. The bite of the substrate came from the
credibility of excommunication as sanction, which in turn came
from the credibility of the sacred cosmology excommunication
operated within. If kith- covenant sanction analogous to it
(exclusion from the compact) is to carry comparable weight, the
compact itself must be sacred- equivalent in the sense that its
substrates of the members treat the integrity of the compact as
a matter of interior binding and not merely of contractual
convenience.

Second, **a peace regime that supplies an externalization path
for the violence it is bounding is structurally vulnerable to
the Crusading redirection**. The Peace of God internally bounded
European violence and externally enabled the Crusades. The
Kith-Covenant compact must design against this specific failure
mode. The success of the compact at bounding violence among
members must not produce a legitimated externalization path
against non-members that replicates at the frontier what it
prevents internally. The Samaritan-parable correction in
[The noticeable hand](noticeable_hand.md#counterweights) and
[Recognition protocols](recognition_protocols.md#members-neighbors-enemies-the-four-categories)
is the substrate-level answer. The compact-level answer is
explicit recognition that the moral obligations of the compact
operate in every actual encounter with a non-member, not only in
encounters with members.

Third, **the confession-restitution-return architecture is
already visible in the mature Peace tradition**: the penances,
the pilgrimage-as-penalty, the formal reconciliation with the
offended party, the restoration of plundered property as a
condition of re-entry to sacramental standing. The protocol
sketch of Kith-Covenant in
[Bounded conflict](bounded_conflict.md#protocol-sketch) is
consistent with this tradition, and the penitential apparatus
developed specifically in the Peace of God (medieval *libri
paenitentiales*; mature sacramental theology of penance from the
12th century forward) is a source of concrete institutional
design for the restitution mechanisms kith-covenant needs.

## The Hanseatic League. Extended treatment

### Origins and early consolidation

The Hanseatic League did not have a single founding moment. Its
origins lie in the 12th- and 13th-century trading associations of
north German merchants operating in the Baltic and North Sea, whose
individual communities (*Hansen*) at foreign trading posts and whose
home-city councils gradually consolidated into an inter-city
network with formal consultative institutions.

Three phases of consolidation deserve specific mention:

- **Late 12th-century beginnings.** the refoundation of Lübeck
  under Henry the Lion (1159) and Imperial city charter (1226)
  positioned it as the hub of a Baltic-oriented trading system.
  German merchants established trading privileges at Visby
  (Gotland), Bergen (Norway), Novgorod, London, and elsewhere
  through the late 12th and early 13th centuries. At this stage
  the "Hanse" refers to merchant communities at specific foreign
  trading posts (the *Hansa teutonicorum* at Visby, for example)
  rather than to a league of cities.
- **13th-century Stadtehanse.** From the mid-13th century onward,
  the *Städtehanse* (league of cities) consolidated as the operative
  institutional form. Lübeck concluded agreements with Hamburg
  (1241), Rostock (1259), Wismar (1259), and others, establishing
  cooperative arrangements on trade privileges, defense, and
  coinage. By ~1280 the *Städtehanse* had effectively formed
  across the southern Baltic.
- **1356 Lübeck Hansetag.** The formal consolidation is
  conventionally dated to the Hansetag at Lübeck in 1356, which
  coordinated a successful campaign against Flemish commercial
  rivals and established the Hansetag as the consultative
  assembly of the League. Membership at peak reached
  approximately 160-200 cities, with variable participation. The
  "Hanse" was never a corporate body with a fixed membership
  list, but rather an overlapping network of associations whose
  core was consistent (Lübeck, Hamburg, Bremen, Cologne, Danzig,
  Riga, Reval, Visby, Bergen merchants, the London Steelyard)
  and whose peripheries shifted with the times.

### Institutional mechanisms

The Hanse operated through several tightly integrated institutions:

**The Hansetag.** The general consultative assembly, normally at
Lübeck (though in various cities on occasion; 73 assemblies are
recorded between 1356 and 1669). Representatives from member cities
attended. Decisions were made by quarter (Wendish, Westphalian-
Prussian, Saxon, Livonian) and consolidated. The Hansetag had no
binding authority over non-attending cities and no enforcement
mechanism beyond collective sanction, but its decisions shaped
League policy on trade agreements with foreign powers, the
privileges granted at the *Kontore*, responses to violations of
Hanseatic privileges, and internal disputes among member cities.
Decisions required broad agreement (consensus was normal. Votes
were rare). The institutional logic favored cautious incremental
adjustment rather than decisive collective action.

**The *Kontore*.** The four major *Kontore*, Steelyard (London),
Tyske Bryggen (Bergen), Peterhof (Novgorod), and Bruges (which
moved to Antwerp in the late 15th century), were self-governing
trading enclaves within foreign jurisdictions. Each had its own
internal governance, its own court for disputes among Hanseatic
merchants resident in the enclave, its own disciplinary
authority, and its own negotiated privileges from the host
sovereign (exemption from local taxes, freedom from local courts
in intra-Hanseatic disputes, exemption from staple-town
restrictions, guaranteed warehouse rights). The *Kontor* was the
operational unit of the commercial presence of the Hanse abroad:
the institutional counterpart to the resident embassy in the
later Westphalian system.

**Verhansung (collective expulsion).** The ultimate sanction against
member cities that defected from agreed positions or violated
League norms was *Verhansung.* Collective expulsion from the
League. A city under *Verhansung* lost access to all Hanseatic
trading privileges across northern Europe simultaneously. Its
merchants could not trade at any *Kontor*, could not claim
Hanseatic privileges in dispute with foreign parties, and could
not participate in League-backed negotiations. Given that the
Hanse controlled access to the major Baltic and North Sea trading
routes, *Verhansung* was a catastrophic commercial penalty. It was
imposed sparingly (Bremen in 1427; Brunswick in 1375; several
smaller cities at various points) and was typically followed by
negotiated re-admission once the violating city had made amends.
The confession-restitution-return architecture is visible here as
well: *Verhansung* was not a permanent exile but a bounded sanction
with a defined return path.

**Collective boycott as external weapon.** The main weapon of
the League against foreign powers who violated Hanseatic
privileges was coordinated boycott. The 1284 boycott of Norway
(over privileges at Bergen) produced Norwegian capitulation
within about a year. The 1367-70 war with Denmark, formally
concluded in the Treaty of Stralsund (1370), was fought by a
collective Hanseatic fleet against Valdemar IV's Denmark and
secured Hanseatic privileges in the Sound for the next century.
The 1436-41 Flemish conflict, the 1470-74 English conflict
(concluded at the Peace of Utrecht 1474), and numerous smaller
campaigns followed similar patterns: collective economic
pressure, occasional military action, negotiated settlement
restoring Hanseatic privileges.

**The *Seestern* and joint coinage.** The League standardized
weights and measures, coinage exchange rates, and maritime
insurance practices across member cities. This reduced
transaction costs across the internal market of the League and
made cross-jurisdictional commerce work without central
enforcement. Merchants could trade across the Baltic and North
Sea with substantial confidence about weights, quality
standards, and monetary value.

### How the Hanse made itself credible

The Hanse sustained cross-jurisdictional commerce at scale in a
period when royal courts were slow, expensive, and unpredictable,
and when the violent prevailing conditions made cross-polity trust
fragile. Several mechanisms made its credibility load-bearing:

**Reputation and exclusion as enforcement.** A merchant who
refused to honor a Hanseatic arbitral decision lost access to
the trading networks that recognized the decision. Because the
networks covered the major Baltic and North Sea ports, the loss
was severe. Because Hanseatic reputation traveled
(merchant-to-merchant at every *Kontor* and every fair), the
sanction applied almost immediately across the whole network.
This is the mechanism
[Reputation bridges](reputation_bridges.md) builds out for
kith- inter-community layer of the covenant.

**Predictable adjudication.** Disputes among Hanseatic merchants
were adjudicated by Hanseatic courts (intra-*Kontor* tribunals; the
Lübeck court for appeals; the Hansetag for League-level disputes)
using customary Hanseatic law. A body of practice that had
consolidated across the 13th and 14th centuries into recognizable
institutional form. Predictability was the key virtue. A merchant
could estimate with reasonable accuracy how a dispute would be
resolved before entering into the transaction, which made
cross-jurisdictional commerce insurable in a way it otherwise
would not be.

**Commonly-held infrastructure.** The *Kontore* provided shared
warehouses, shared translators, shared legal counsel, and (in
some cases) shared fortification. These were capital-intensive
investments whose value depended on the collective maintenance
of the League and whose commons-character locked in cooperative
behavior (see [Commons federation](commons_federation.md)).

### The decay in detail

the 16th-17th-century decay of the Hanse had multiple causes
whose relative weights have been debated. The standard account,
refined here:

**Rise of strong territorial states.** The consolidation of
royal authority in Sweden (Vasa dynasty from 1523), Denmark
(Christian III from 1534), Brandenburg-Prussia (Great Elector
from 1640), England (Tudor consolidation), and (to an extent)
France progressively produced states with standing navies and
capacity for sustained naval operations. The Hanse had no
comparable military instrument (its naval actions were ad-hoc
levies against specific opponents, not a standing force), and by
the 17th century the territorial states could simply force open
Baltic markets at will. Sweden's "dominion of the Baltic" after
the Peace of Stolbovo (1617) and the subsequent Thirty Years'
War made Sweden the effective master of the northern Baltic and
eastern shores; the position of Denmark in the Sound; the
rising commercial power of Brandenburg-Prussia, its reach: each
of these progressively ate into the Hanseatic operating space.

**Atlantic shift.** The 1490s-onward European Atlantic expansion
(Iberian voyages, later Dutch and English) shifted economic
gravity from the Baltic and North Sea toward the Atlantic and
beyond. The Dutch Republic in particular became a commercial
center that out-competed Hanseatic operations in the 16th-17th
centuries, absorbing much of the Baltic grain trade into its
Amsterdam-based networks. The commercial advantages of Amsterdam
were structural (capital pool, banking, insurance, information
networks) and were reinforced by Dutch naval power that the
Hanse could not match. By 1650, Amsterdam was the commercial hub
of the northern European economy; Lübeck was peripheral.

**The Reformation confessional split.** Hanseatic cities ended
up on different sides of the Lutheran/Catholic/Calvinist divide.
The first-order confessional fracture had secondary effects on
League operation. The religious settlements of rulers
constrained civic autonomy. Confessional alliance politics (the
Schmalkaldic League, the Catholic League) overrode League
loyalty. Inter-city trust attrited at the first-order
theological layer. But (as treated in
[Historical cases](historical_cases.md) and in the Hanse
section there) the substrate was *not* fractured by the
Reformation. Both Lutheran and Catholic cities continued to
swear before the God of the same scripture. The Hansetag
continued to meet; *Verhansung* continued to be a real sanction.
The ~150 years of continued operation after the Peace of
Augsburg (1555) is consistent with the substrate-thesis reading.
Although the first-order confessional split was total, the
cooperation mechanism survived it, because the substrate
persisted across it. The confessional split was an accelerant,
not a primary cause.

**Capture by host sovereigns.** As home territorial rulers
consolidated power, League membership progressively subordinated
to state policy. A 16th-century Hamburg still had substantial
civic autonomy. An 18th-century Hamburg (under Imperial
administration) had substantially less. A Baltic city under
Brandenburg-Prussian or Swedish rule could not collectively defy
its sovereign to honor Hanseatic positions. The capture was
gradual but cumulative, and by 1650 the major Hanseatic
foreign-policy autonomy of the cities was substantially
reduced.

**Failure to adapt institutional form.** The Hanse never developed
regular taxation, a standing fleet, a permanent secretariat, or
any other durable apparatus. It remained a consultative league
with ad-hoc mobilization in a period when the successful
cooperation frameworks (post-Westphalia) had acquired permanent
diplomatic corps, standing institutional mass, and sustained
coordination mechanisms. Several 17th-century reform proposals
(notably the Cologne Confederation of 1367 as precedent; several
16th-century proposals for a Hanseatic standing fleet) failed to
produce institutional change. The Hanse in 1650 was
institutionally almost identical to the Hanse of 1400, in a
European environment that had transformed.

**The symbolic endpoint.** The last Hansetag, at Lübeck in 1669,
was attended by nine cities: Lübeck, Hamburg, Bremen, Danzig,
Rostock, Brunswick, Cologne, Hildesheim, and Osnabrück. After
1669 no further Hansetag was convoked. The *Hansa Teutonica* (the
three "Hanseatic Cities" of Lübeck, Hamburg, and Bremen) continued
a reduced symbolic existence into the 19th century and remain
recognizable in modern German civic identity (Hansestadt in city
names; the FC Bayern Munich of Hansa Rostock; etc.), but the
institution as a working cross-polity cooperation regime ended
in the late 17th century.

### Lesson for kith-covenant

Three sharp lessons beyond those already summarized in
historical_cases.md, on capacity, distinction, and expulsion.

First, **a cross-polity league without standing institutional
capacity is brittle to the rise of strong territorial states**.
The greatest weakness of the Hanse was its refusal to develop
durable institutional mass (taxation, permanent staff, standing
fleet) that would have made it a genuine actor against states
with such capacity. Kith-Covenant must answer what its
*permanent capacity* is, in staff, revenue, and standing. This
is not "central authority" (the absence of central authority in
the Hanse was a feature, not a bug, for much of its working
life); it is some durable institutional mass that cannot be
casually overwhelmed. Continuous staff, continuous records,
continuous operational capacity in shared infrastructure.

Second, **the substrate/first-order distinction is load-bearing,
and the 150-year Reformation of the Hanse survival is the
clearest empirical demonstration of it**. If the substrate
persists, cooperation mechanisms that depend on substrate
continuity survive first-order theological or ideological
disagreement. The member communities of kith-covenant will
disagree on first-order matters. The compact will survive the
disagreement if its substrate (the anti-spiral features as
specified in
[Justus hostis](justus_hostis.md#the-anti-spiral-features-why-anchor-above-self-is-necessary-but-not-sufficient))
persists across the member communities.

Third, ***Verhansung* is a working precedent for collective
sanction by a sovereign-less league, and it operates through
exclusion from real commons.** The sanction had bite because the
*Kontore*, the trading privileges, and the shared infrastructure
were genuine commons whose loss was operationally severe. Kith-
Covenant sanction analogous to it (exclusion from compact
commons, as named in
[Commons federation](commons_federation.md)) has comparable
bite only to the extent that the compact commons are
operationally real: life support, navigation, rescue,
communications. This is why the commons federation work is the
infrastructure on which the anathema sanction of the compact
operates.

## Lex Mercatoria. Extended treatment

### Origins and historiographical note

The medieval law merchant (*lex mercatoria*) was customary law
made by and for merchants, developing from the late 11th century
onward around three major nodes. The Champagne fairs (late
12th-13th c.), the Mediterranean maritime cities (Venice, Genoa,
Pisa, Amalfi, Barcelona), and the Hanseatic North. Its classical
account in Berman's *Law and Revolution* (1983) presents it as a
unified body of transnational commercial law, administered by
merchant courts across Europe, whose substantive content was
remarkably consistent across jurisdictions. Recent scholarship,
particularly Emily Kadens, "The Myth of the Customary Law
Merchant" (2012), and Ron the work by Harris on early corporate
forms, has challenged this picture, arguing that actual practice
was more local and varied than the classical account suggests,
and that the unified "law merchant" is a 19th-century legal
fiction projected backward.

The precise formulation: **the lex mercatoria existed in a weaker
form than the classical account claims but in a stronger form than
the revisionists sometimes imply**. The medieval merchants did
have recognizable customs that traveled with them across
jurisdictions. They did have merchant-operated courts that
administered those customs. The customs did converge across
trading nodes to a substantial (though not total) degree. And the
resulting system did enable cross-jurisdictional commerce at a
scale that royal-court-only adjudication would not have supported.
What it did *not* have was a single unified code or a single
international jurisdiction. The customs varied by location and by
trade, the courts were local with inter-local coordination, and
the convergence was produced by network effects and mutual
adjustment rather than by top-down harmonization.

### Institutional mechanisms

**Custom-based rules.** The operative sources of the *lex
mercatoria* were customary. Accumulated practice recorded over
time in compilations. The major compilations included:

- **The *Consolato del Mare*** (Catalan-language compilation, 14th
  c., though drawing on earlier Mediterranean practice from the
  12th c. and before). Authoritative across the Mediterranean
  from the 14th century onward.
- **The *Lex Rhodia*** (the maritime law attributed to ancient
  Rhodes, preserved through Byzantine transmission as *Nomos
  Rhodion Nautikos* and applied across the Eastern Mediterranean
  through the Byzantine period and into the Islamic and medieval
  Christian successor states).
- **The Rolls of Oléron** (Atlantic French, 12th c.), the
  foundation of northern maritime law.
- **The Laws of Wisby** (Gotland, 14th c.), governing Baltic
  maritime practice.
- **The *Consuetudines* of Italian cities** (Genoa, Venice, Pisa)
  that partially codified commercial practice.

These compilations were not unified codes but rather attempts to
record and make authoritative the practices actually in use at
specific trading nodes. The practices overlapped substantially in
bills of exchange, maritime insurance, general average,
partnership forms (*commenda*), and bottomry, but differed in
specifics. A merchant operating across nodes needed to be
competent in the local variant at each node, with the common
denominator of the underlying commercial substance.

**Pie-powder courts (*curia pedis pulverizati*, "dusty feet").**
At English fairs (Bartholomew's Fair, St. Ives, Boston, etc.), a
special court operated during the fair itself, administered by
the organizer of the fair (a lord, an abbot, a town corporation)
and specializing in summary disposal of merchant disputes. The
court was named for the dusty feet of the merchants who brought
disputes. The feet of a merchant were still dusty from the road
when his case was heard and decided. Cases were decided same-day
where possible. Merchants could continue trading or move on to
the next fair without leaving unresolved disputes behind. The
pie-powder jurisdiction was a well-defined exception to
royal-court monopoly. It was recognized across English royal and
ecclesiastical fair grants, and analogous institutions operated
at continental fairs (Champagne fairs especially, where the
fair-wardens administered fair justice).

**Fair courts and consular courts.** The Champagne fairs, six
annual fairs in four Champenois towns (Lagny, Bar, Provins,
Troyes) running between 1100 and 1300, had standing fair courts
administered by the *Gardes des Foires*, with wide jurisdiction
over disputes among participating merchants. At the
Mediterranean ports, consular courts (e.g., at Barcelona,
Marseille, Genoa) administered commercial justice among
merchants of different nations resident at the port. Both
institutions provided dispute resolution tailored to needs of
the merchants: fast, predictable, commercial, and available
across jurisdictions.

**Reputation and exclusion.** Because the merchant networks were
relatively dense (the Champagne-fair attendees, the
Mediterranean-port traders, the Hanseatic merchants were
recognizable communities), the reputation of a merchant traveled
with him. A merchant who refused to honor an arbitral award or
who was known to cheat lost standing across the network. Other
merchants would not extend credit, would not partner on
ventures, would not honor bills of exchange drawn on him. The
enforcement was distributed and informal, but in the relatively
dense networks of the period it was operationally sufficient for
routine enforcement of the commercial bargain.

### What the lex mercatoria accomplished

For roughly 600 years (from the late 11th century to the mid-18th
century), cross-jurisdictional commerce operated at substantial
scale in a period when:

- Royal courts were slow, expensive, and unpredictable;
- No state had the institutional reach to adjudicate commercial
  disputes across jurisdictions directly;
- Multiple overlapping jurisdictions (royal, ecclesiastical,
  manorial, municipal) produced conflict-of-laws problems that
  state courts could not cleanly resolve;
- Warfare and political instability repeatedly disrupted
  state-level enforcement.

In this environment the lex mercatoria provided a functional
substitute. Merchants could trade with strangers in distant
lands with reasonable confidence that disputes would be
adjudicated by familiar standards, that the reputation of the
counterparty was legible, and that enforcement would be
available through the merchant network itself. The resulting
commerce, the 12th-13th- century Champagne fairs, the
13th-15th-century Mediterranean long- distance trade, the
14th-17th-century Hanseatic network, the 15th-17th-century
Italian financial houses (Medici, Bardi, Peruzzi), was
substantial in scale and consequential in its effects on
European economic development.

### Decay in detail

**Absorption into national law.** The absorption began in the
15th-16th centuries with the consolidation of national
commercial codes, accelerated in the 17th century, and
effectively completed in the 18th century. Key moments:

- **Lord Mansfield's incorporation** of the law merchant into
  English common law (1756-1788, through successive King's Bench
  decisions). Mansfield took the substantive content of mercantile
  custom as he found it in practice and incorporated it into
  common-law commercial jurisprudence, replacing pie-powder and
  fair-court jurisdictions with royal-court administration of
  the same substantive rules.
- **The Napoleonic Code de Commerce (1807)** codified French
  commercial law on the same principle. The customs were
  preserved in substance but made state-administered.
- **German commercial law consolidation** (*Allgemeines Deutsches
  Handelsgesetzbuch*, 1861; *Handelsgesetzbuch*, 1897) carried
  out the same operation for the German states.

**State-court jurisdictional assertion.** As royal and then
national courts consolidated, they asserted jurisdiction over
commercial disputes previously handled by merchant forums. The
assertion was not always resisted (merchants often preferred
state enforcement power of the courts once royal adjudication
had become adequately predictable), but the effect was to
eliminate the merchant forums as independent institutions.

**Standardization of contracts** moved from merchant-custom
development to state regulation. The form of the bill of exchange,
the corporate forms available for joint enterprise, the insurance
contract. All became state-regulated rather than merchant-
customary.

The *new lex mercatoria* (since the 1960s: ICC arbitration,
UNIDROIT Principles of International Commercial Contracts, the
CISG) is a partial revival, but it operates within space the
states permit. It differs from the medieval law merchant in that
its enforceability depends ultimately on state courts honoring
arbitration awards (New York Convention, 1958) rather than on an
independent merchant collective sanction of the community.

### Lesson for kith-covenant

Three lessons specific to this case:

First, **reputation-based, non-state cross-jurisdictional
cooperation is historically real, not an idealization**. The
classical account of the lex mercatoria may have been
exaggerated, but the underlying mechanism (merchant customs
administered by merchant forums with reputation-based
enforcement) was genuine and worked at scale for centuries. This
is the existence-proof for the kind of cooperation
kith-inter-community layer of the covenant needs to sustain.

Second, **the durability of the regime depends on what
reputation can travel on**. The medieval merchant network was
the substrate on which reputation traveled. Once states
centralized communication, banking, and adjudication, the
substrate was undercut and the regime was absorbed.
Kith-Covenant must answer. When most infrastructure is
captured by state actors, what is reputation portable on? The
[Reputation bridges](reputation_bridges.md) treatment
addresses this directly. The lex mercatoria case is the
principal historical reference.

Third, **absorption-by-state is a distinctive decay mode**
different from the Westphalian decay (insider collusion;
external ideological challenge) and the Hanse decay
(strong-state capture plus institutional ossification). Though
the substantive content of the *lex mercatoria* survived,
incorporated into state commercial law, the autonomous merchant
institutions that had sustained it did not. Kith-Covenant should
distinguish between substrate survival (the anti-spiral features
persisting in member communities) and institutional survival
(the specific institutions of the compact remaining as
independent actors). The two can come apart. The latter is the
harder test.

## Codes Duello. Extended treatment

### The emergence of the institution

The personal duel as a formal institution emerges in the Italian
courts of the late 15th and 16th centuries and spreads through the
European aristocracy in the 16th-17th centuries. Its specific
historical moment is the transition from medieval trial-by-combat
(a judicial procedure, framed as divine judgment) to early-modern
duel-of-honor (a private procedure, framed as the defense of
personal reputation).

Key codifications:

- **Italian treatises.** Girolamo Muzio's *Il Duello* (1550);
  earlier treatments in Paride del Pozzo's *De Duello* (1476)
  and Andrea Alciato's *Duello* (1544). These systematized a
  body of dueling practice whose substantive content had
  developed in the Italian city-states and migrated northward
  with Italian military and courtly influence.
- **French formalization.** In 16th-17th c. France the duel of
  honor became an institution of the nobility, with elaborate
  conventions around challenges, seconds, ground, weapons, and
  outcomes. The frequency was such that the French monarchy
  repeatedly attempted to suppress dueling (Henri IV's 1609
  edict; Richelieu's 1626 edict; Louis XIV's 1679 edict), and each
  attempt failed in practice while the practice continued
  under a thin veneer of concealment.
- **The Irish *Code Duello*** (formalized at Clonmel, County
  Tipperary, in 1777). The 26-article code represented a
  consolidation of 18th-century dueling practice and was adopted
  across the British Isles and the United States. It remains the
  most complete surviving procedural code of the institution.
- **German and Austrian codes** (various, 18th-19th c.). The
  German duel (particularly in the Austrian officer corps and
  among German student fraternities) developed its own
  codifications. Student dueling (*Mensur*) in German
  universities was a distinct institution, continuing in a
  ritualized non-lethal form into the 20th century.

### Institutional mechanisms in detail

**The challenge.** the entry point of the institution was the
formal challenge, which converted a perceived offense into a
procedural track with named rungs. The challenge was typically
in writing, specifying the offense and demanding satisfaction.
It was delivered by a second rather than by the challenger
directly. The acceptance by the recipient opened the procedure,
his refusal carried social consequences (reputation damage; in
some traditions, a public designation of cowardice) that
themselves functioned as sanction. The
challenge-as-procedure-entry is the critical innovation of the
institution. It converted private animosity into a channel with
defined escalation rungs and defined exits at each rung.

**Seconds.** Each principal appointed two seconds: typically
friends, occasionally brothers, sometimes professional "friends
of the duellists " hired for the purpose. The duties of the
seconds were elaborate:

- **Receiving and transmitting the challenge.** The seconds
  intermediated the initial exchange, so the principals did not
  speak directly during the pre-meeting phase.
- **Attempting resolution without a meeting.** This was the
  single most important duty of the seconds, their function.
  The procedural code directed them to exhaust possibilities of
  apology, explanation, or compromise before allowing the
  meeting to proceed. A competent second was an active
  de-escalator, not a mere witness. The Clonmel code gives
  detailed specification of what counts as adequate apology:
  apology sufficient to satisfy the honor of a gentleman,
  typically in writing, with specified scope corresponding to
  the scope of the offense.
- **Agreeing on weapons, time, ground, and rules.** The seconds
  negotiated the specifics. Swords (French smallsword was
  standard in 17th-18th c. France; rapier earlier in Italy),
  pistols (later), occasionally sabre (Germany, Austria). The
  measured paces for pistol duels. The ground (isolated,
  neutral, typically at dawn to minimize witnesses); the count
  (sometimes "1-2-3, fire"; sometimes "fire at your own election
  between the signals from the seconds "). Each specification
  was a substantive negotiation with implications for lethality.
- **Ensuring the meeting was conducted according to form**. If
  the meeting occurred, the seconds ensured that the rules were
  followed, the count was called correctly, and (if needed) that
  medical attention was available.
- **Witnessing the outcome and attesting to satisfaction of
  honor**. After the meeting, the seconds attested publicly that
  the procedure had been followed and that honor was satisfied.

**The meeting.** If the seconds could not resolve the dispute
without a meeting, the principals met on prepared ground under
the negotiated rules. The character of the meeting varied
substantially by weapon and tradition. Sword duels to first
blood (Italian tradition); sword duels to more serious wounding
(French 17th-century); pistol duels at measured paces with one
shot each (Irish 18th-century standard); pistol duels to
satisfaction (i.e., continuing until honor was satisfied,
potentially through multiple exchanges). The *deloping* practice
(deliberately firing wide or into the air, typically mutual) was
an important procedural out. Both principals could satisfy the
demand of the code for a meeting without either seriously trying
to kill the other. Though the Clonmel code explicitly forbids
deloping (Rule 13), reflecting one strand of the 18th-century
tradition, in practice deloping was common and accepted across
much of the period.

**Honor satisfied.** Once the meeting occurred according to form,
the dispute was formally closed regardless of outcome. The
principals resumed normal social relations (unless one had been
killed); subsequent challenge over the same matter was
procedurally barred. The honor-accounting was marked as resolved.

### What codes duello actually accomplished

The duel of honor bounded the lethal violence of an armed
aristocratic and officer class during a period of ~300 years
(16th-19th c.) across European society. Its accomplishments:

- **Elimination of blood-feud as the ordinary resolution path
  for aristocratic disputes**. Before the duel of consolidation
  of honor, aristocratic grievance often escalated through
  household-vs-household feud, ambush, or extended private war
  among families and their retainers. The duel of honor
  substituted a bounded procedure (two principals, two seconds,
  one meeting, one outcome) for the extended violence of the
  feud.
- **De-escalation by seconds in the overwhelming majority of
  cases**. Historical records (e.g., the survey by Kiernan)
  indicate that something like 80-90% of formal challenges were
  resolved without a meeting, through negotiation between the
  seconds. The institution succeeded primarily as a
  *de-escalation framework*, with the meeting as the backstop.
- **Procedural dignity in even the most personal disputes**.
  Honor disputes that would otherwise have degenerated into
  open insult or public brawling were channeled into formal
  exchange, allowing the offended party to be publicly seen as
  having received procedural dignity regardless of the substantive
  outcome.

### Decay in detail

Multiple pressures produced the decay of the institution
through the 19th-20th centuries:

**Criminalization.** National states progressively criminalized
dueling. Britain banned Army officer duels in 1844 (Articles of
War revision); Prussia tightened enforcement through the late
19th c.; American states banned dueling in successive waves
through the 19th c. (most famously Illinois in 1819, after the
Hamilton-Burr 1804 notoriety of the duel); France retained
legal tolerance longer than most but reduced social acceptance
made practice increasingly private. Criminalization was not by
itself sufficient (the Richelieu and Louis XIV edicts had been
ignored for a century), but combined with the other decay
vectors, it progressively withdrew institutional support for the
practice.

**Public ridicule via mass journalism.** This was arguably the
most decisive vector. As the periodical press developed in the
19th century and mass literacy spread, dueling shifted from
honorable to ridiculous in mass culture. Thackeray and Dickens
satirized the institution; *Punch* caricatured it regularly;
Dostoevsky's *Notes from Underground* treated it as pathetic. By
the end of the 19th century, the duel was primarily a literary
set-piece for bourgeois presentation in the novels of
aristocratic absurdity. The institution depended on its public
meaning as honor-restoring. Once that meaning curdled into
mockery, the institution could not be maintained by its
participants. A gentleman willing to be ridiculed for a duel was
not recognizably performing the function of the institution.

**Democratization.** The honor code did not generalize beyond
the aristocratic and officer class. As political power broadened
(extension of suffrage, rise of bourgeois and then working-
class political participation) and the aristocracy lost its
institutional position, the duel had no broader cultural
carrier. A bourgeois burgher could not challenge an aristocrat
without category confusion. A working-class man had no standing
in the institution at all. The specific sociology of the
institution (aristocratic-officer class) meant that as the
position of that class declined, the institution declined with
it.

**Lethality of firearms.** Firearms technology in the 19th
century made duels substantially more lethal than they had been.
Smoothbore pistols at 30 paces produced highly unreliable
outcomes. Rifled percussion-cap pistols produced more reliable
and more lethal outcomes. The cost-benefit calculation shifted.
A meeting that had been likely a flesh wound or a face-saving
exchange became likely fatal. The institution had been bearable
as a low-probability-fatal ritual. It was not bearable as a
high-probability-fatal ritual, once the participants could
calculate the actual risk.

**Industrial-scale war.** The First World War made the honor
code incompatible with the scale of industrial mass violence.
Surviving duelists came home from the Somme and Verdun with
no remaining use for formal personal-honor challenge. The scale
of violence they had just lived through was several orders of
magnitude larger than any personal-honor framework could bound.
The institution had effectively ceased by the mid-20th century.
The German student *Mensur* in its ritualized non-lethal form
is the last surviving trace of the tradition.

### Lesson for kith-covenant

Three lessons.

First, **the seconds-as-de-escalators role is the direct
precedent for mediator architecture of kith-covenant **. Seconds
were not mere witnesses. They were active de-escalators whose
procedural duty was to resolve the dispute without a meeting
wherever possible. The mediator pool of Kith-Covenant (described
in [Liminal institutions](liminal_institutions.md)) should be
designed as active mediators on the seconds model, not passive
witnesses on the later arbitrator model.

Second, **the escalation ladder with face-saving exits at every
rung is the structural pattern**. The specific rungs of the duel
(challenge, negotiation between seconds, apology options,
meeting-conditions negotiation, the meeting itself, deloping, and
acknowledged outcome) each provided an exit at which honor could
be satisfied short of escalation to the next rung. The
escalation protocols of Kith-Covenant
([Bounded conflict](bounded_conflict.md#escalation-ladders))
should be designed with explicit exits at every rung, not merely
at the top and bottom.

Third, **a bounded-conflict regime that depends on a specific
sociological carrier class is vulnerable to that class losing
position**. The decline of the duel tracked the aristocratic
decline of the class. Kith-Covenant cannot stake itself on any
single sociological carrier whose cultural position is itself
contingent. The compact must be designed to work across the
sociological range of its members rather than with a specific
class as its operative carrier.

## Cross-case patterns within Christendom

Six patterns recur across the Christendom cases, beyond those
already summarized in
[Historical cases](historical_cases.md#patterns-across-the-cases).

**First, each case institutionalizes a substrate-level moral
commitment**. Westphalia institutionalizes *justus hostis* at
system scale. The Peace of God institutionalizes the protected
categories of non-combatants. The Hanse institutionalizes commercial
good faith and collective sanction. Lex mercatoria institutionalizes
cross-jurisdictional commercial trust. Codes duello institutionalize
bounded personal-honor conflict. In each case, an underlying
Christian moral commitment (peer recognition, protection of the
innocent, good faith in dealing, trust among strangers, bounded
conflict) is given institutional form that makes the commitment
operative across polities.

**Second, each case has a named path to restitution and
return**. They are the amnesty clauses of Westphalia; the penances
and formal reconciliation of the Peace of God; *Verhansung* with
defined re-admission; the restitution requirements of the *lex
mercatoria*; and the honor-satisfied formula of the duel. Each is a
structural provision for restoring a violating party to standing
once the violation is addressed, through amnesty, penance, and
restitution. The confession-restitution-return architecture is
not an abstraction imposed on the record. It is visible in every
one of these cases.

**Third, each case recognizes the adversary as a continuing peer
(within scope)**. Westphalia recognizes enemy sovereigns as peers
throughout and after the war. The Peace of God treats violators as
sinners calling for reconciliation, not as beings outside the moral
community. The Hanse treats *Verhansed* cities as candidates for
re-admission, not as permanently excluded rivals. Lex mercatoria
treats contract-breakers as merchants whose reputation is damaged
but who remain merchants. The duel treats opponents as
honor-bearing peers before, during, and after the meeting. The
*justus hostis* move operates in specifically Christian form
across all five cases.

**Fourth, each case has a load-bearing institution external to
but recognized by the sovereign**. Westphalia: external
guarantor powers, the Imperial structure of the Diet that the
Emperor could not dissolve, and the permanent diplomatic corps.
Peace of God: the episcopate and the monastic networks. Hanse:
the Hansetag and the *Kontore*. Lex mercatoria: the merchant
forums and the consular-court traditions. Codes duello: the
seconds and the procedural community of the honor code. The
substrate-holder- independent-of-sovereign pattern is pervasive.

**Fifth, each decay of the case follows a recognizable
pattern**. State-absorption (Westphalia and Hanse),
externalization (Peace of God into Crusade),
dissolution-by-irrelevance (codes duello after 1914), or
formal-continuation-under-state-control (lex mercatoria
incorporated into national commercial law). No Christendom case
decayed primarily by substrate failure. Each decayed by external
institutional pressure on a still-functional internal mechanism.
This is structurally distinct from the Buddhist nationalism
failure mode ([The Buddhist sangha](buddhist_sangha.md#decay)),
where a functional institution was subverted by a specific
anti-spiral- feature betrayal internal to the tradition. The
Christendom failure modes are mostly external pressure. The
Buddhist failure modes include internal subversion.

**Sixth, each case left doctrinal debris that proved durable
across the decay**. The sovereignty principles of Westphalia
survive in international law. The protected categories of the
Peace of God survive in the Geneva Conventions; Hanse commercial
law survives in modern *lex mercatoria* arbitration. Medieval
*lex mercatoria* survives in common-law commercial doctrine. The
seconds-as-mediators pattern survives in modern
dispute-resolution practice. The institutions died. The
doctrinal achievements outlasted them. This is important for
kith-covenant. Even a successful compact that eventually decays
can leave durable contributions to successor institutions.

## The five anti-spiral features in Christendom form

The operative formulation from
[Justus hostis](justus_hostis.md#the-anti-spiral-features-why-anchor-above-self-is-necessary-but-not-sufficient)
identifies five features whose combination blocks the
purity-spiral failure mode. While the features are what the
compact credentials, the specifically Christian form each
Christendom case gave them deserves naming, because the
intra-Christian variation across the cases is itself
instructive.

### (1) Universal fallen-actor anthropology

**Pervasive across all five cases.** Western Christian
anthropology from Augustine forward is built on original sin
applied universally (including to the baptized, including to the
saints, including to the ecclesiastical hierarchy). Even at the
end of a confessional war, Westphalia could treat enemy
sovereigns as peers, since all sovereigns are fallen and no one
has standing to claim the cause of the enemy is uniquely wicked;
why the Peace of God could treat violators as sinners rather
than as monsters (sinners are inside the moral community; the
mechanism is reconciliation, not exclusion); why lex mercatoria
could assume that all merchants need the institutional check of
reputation (because all are tempted to cheat); why the procedure
of the duel assumed honor-maintenance was a general concern
rather than a concern only of specific individuals. The
Christian anthropology is distinctive in its rigor on this
point: the treatment by Augustine in *De civitate Dei*, Thomas
the discussion by Aquinas in *Summa Theologiae*, and Calvin's
and Luther's Reformation treatments all maintain the
universal-fallen-actor move against various attempts to soften
it. Niebuhr's *Nature and Destiny of Man* (1941) is the modern
consolidation. This feature is the most continuous across the
Christendom tradition, and it is what makes *justus hostis*
theologically coherent in Christian terms.

### (2) Grace over merit

**Pervasive but with internal Christian variation.** The Pauline
insistence that grace is gratuitous and cannot be earned (Romans
3-4; Ephesians 2:8-9) is the doctrinal form; the elaboration by
Augustine against Pelagius is the classical exposition. The
*sola gratia* of the Reformation is the Protestant
consolidation. The Council of Trent's decree on justification
(1547) is the Catholic specification. The feature is Christian
from the apostolic period forward. Its institutional work in the
Christendom cases:

- the amnesty clauses of Westphalia are doctrinally
  grace-over-merit. Although the violating parties do not
  deserve restoration, they are restored to standing, because
  the settlement grants it.
- The Peace of God's reconciliation ceremonies are grace-over-
  merit. The violator is restored to sacramental standing
  through confession and penance, not through meritorious works
  that would offset the violation.
- the re-admission of the Hanse procedures, the restitution
  paths of the *lex mercatoria*, and the honor-satisfied formula
  of the duel all reflect the same pattern. Standing restored
  through procedural recognition rather than through
  merit-accumulation.

### (3) Scapegoat-revelation

**Distinctively Christian, most fully developed.** This is the
feature where the form of Christianity is structurally
strongest. Girard's argument (that the Cross reveals the
scapegoat mechanism *as* scapegoating by presenting the innocent
victim as innocent from God's point of view) is the formal
statement. The structural effect is that every subsequent
scapegoating in Christian-influenced cultures is visible *as*
scapegoating, to an audience whose central religious narrative
is the story of a wrongly-sacrificed innocent. This visibility
is the protective mechanism of the feature.

The Christendom cases show the feature at work:

- **the resistance of Westphalia to treating enemy sovereigns
  as criminally guilty** even after the Thirty Years' War's
  atrocities reflects the scapegoat-revelation feature. The war
  was not resolved by prosecuting the losing side. It was
  resolved by settlement among continuing peers.
- **The Peace of God's insistence that violators remain
  reconcilable** rather than being definitively expelled
  reflects the refusal of the feature to permit the scapegoat
  mechanism to terminate in the destruction of the violator.
- **the failure of the Reformation to produce a generalized
  inquisitorial purge** on either side (despite individual
  inquisitorial episodes) and the eventual Westphalian
  stabilization on *cujus regio, eius religio* reflect the
  continuing operation of the feature across the confessional
  divide.
- **The achievement of the abolitionist movement** in the
  18th-19th centuries, abolishing slavery in the British Empire
  (1833), the French Empire (1848), the United States (1865),
  and elsewhere, operated on the scapegoat-revelation reading of
  the slavery institution. Slaves as innocent victims whose
  sacrificial status (in the economic-theological sense) was
  revealed as illegitimate. William Wilberforce, the Clapham
  Sect, and their American abolitionist counterparts drew
  explicitly on the Christian-scriptural revelation of the
  innocent-victim pattern.

The failures of the feature are also visible: the Crusades, the
Wars of Religion, the Albigensian Crusade, the colonial-frontier
treatment of non-Europeans. These are Christian betrayals of the
scriptural mandate, rather than absence of the feature. The
pattern is that failure occurs when the feature is suspended for
specific out-groups (the infidel, the heretic, the non-European)
while remaining operative within the in-group. This is exactly
the failure mode the feature is designed to prevent, and the
betrayal is recognizable against the standard of the feature
itself.

### (4) Enemy-love as operational norm

**Christian scripturally central, institutionally mixed.** The
Sermon on the Mount (Matthew 5:43-48) makes enemy-love
scripturally central. The parable of the Good Samaritan (Luke
10:25-37) makes it operationally specific. This is the strongest
Christian distinctive on the feature list: "love your enemies,
bless them that curse you, do good to them that hate you, and
pray for them which despitefully use you, and persecute you."

In the Christendom cases:

- **the treatment at Westphalia of enemy sovereigns as
  continuing peers** is the institutionalization of the feature
  at state scale.
- **The Peace of God's protection of all classes of
  non-combatants including non-Christians (Jews in several
  synods)** reflects the operation of the feature al scope
  extending beyond the Christian in-group.
- **the honor-restoration formula of the duel** reflects the
  operation of the feature at the individual-honor scale. The
  opponent is a peer whose honor deserves recognition.
- **The Geneva Convention tradition** (from Henri Dunant's
  *A Memory of Solferino* 1862 through the 1864 convention and
  its successors) explicitly institutionalized the feature at
  the frontier of lawful warfare. Medical assistance is owed to
  the wounded enemy on the same basis as to the wounded friend.

The failure modes of the feature (the Crusades against the
non-Christian, the colonial frontier, the intra-confessional
persecutions) are, as above, betrayals of the scriptural mandate
of the feature rather than absence of the feature. The
Samaritan-parable correction
([The noticeable hand](noticeable_hand.md#counterweights)) is the
explicit scriptural basis for the corrective to these betrayals.

### (5) Non-ultimacy of the political community

**Augustinian at the core, institutionally varied.** Augustine's
*De civitate Dei* (5th c.) supplies the deepest Christian form.
The earthly city (civitas terrena) is not the ultimate. The city
of God is. Political community is at best penultimate, and its
attempt to claim ultimate standing is idolatry.

This operation of the feature across the Christendom cases:

- **the explicit secularization at Westphalia of sovereign
  authority** reflects the feature at state scale. Sovereigns
  govern the earthly city, they do not constitute the ultimate,
  and the authority of the sovereign is bounded by what the
  earthly business of the city actually is. The separation of
  church and state tradition derives from this.
- **The Peace of God's authority deriving from the episcopate, not
  from royal power** reflects the feature explicitly. The sacred
  authority exceeds and outranks the political authority.
- **the structural independence of the Hanse from any single
  sovereign** is the feature operating at the
  commercial-community scale.
- **The operation of the *lex mercatoria* outside the direct
  reach of state law** reflects the same operation at the
  merchant-community scale.
- **Codes operation of the duello inside a class-specific honor
  order that was explicitly distinct from the royal order** is
  the same at the individual-honor scale.

The failure modes for this feature in Christendom history are
significant: Divine-right monarchy (claim that the sovereign
*is* the representative of the ultimate in such a way that
resistance is sin); state-churches that collapsed church into
state; and modern civil-religious nationalism that treats the
nation as quasi-divine. These are all Christian-ish betrayals of the
feature. Each was substantially resisted by some strand of the
tradition (the two-swords doctrine against caesaropapism; the
Huguenot and other Reformed traditions against divine-right
claims; the Confessing Church against Nazi civil religion), but
the betrayals themselves were real and consequential.

### Overall assessment

Christendom supplies the five features in their most fully
developed single combination across the traditions surveyed in
this research set. Features (1), (3), and (4) are Christian
distinctives in specific forms (universal fallen-actor with
rigor. Scapegoat-revelation through the Cross. Enemy-love as
positive scriptural commandment). Features (2) and (5) have
strong analogs in other traditions (Buddhist grace and
renunciation; Jewish covenantal grace and two-ages doctrine;
Sufi *tawakkul*) but the form of Christianity is fully
developed and institutionally extensive. The Christendom
institutional density of the cases (five distinct, substantial,
cross-polity cooperation regimes of multiple centuries of
duration each) is the empirical expression of these features
operating together across a civilizational matrix.

The failure modes visible across the cases are betrayals of the
features by their nominal carriers, not absences of the
features. This is operationally important for kith-covenant. The
ongoing anti-spiral-features check
([Recognition protocols](recognition_protocols.md#two-layers-intra-community-authorization-and-inter-community-grammar))
must examine current operation of the features, not merely their
in-principle supply by the substrate. A Christian community that
has betrayed the features is a failed-features community for
compact purposes, regardless of its nominal Christianity.

## Verdict on the Christian-particularist reading

The detailed Christendom treatment here confirms three propositions
that the earlier compact treatment in
[Historical cases](historical_cases.md) stated and that
[The noticeable hand](noticeable_hand.md) developed:

**First, Christendom produced the greatest density of voluntary
cross-polity cooperation frameworks among separate sovereigns in
the historical record**. Westphalia is the most developed single
instance of exactly the institutional form kith-covenant is
trying to instantiate (formal-equal sovereigns cooperating
across boundaries without a central authority), and it ran for
roughly 270 years in its mature form. The four other cases
(Peace of God, Hanse, lex mercatoria, codes duello) add further
centuries of parallel and complementary cooperation at different
scales. Against the Buddhist longer duration of the sangha but
different-kind (trans-polity community) achievement
([The Buddhist sangha](buddhist_sangha.md)), the claim of
Christendom is specifically about *cross-polity cooperation
frameworks among separate sovereigns*, which is what
kith-covenant needs.

**Second, the anti-spiral features in their Christian form are the
most fully developed single combination available in the
cross-traditional survey**. Features (1), (3), and (4) have
distinctively Christian forms whose structural robustness is real.
Features (2) and (5) have strong Christian forms alongside strong
non-Christian analogs. The combination of all five in developed
form is what the Christendom cases instantiate.

**Third, the failure modes are betrayals rather than absences**.
They are intra-Christian violence, colonial selectivity, and
Crusading externalization. Each is a failure of Christian actors to apply
the anti-spiral features their substrate supplies, not an
absence of the features in the substrate. This is the same form
of failure visible in the Buddhist nationalism cases, and it
confirms that the features describe what the substrate *makes
available*, not what the substrate *guarantees*. The
Kith-Covenant compact must expect occasional betrayal and must
include continuous features-checking rather than one-time
substrate-credentialing.

The net position is narrower than the strong claim. **The
strongest Christian-particularist reading consistent with the
full cross-traditional evidence is not "only Christianity can do
this," but "Christianity supplies the most complete single
combination of the features, produces the greatest density of
cross-polity cooperation frameworks among separate sovereigns,
and produces these achievements despite, and through recognition
of, recurring betrayals by its nominal carriers."**

This is the position documentation of kith-covenant can hold
without overclaiming. It is more modest than the strong
Christian-particularist reading and more specific than the
catholic / perennialist reading alone would support. The
Buddhist sangha evidence is accommodated. The achievement of the
sangha is acknowledged as real and as structurally different.
The Christendom achievement is named in the specific form it
actually takes. And the compact design follows. The anti-spiral
features rather than specific theology are the membership
criterion, and Christian communities applying for membership are
examined for continuing operation of the features exactly as
other communities are.

## Lessons for kith-covenant

The Christendom cases have been the principal historical
reference for design of kith-covenant throughout the research
corpus. The increased-detail treatment here confirms and
sharpens the earlier lessons. No lesson established in other
documents is contradicted by the more detailed examination.
Several lessons gain additional specificity:

**From Westphalia.** Formal equality among members must be
structurally protected against combined action by a subset of
members (against the Polish-partitions failure mode). The
compact must include a credible procedural defense: standing
objection rights, external guarantors invited in the text, and
compulsory consultation before armed action by any members
against any other. The text of the compact, guarantors, and
registration mechanism should be at least as operationally real
as the Westphalian instruments.

**From the Peace of God.** If the substrate has real bite,
sacred-substrate-only enforcement can work at cross-polity
scale. The sanction of the compact (exclusion from compact
commons) must operate through real commons whose loss is
operationally severe. The credibility of the compact ultimately
rests on the materiality of the commons.

**From the Hanse.** Institutional mass matters. A cross-polity
cooperation regime without standing capacity is brittle to the
rise of strong states. The compact must develop and maintain
durable institutional mass (continuous staff, continuous records,
continuous operational capacity in shared infrastructure) rather
than depending on ad-hoc mobilization.

**From the lex mercatoria.** Reputation-based enforcement works,
and its durability depends on what reputation travels on. In a
state-captured-infrastructure world, the compact must build
reputation-portable infrastructure
([Reputation bridges](reputation_bridges.md)) that does not
require state permission to function.

**From codes duello.** Active mediator de-escalation (the
seconds pattern) and escalation ladders with exits at every rung
are the structural innovations worth adopting. The mediator
architecture of the compact should be active rather than
passive. Its escalation procedures should provide face-saving
exits at every rung rather than only at the terminal points.

**From the Christendom failure patterns.** The compact must expect
betrayal of the anti-spiral features by its own members, must
examine for continuing operation of the features rather than
in-principle substrate supply, and must design specifically
against the colonial-frontier failure mode by ensuring symmetric
application of recognition machinery to all encountered parties.

## Open questions

- The Westphalian partitions of Poland demonstrate a specific
  failure mode (combined action by a subset against another
  member) that the *jus publicum* had no institutional answer
  to. What is the strongest available institutional answer?
  External guarantors? Standing third-party intervention?
  Mandatory dispute resolution before action? Procedural
  invalidation of territorial transfers absent consent? And how
  should the text encode of the compact it?
- The Peace of God's Crusading redirection is a sobering case. A
  successful peace regime supplied the moral grammar for its
  externalization into sanctioned violence against non-members.
  What structural features prevent this redirection? The
  Samaritan-parable-style correction of neighbor-status operating
  in every encounter is the substrate-level answer. What is the
  compact-level answer that operationalizes it?
- The absorption of the *lex mercatoria* into national law is a
  distinctive decay mode. Substantive content survives,
  institutional autonomy does not. What conditions would make
  the institutions of the compact sufficiently resistant to
  absorption? Can the compact design for its own eventual
  absorption as a success case (substantive content becoming
  default infrastructure of successor institutions), as distinct
  from absorption as a failure case (autonomous institutions
  dissolving into state control)?
- the dependence of the duel on a specific sociological carrier
  class (aristocratic-officer) is a cautionary tale for
  membership design of kith-covenant. To what extent does
  kith-covenant depend on a specific carrier class (space-going
  professionals. Aerospace engineers. Astronauts), and what
  would the durability of the compact look like if that carrier
  class were displaced?
- The 1914 collapse of the *jus publicum* was partly a collapse
  of the substrate carrier class (European upper-class shared
  Christian civilization). Modern kith-covenant must work across
  a broader substrate base than that carrier class provided. Is
  the anti-spiral-features formulation sufficient to sustain
  cross-substrate cooperation comparable to the pre-1914
  *jus publicum*, or does cross-substrate cooperation require
  additional structural support?
- The Westphalian *jus in bello / jus ad bellum* distinction is
  under continuing pressure from the post-1945 humanitarian-
  intervention and war-crimes-prosecution frameworks. How does
  anathema procedure of kith-covenant relate to these
  frameworks, and can the deliberate narrowness of the procedure
  be sustained in an operational environment shaped by the
  broader late-modern slide against the *jus in bello*
  distinction?

# Exit and Succession: Leaving a Compact, Splitting, Dissolving, Reconstituting

## Thesis

Recognition has a counterpart in withdrawal. The five-movement
recognition ceremony ([recognition_protocols.md](recognition_protocols.md))
brings a community into the compact through a weighty, public,
witnessed commitment. **The same procedural weight that applies to
admission must apply to formal exit**, not to trap members, but
because a compact that can be exited trivially is a compact in which
commitment means nothing, while a compact that cannot be exited at
all is a prison. The exit procedure must be difficult enough to signal
seriousness and easy enough to prevent entrapment.

The historical record offers four distinct exit modes, each with a
different structural logic, different obligations, and different
reconstitution pathways:

1. **Voluntary withdrawal.** A community chooses to leave the compact
   in good standing. The Hanseatic cities that chose not to attend
   the later Hansetage exercised this option tacitly. Kith-covenant
   requires it to be explicit.
2. **Expulsion.** The compact removes a community through the *hostis
   humani generis* designation procedure's terminal consequence. This
   is involuntary exit initiated by the compact.
3. **Fracture under external pressure.** An external force (war,
   catastrophe, or intervention from outside) splits the compact
   members into incompatible camps. The division of the Iroquois
   Confederacy during the American Revolution is the defining
   case.
4. **Dissolution.** The compact itself ceases to function, either
   through deliberate decision or through gradual decay. The last
   Hansetag of the Hanse in 1669, attended by only nine cities,
   is the decay case. The dissolution of the monasteries under
   Henry VIII is the forced-dissolution case.

The deepest question is the one the Iroquois case raises: **can a
fractured compact be reassembled?** The answer appears to be.
Sometimes, partially, painfully, and only if the substrate survives
the fracture.

## Voluntary withdrawal

### The procedure

Voluntary withdrawal is the mirror of the recognition ceremony. A
formal, public, witnessed act. The withdrawing community:

1. **Gives formal notice** to the compact through its liminal
   institutions ([liminal_institutions.md](liminal_institutions.md)).
   The notice specifies the effective date and the reasons for
   withdrawal. The reasons need not be approved by the compact. The
   right to withdraw is unconditional. But the reasons are part of
   the record and serve the compact institutional memory.
2. **Enters a transition period** during which all compact obligations
   are honored. The transition period serves the same function as a
   notice period in a contract. It allows the compact to adjust, it
   allows the withdrawing community to settle its obligations, and
   it prevents strategic withdrawal (exiting just before an obligation
   comes due). The proposed duration is one full cycle of the deliberative
   calendar, typically one year.
3. **Settles its commons stake.** The share a withdrawing
   community holds in the compact common resources
   ([commons_federation.md](commons_federation.md)) must be
   either (a) transferred to the remaining compact, (b) replaced
   by the remaining compact, or (c) maintained by mutual
   agreement. Physical infrastructure that the withdrawing
   community contributed to the compact liminal institutions
   (relays, docks, and rescue stations) cannot simply be removed
   without harming the remaining members. The settlement
   procedure must specify how shared infrastructure is handled.
4. **Conducts a withdrawal ceremony.** Just as the recognition ceremony
   is a public, witnessed, spoken commitment to enter the compact, the
   withdrawal ceremony is a public, witnessed, spoken statement of
   departure. The ceremony acknowledges what the compact meant, what
   the withdrawing community received, and the residual obligations
   that persist after departure (see §"Residual obligations" below).
   Witnesses from the mediator pool attend and sign the cryptographic
   record.

### What the withdrawing community loses

Departure costs must be real but not punitive. The Hanseatic
model is instructive. A city that left the Hanse lost all
trading privileges: preferential tariffs, Kontor access, and
collective-security protection. The loss was substantial enough
that most cities stayed, but not so catastrophic that departure
was unthinkable.

The kith-covenant analogue. A withdrawing community loses:

- **Compact membership.** It is no longer a party to compact
  deliberations, mediation, or mutual commitments.
- **Liminal-institution access.** It no longer holds standing at
  the mediator pool, the shared docks, or the compact
  ceremonies. Pool, docks, and ceremonies alike are closed to
  it.
- **Reputation bridge benefits.** Its compact-facing record remains
  in the compact archive (see
  [reputation_bridges.md](reputation_bridges.md) §"Reputation upon
  exit"), but it no longer accrues reputation within the compact.
- **Commons rights.** It loses its appropriation rights in the
  compact shared commons (communication relays, navigational data,
  knowledge archive) unless mutual agreement provides otherwise.

### What the withdrawing community retains

- **Its compact-facing record.** This is a factual record, not a
  reward. A community that exits in good standing retains the record
  as a positive signal to future compacts.
- **The common virtues.** A community that internalized the common
  virtues during its membership does not cease to hold them upon
  departure. The virtues are dispositions, not contractual obligations.
  They survive the dissolution of the contractual relationship.
- **Residual obligations** (see §"Residual obligations" below).

## Expulsion

Expulsion is the terminal consequence of the anathema designation
procedure
([bounded_conflict.md](bounded_conflict.md) §"Handling the anathema").
A community designated as anathema after the full compact deliberation
(meeting all four conditions, with multiple communities concurring)
is expelled from the compact.

### How expulsion differs from voluntary withdrawal

The differences are procedural and moral, not merely administrative:

- **Involuntary.** The community does not choose to leave; the
  compact removes it. The community may contest the designation
  through the compact mediation mechanisms, but once the
  designation is confirmed, the expulsion is mandatory.
- **No transition period.** The designation itself implies that
  continued presence in the compact is actively harmful. The
  interim-injunction mechanism
  ([liminal_institutions.md](liminal_institutions.md)) may
  already be in effect. The transition from designation to
  expulsion should be as short as the settlement of material
  obligations permits.
- **Material stake.** The share held by the expelled community
  in the compact commons is forfeit. This is not confiscation
  for punishment. It is the natural consequence of designation.
  A community designated as anathema has been judged to have
  violated the common virtues so severely that its continued
  participation in the commons is itself harmful. The commons
  must be protected.
- **Post-expulsion status.** The expelled community becomes a
  non-member encountered. Not a member, not a legitimate adversary
  (*justus hostis*), and not nonexistent. The common virtues apply
  within any encounter with the expelled community (the encounter-based
  neighbor ethic), but the compact protocols do not. The expelled
  community is, in effect, returned to the state it was in before it
  joined the compact.

### Re-entry after expulsion

The anathema designation includes a re-entry procedure (per
[bounded_conflict.md](bounded_conflict.md)). The designated
community may petition for re-admission after demonstrating sustained
change. The re-entry procedure is structurally identical to the
original recognition ceremony (the five-movement recognition
statement, with witnesses assessing sincerity) with the additional
burden that the petitioning community must account for the conduct
that led to its designation.

The re-entry burden should be heavy but not impossible. A community
that has genuinely reformed (whose internal governance has changed,
whose leadership has been replaced, whose population has repudiated
the conduct that led to designation) should not be permanently
excluded. But the sincerity assessment must be *more* demanding than
for first admission, because the community has demonstrated a
capacity for the conduct that the compact exists to prevent.

## Compact splitting

### The problem

Voluntary withdrawal and expulsion both assume that the compact
continues to function after the departure. Splitting is different.
Two or more factions within the compact disagree on foundational
matters (specifically, on the content or interpretation of the
five-movement recognition statement) and the disagreement is so
deep that continued cooperation under the existing framework is
impossible.

This is not a policy dispute (which is resolved by the escalation
ladder) or a virtue violation (which is resolved by the designation
procedure). It is a **substrate-level disagreement.** A
disagreement about what the recognition statement means, what the
sacred substrate requires, or what the common virtues entail. Policy
disputes presuppose a shared foundation. Splitting occurs when the
foundation itself is contested.

### The Protestant Reformation as the defining case

The most consequential compact-splitting event in Western history
is the Protestant Reformation. The Christendom substrate, which
had underwritten European cooperation for a millennium, fractured
when communities disagreed about the content of the substrate
itself. Was the Pope the legitimate vicar of the substrate? Was
the Mass a sacrifice? Was salvation by faith alone? These were
not policy questions. They were questions about the nature of the
sacred substrate.

The fracture was violent, prolonged (the Wars of Religion lasted over
a century), and never fully healed. But it produced, eventually, a
*new* cooperation regime (the Westphalian system) that was designed
specifically to function *despite* the substrate disagreement. The
Westphalian innovation was to depoliticize the substrate question:
*cuius regio, eius religio*. Each sovereign decides the substrate
question within their own territory, and the inter-sovereign
cooperation framework is agnostic on the question.

### The futility lesson and its limits

The first lesson of the Reformation is about **futility.**
Communities slaughtered each other for over a century over
questions about the nature of God that cannot be empirically
proven. Whether the bread *is* Christ's body or *represents* it,
whether grace operates through sacraments or through faith alone,
whether ecclesiastical authority descends through apostolic
succession or through the congregation. These are assertions
about the unknowable, and the Wars of Religion demonstrated that
killing over them is not merely wicked but *pointless*, because
the killing cannot settle the question.

Kith-covenant must internalize this lesson. Where a theological
disagreement concerns the *direct nature of God* (questions that
lie beyond empirical or structural verification) the compact has
no business adjudicating, and splitting over such questions is
futile in the same way the Wars of Religion were futile. The
compact recognition ceremony deliberately does not specify the
content of the substrate beyond requiring that it point to a
single ultimate (the transcendent-moral-authority structure). The
*character* of that ultimate is the business of the member
community.

**But this lesson has 2nd- and 3rd-order limits that must be handled
with great care.**

Not all assertions about the divine nature are
unknowable-and-irrelevant to the compact. Some cross a boundary
(often indirectly, even circuitously) from the purely theological
into the structurally consequential. Where they do, the compact
not only *may* but *must* make an orthodox determination, because
the downstream effects touch how the compact functions.

Consider the progression:

- **First order (unknowable, irrelevant to the compact):** "God
  is three persons in one substance" vs. "God is absolutely one."
  This is a question about the inner divine nature. The compact
  cannot adjudicate it, should not try, and should not split over
  it.

- **Second order (indirectly relevant, knowable by effects).** A
  theology of the divine nature produces a theology of *human*
  nature that denies fallenness, holds that humans are naturally
  good and require only the right social arrangements. This
  theological claim, while nominally about God (or about
  creation), has a direct structural consequence. It undermines
  the fallen-actor anthropology that the compact escalation
  ladder, virtue framework, and anathema procedure are all
  designed around. A community holding this theology will
  consistently resist the mechanisms the compact depends on, not
  out of bad faith but out of genuine theological conviction that
  the mechanisms are unnecessary. The compact *can* evaluate this
  (not by proving the theology wrong but by observing its
  structural effects) and *must* make a determination, because
  the downstream consequences are not speculative but
  operational.

- **Third order (circuitously relevant, requiring
  discernment).** A theology may produce a cosmology in which
  the sacred substrate does not stand *above* the authority of
  the community but is *identical* with it. The community *is*
  the voice of God, and therefore its pronouncements carry
  substrate authority. This is the civil-religion failure mode
  warned against in [justus_hostis.md](justus_hostis.md)
  §"Substrate affords emergence." The theological claim is
  nominally about the divine relationship to human institutions,
  which sounds like first-order theology, but its effect is to
  collapse the distinction between the substrate and the
  compact, which destroys the check that the substrate exercises
  on compact ambition. The path from the theological claim to
  the structural consequence is circuitous, but the consequence
  is real and detectable.

The design principle: **the compact does not adjudicate theology
but does adjudicate structural effects.** When the theological
assertions of a community (however sincerely held) produce
downstream effects that undermine the compact foundational
architecture (the fallen-actor anthropology, the
transcendent-moral-authority substrate, the distinction between
substrate and compact, the common-virtue framework), the compact
must be willing to make an orthodox determination: *this
theological position, whatever its merits as theology, produces
effects incompatible with membership*. This is not the compact
judging the truth of the theology. It is the compact observing
that the structural consequences are incompatible with the
compact design.

The Old Testament prophetic tradition provides the precedent and
the warning. The prophets were, in many cases, making exactly
these second- and third-order orthodox determinations. Amos did
not dispute the theology Israel held. He said that Israelite
worship (first order) had produced exploitation of the poor
(second order) which constituted covenant-repudiation (third
order). The communities that heard this determination frequently
responded by killing the prophet (see
[jester_and_dissent.md](jester_and_dissent.md) §"The Old
Testament prophetic tradition"). The killing was itself
diagnostic. A community secure in its fidelity does not need to
silence the voice pointing out its infidelity. The pattern
teaches two things: (1) orthodox determination at the second and
third order is genuinely dangerous for the person making it, and
the compact must design protections (knowing they will sometimes
fail); (2) a violent rejection of the determination by that
community is often the strongest evidence that the determination
was correct.

The tragedy of the Reformation was that it fought over
first-order questions (which are genuinely unknowable and should
have been left to each community) while failing to develop the
institutional capacity to adjudicate second- and third-order
consequences (which are knowable by their fruits and must be
adjudicated). Kith-covenant must do the opposite. Tolerate
maximal theological diversity at the first order while
maintaining the discernment to detect and respond to structural
threats at the second and third orders. The discernment function
of the mediator pool
([liminal_institutions.md](liminal_institutions.md)) is the
institutional mechanism. The transcendent-moral-authority
substrate is the reference point against which structural effects
can be measured.

### Lessons for kith-covenant

1. **Substrate-level disagreements cannot be resolved by the
   escalation ladder.** The escalation ladder presupposes the
   substrate. It cannot adjudicate the substrate.
2. **Splitting is not necessarily failure.** The Reformation produced
   new cooperation frameworks that, in some respects, were more
   robust than the pre-Reformation Christendom framework (because
   they were designed for diversity rather than assuming unity).
3. **The violence of the split is proportional to the ambiguity of the
   succession question.** If the compact founding documents specify
   a splitting procedure, the split can be orderly. If they do not,
   both sides claim to be the legitimate continuation, and the dispute
   becomes existential.
4. **Not all theological disagreements are equal.** First-order
   questions about the divine nature (unknowable, irrelevant to
   the compact) must be tolerated. Second- and third-order
   consequences that undermine the compact structural foundations
   (knowable by their fruits, operationally relevant) must be
   adjudicated. The boundary between the two requires
   discernment, not rules.
5. **Orthodox determination is sometimes necessary.** Where the
   downstream effects of a theological position threaten the
   compact architecture, the compact must be willing to say so,
   clearly, publicly, and with the full procedural weight of the
   deliberative process. The fear of appearing intolerant must
   not prevent the compact from protecting its structural
   foundations.

### Splitting procedure

The kith-covenant founding documents should include an explicit splitting
procedure, precisely to prevent the Reformation scenario of ambiguous
succession. Proposed:

1. **Declaration of fundamental disagreement.** One or more communities
   formally declare that a specific element of the recognition statement
   or common-virtue framework is understood differently by the
   declaring communities than by the remainder. This is not an
   accusation. It is a *declaration of divergence*.
2. **Deliberative period.** The compact enters a formal deliberative
   period (proposal. One year) during which the disagreement is
   examined through the mediator pool. The examination serves three
   purposes: (a) to ensure the disagreement is genuine and not a
   policy dispute masquerading as a foundational one; (b) to
   determine whether the disagreement is first-order (unknowable,
   irrelevant to the compact, in which case splitting is futile
   and the declaring communities should be encouraged to tolerate
   the difference) or second/third-order (structurally consequential,
   in which case an orthodox determination may resolve the dispute
   without splitting). And (c) if the disagreement is genuinely
   irresolvable and structurally consequential, to prepare for an
   orderly split.
3. **Splitting vote.** After the deliberative period, the compact
   votes on whether the disagreement warrants splitting. A
   supermajority (two-thirds of member communities, proposed) is
   required to confirm the split. If the vote fails, the declaring
   communities may still withdraw voluntarily (§"Voluntary
   withdrawal"), but they may not claim compact succession.
4. **Succession determination.** If the vote confirms the split,
   the compact divides into two successor compacts. Neither
   successor *is* the original compact. Both are new compacts
   inheriting from the original. The original compact name,
   records, and institutional memory are preserved in the archive
   maintained by the original compact liminal institutions, which
   are dissolved and reconstituted separately by each successor.
5. **Commons partition.** Shared infrastructure is partitioned by
   negotiation, with the mediator pool (drawn from communities not
   joining either successor) adjudicating disputes. The partition
   principles from §"Shared infrastructure after split" apply.

## Compact dissolution

### Deliberate dissolution

A compact that has shrunk to the point where it can no longer
function (too few members to populate the mediator pool, too little
shared infrastructure to justify the governance overhead, too little
common ground to sustain the recognition ceremony) may choose to
dissolve.

Dissolution is a formal act, not merely a cessation of activity. The
distinction matters because formal dissolution can preserve assets
that decay would destroy:

- **The archive.** The compact records (ceremonies, mediations,
  and commons governance, together with the institutional memory
  of the liminal institutions) are preserved and entrusted to a
  custodian (a former member community, a successor institution,
  or a neutral party).
- **The template.** The compact founding documents (the
  recognition statement, the virtues, and the procedural rules)
  are preserved as a template that future communities may use to
  reconstitute a compact.
- **The reputation data.** The compact-facing records of all
  former member communities are preserved as part of the
  archive, dated, attributed, and available to future compacts
  that may be considering admission of those communities.

The Hanseatic failure is the cautionary case. The Hanse did not
formally dissolve. It simply stopped functioning. The last
Hansetag in 1669 was attended by nine cities (out of a peak of
nearly 200). No formal dissolution act was performed. No archive
was systematically preserved. No template was maintained.
Hanseatic institutional knowledge was scattered and largely lost.
Kith-covenant should include a formal dissolution procedure in
its founding documents, precisely so that dissolution, if it
comes, is orderly.

### Dissolution by decay

When a compact decays without formally dissolving, the consequences
are worse than deliberate dissolution in every respect. The archive
is not preserved (or is preserved haphazardly), the template is not
maintained, the reputation data is not secured, and the institutional
memory of the liminal institutions is lost. Communities drift away.
The mediator pool empties. The shared commons degrade. The recognition
ceremony is no longer performed.

The tragedy of dissolution by decay is that it is invisible until it
is irreversible. The compact does not die. It *hollows out*. The forms
persist (the ceremonies are performed, the deliberative calendar is
maintained) but the substance is gone. Hollow compacts are more
dangerous than dissolved ones because they create a false sense of
security. Communities believe they are part of a functioning
cooperation framework when they are not.

**Detection mechanism.** The generational re-affirmation cadence from
[recognition_protocols.md](recognition_protocols.md) is a
hollowing detector. If re-affirmation becomes perfunctory (if
communities recite without conviction, if witnesses stop assessing
sincerity, if the psychological-cost mechanism no longer engages)
then the compact is hollowing. The mediator pool and the jester
function ([jester_and_dissent.md](jester_and_dissent.md)) are the
institutions responsible for detecting and naming this hollowing.

## Succession. The generational problem

The most insidious exit is the one that happens without anyone
leaving. The founding generation dies and the succeeding generation
does not understand what they inherited.

### The transmission problem

The re-affirmation cadence of the recognition ceremony (25 to 30 years) is
designed to force each generation to re-commit. But re-commitment
requires *understanding*, not merely reciting the words but
grasping the meaning behind them, the historical experience that
produced them, the anthropological assumptions that ground them.
A generation that recites without understanding will, over time,
modify the recitation to match their diminished understanding, and
the compact will drift.

This is the normal mode of institutional death. Institutions do not
usually die by catastrophe. They die by **drift.** Each generation
understanding a little less than the one before, each modification
seeming reasonable in isolation, until the accumulated drift has
produced an institution unrecognizable to its founders.

### Historical models of generational transmission

**The Haudenosaunee model.** New sachems were selected by clan mothers
and trained by experienced sachems in the Great Law. The training was
not merely procedural. It was *formative.* The new sachem was shaped
by the tradition, not merely informed about it. The Great Law was
recited at length at the installation of every new sachem, and the
recitation was itself a teaching moment.

**The catechetical model.** Christian churches transmitted their
foundational commitments through catechesis. Structured
instruction of the next generation in the meaning of the creed,
the sacraments, and the moral law. Catechesis was not merely
cognitive. It was liturgical (the creed was recited in worship),
communal (the community witnessed each catechumen in progress),
and embodied (the sacraments enacted the commitments).

**The apprenticeship model.** Medieval guilds transmitted craft
knowledge through multi-year apprenticeships in which the
apprentice learned the techniques and the *ethos* of the craft
alike, its standards, its pride, and its sense of obligation to
fellow craftsmen and to the craft itself. Guild quality
standards were not rules imposed from outside but dispositions
cultivated from within.

**The professional-training model.** Modern secular institutions
transmit their foundational commitments through professional
education, in law, medicine, and the military. The
characteristic failure. Professional training transmits
*technical skill* without *virtue*. A lawyer who knows the law
but has no commitment to justice, a doctor who knows medicine
but has no commitment to healing, a soldier who knows tactics
but has no commitment to honor. These are the products of a
transmission system that has separated skill from disposition.

### Kith-covenant's design for generational transmission

The compact generational-transmission architecture should combine
elements from the successful models:

- **The re-affirmation ceremony** (from recognition_protocols.md)
  forces each generation to commit publicly, with witnesses who can
  assess sincerity. This is the catechetical element.
- **Mediator apprenticeship** (from
  [liminal_institutions.md](liminal_institutions.md)) trains the
  next generation of mediators through practice under experienced
  mentors. This is the apprenticeship element.
- **The compact archive** preserves the institutional memory.
  Not only the records of what happened but the reasoning behind
  the compact design decisions. This is the chronicle element.
  Future generations should be able to read *why* the compact was
  designed as it was, not merely *what* the design is.
- **The jester function**
  ([jester_and_dissent.md](jester_and_dissent.md)) provides the
  critical voice that detects drift. A generation drifting from
  what its founders intended may not notice from within. The
  jester is there to name the drift.

## Historical cases of exit and reconstitution

### Verhansung: Hanseatic expulsion

The Hanse practiced *Verhansung*, the formal expulsion of a
member city from the League, with loss of all trading
privileges. The expelled city was excluded from Hanseatic trade
routes, Kontore, and collective security. Verhansung was rare
(the threat was usually sufficient) but real: Brunswick was
expelled in 1375 for refusing to comply with a Hansetag
resolution.

**Re-entry.** Expelled cities could petition for re-admission,
and several did. The re-admission process was humbling. The
petitioning city had to acknowledge its transgression, accept the
conditions the Hansetag set, and resume its obligations. The
process worked because the costs of exclusion (loss of trade)
were concrete and ongoing, creating a sustained incentive for
reconciliation.

**Lesson.** Expulsion is effective only when the costs of exclusion
are real and ongoing. If the expelled community can thrive outside
the compact, expulsion is merely symbolic.

### The Iroquois fracture during the American Revolution

The Haudenosaunee Confederacy split during the American Revolution
when different nations allied with different sides. The Mohawk and
Seneca with the British, the Oneida and Tuscarora with the Americans,
the Onondaga and Cayuga divided internally. The Council Fire was
symbolically extinguished. The liminal institution that held the
compact together was destroyed by the external pressure.

**Reconstitution.** The fire was rekindled at Grand River
(Ontario) by the Loyalist nations and continues at Onondaga (New
York) by the remaining nations. Both claim legitimate succession.
The compact was not reconstituted in its original form. Instead,
two successor institutions emerged, each carrying part of the
original compact institutional memory.

**Lesson.** External existential conflicts that force members into
incompatible alliances destroy compacts not by eroding them from
within but by tearing them apart from without. The reconstitution
question is whether the substrate survives the fracture. In the
Iroquois case, the Great Law survived, but the institutional
unity did not.

### The Protestant Reformation as exit-and-succession

The Reformation was, structurally, a massive compact-splitting event.
Communities that had been part of the Christendom compact for
centuries withdrew (or were expelled) over foundational disagreements.
No orderly splitting procedure existed. The result was over a century
of warfare.

**Reconstitution.** Christendom was never reconstituted as a single
compact. Instead, the Westphalian system emerged as a *new* compact
designed for the post-Reformation reality. Cooperation across
substrate differences rather than cooperation within a shared
substrate. The ecumenical movement (20th century) is a partial
reconstitution, but at the level of dialogue rather than institutional
unity.

**Lesson.** When a compact splits over substrate-level disagreements,
reconstitution of the original compact is probably impossible.
What *is* possible is the formation of a new, higher-level cooperation
framework that accommodates the substrate diversity the split created.

### Brexit as modern voluntary withdrawal

The departure of the United Kingdom from the European Union (2016 to 2020)
is the most detailed modern case of voluntary withdrawal from a
cooperation regime. The withdrawal was legally authorized (Article 50
of the Treaty of Lisbon), but the process was far more complex and
painful than the authorizing text suggested.

**Relevant observations:**

- The transition period (31 January 2020 to 31 December 2020) proved
  too short for the complexity of the settlement.
- The most contentious issues were the shared-commons questions
  of citizenship, money, and the border: the rights of citizens
  (people as a commons of sorts), the financial obligations (the
  "divorce bill"), and the Northern Ireland frontier (a physical
  commons problem).
- The reputation consequences were bilateral. Both sides suffered
  reputational damage from the acrimony of the process.
- No formal "withdrawal ceremony" was performed. The departure was
  administrative, not sacral. This is precisely the gap kith-covenant
  should fill.

**Lesson.** Even with an explicit withdrawal clause, the commons-
settlement problem dominates the withdrawal process. The
kith-covenant founding documents should specify the
commons-settlement principles in advance, not leave them to
negotiation at the moment of departure.

## Shared infrastructure after split

When a compact splits, its shared infrastructure (relays, docks,
stations, the knowledge archive, and the liminal institutions
themselves) must be allocated between the successor compacts.
Three models:

### Partition

Each successor compact receives a portion of the shared
infrastructure, allocated by negotiation or by the mediator pool.
Physical infrastructure is allocated geographically (each successor
gets the facilities in its region). Non-physical infrastructure
(archives, reputation data, procedural templates) is duplicated
(each successor gets a complete copy).

**Strengths.** The separation is clean, and each successor is
self-sufficient. **Weaknesses.** Duplication is wasteful.
Partition of physical infrastructure may leave some successors
underserved. The negotiation process is itself a source of
conflict.

This is the Iroquois model: the divided fire, with each
successor maintaining its own Council Fire and its own
institutional memory.

### Joint administration

Both successor compacts continue to share the infrastructure under
joint governance. A mini-compact for infrastructure administration
is formed, with representatives from both successors.

**Strengths.** The arrangement preserves the investment in
shared infrastructure and maintains the network effects that
make the infrastructure worthwhile. **Weaknesses.** Joint
administration is fragile. The same disagreement that caused the
split will likely infect the administration of the shared
infrastructure. If the successors cannot cooperate on
foundational matters, expecting them to cooperate on
infrastructure administration is optimistic.

### Abandonment

Neither successor takes responsibility for the shared infrastructure.
It decays. This is the Hanseatic failure mode. The Kontore were not
partitioned or jointly administered. They were simply abandoned as
the Hanse weakened.

**Strengths.** None. **Weaknesses.** Irreplaceable
infrastructure is lost, the communities that depend on it are
harmed, and institutional memory and reputation data go with it.

### Recommendation

Kith-covenant should design for partition as the default, with
joint administration as an aspiration where the relationship
between successors permits it. Abandonment should be treated as a
governance failure to be prevented, not an acceptable outcome.
The founding documents should include a partition protocol
specifying how shared infrastructure is divided when no agreement
can be reached.

## Reconstitution

Can a fractured compact be reassembled? The historical evidence:

- **The Iroquois Confederacy** continues in two forms (Grand River
  and Onondaga). The undivided compact was never reassembled.
  Partial reconstitution happened, and full reconstitution did not.
- **Post-Reformation Christendom** was never reunified. The ecumenical
  movement produced dialogue and mutual recognition but not
  institutional unity. New cooperation frameworks (Westphalia) emerged
  instead.
- **The Hanseatic League** was never reconstituted. The institutional
  form was lost.

Three conditions appear necessary for reconstitution:

1. **The substrate must survive the fracture.** If the sacred
   substrate that underwrote the original compact is still
   recognized by the successor communities (if the Great Law is
   still recited, if the creed is still confessed, if the common
   virtues are still held) then reconstitution is possible in
   principle. If the substrate has been abandoned or has diverged
   so far that the successors no longer recognize the substrate
   on the other side, reconstitution is probably impossible.
2. **The procedural forms must be preserved.** If the recognition-
   ceremony template survives (if the five-movement structure, the
   witness infrastructure, and the re-affirmation cadence are still
   practiced by the successors) then the mechanism for reconstitution
   exists. New communities (or successor communities) can re-form
   around the preserved template.
3. **The institutional memory must persist.** If the compact archive
   (the record of its history, its design decisions, its successes
   and failures) survives, then the successor communities have the
   information needed to reconstitute intelligently rather than
   naively. An archive that survives is a guide. Lost institutional
   memory means starting over.

## Residual obligations

What does a departed community still owe the compact, and what does
the compact still owe a departed community?

### The rescue obligation (persists indefinitely)

The "sanctuary of the rescuer" virtue (the duty to render aid in
extremis) is not compact-contingent. It is a common virtue, and
common virtues are dispositions, not contracts. A community that
has internalized the rescue virtue does not cease to hold it upon
departure. A compact that encounters a departing community in
distress does not refuse aid because the community is no longer a
member.

The proposal is that the rescue obligation is **unconditional and
permanent**.
It applies between former compact members, between compact members
and non-members, and between communities that have never been in a
compact together. It is the irreducible minimum of the neighbor ethic,
the Good Samaritan obligation applied to the specific case of
life-threatening emergency.

### The knowledge-commons obligation (persists by design)

The compact knowledge archive includes contributions from the
departing community. Those contributions remain in the archive
after departure (they are part of the shared record, not private
property). In return, the departing community retains access to
the archive, not as a compact member but as a party to the
knowledge-commons arrangement.

The proposal is that knowledge-commons access is **mutual and
permanent**. The compact retains the contributions of the
departing community. The departing community retains read access
to the archive. Neither side can retroactively remove
contributions or deny access to the shared record.

### The non-aggression obligation (persists conditionally)

A departed community is a non-member encountered. Not a *justus
hostis*, not an anathema, but a community encountered
without compact-level commitments. The common virtues apply within
any encounter (the encounter-based neighbor ethic from
[recognition_protocols.md](recognition_protocols.md)), but the
non-aggression obligation goes further. A departed community that
was in good standing at departure should not be treated as a
potential adversary absent new cause for conflict.

The proposal is that the non-aggression obligation persists **unless and until
new conduct provides grounds for the *justus hostis* or anathema
categories**. Departure itself is not an act of
aggression and does not trigger the escalation ladder.

## Open questions

- Is there a **minimum compact size** below which the compact
  should formally dissolve rather than continue? The last
  Hansetag with nine cities suggests yes, but the threshold is
  unclear.
- What is the **procedural threshold** between "policy disagreement
  within the compact" (resolved by the escalation ladder),
  "second/third-order theological consequence" (resolved by orthodox
  determination without splitting), and "genuinely irresolvable
  foundational disagreement" (resolved by the splitting procedure)?
  The first-order / second-order / third-order framework provides
  the conceptual map, but applying it in practice requires discernment
  that cannot be fully proceduralized.
- Can compacts that emerged from a **common ancestor compact** form a
  federation of compacts (a meta-compact) and if so, does the
  recognition framework scale to that level? The Westphalian system
  is evidence that it can, but the Westphalian system also required
  a *weaker* substrate than the original Christendom compact.
- The **"external existential war" failure mode** (the Iroquois case)
  appears unsolved. Is there any compact design that can survive its
  members being forced into incompatible alliances by an external
  power? Or is this an irreducible vulnerability?
- How does the compact handle a community that **withdraws in bad
  faith**, using voluntary withdrawal to escape an impending
  designation? Should there be a lockout period during which
  withdrawal is suspended if a designation procedure is underway?

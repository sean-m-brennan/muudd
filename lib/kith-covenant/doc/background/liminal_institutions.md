# Liminal Institutions: Free Ports, Neutral Arbitration, and the Third Place Between Communities

## Thesis

Every durable cooperation regime in the historical record
maintained *liminal spaces*: institutions that belonged to no
single community but served the compact as a whole. The Iroquois
Council Fire at Onondaga, the Hanseatic Kontore in Novgorod and
Bergen, the precinct of the Delphic oracle, the medieval
pie-powder court at a fair under the Peace of God, the
capitulatory consulates of the Ottoman Empire, the free ports of
Danzig and Livorno, all share a structural position. They sit
*between* the communities they serve, drawing authority from the
compact rather than from any member, and their neutrality is
protected (when it works) by the same sacred substrate that
underwrites the compact's recognition ceremony.

These institutions carry three loads that no member community can carry alone:

1. **Neutral ground for disputes** that have escalated past
   bilateral resolution. The escalation ladder described in
   [bounded_conflict.md](bounded_conflict.md) requires mediators
   and arbiters who are not parties to the dispute. The liminal
   institution provides venue, personnel, and legitimacy for
   that mediation.

2. **Witness infrastructure** for the compact's constitutive
   ceremonies. The recognition ceremony described in
   [recognition_protocols.md](recognition_protocols.md) requires
   witnesses who hear, judge, and remember, detecting
   inauthenticity and maintaining continuity across generations.
   Liminal institutions are the natural custodians of the
   witness role.

3. **Cultivation of discernment.** The capacity to distinguish the
   fallen-but-recoverable from the demonic, which is the deepest requirement
   of the anathema designation procedure. This is a virtue,
   not a certification, and it must be cultivated somewhere. The liminal
   institution is that somewhere.

Although bilateral cooperation regimes (those without
third-party institutions) can work for a time, they decay faster
than trilateral ones, because they lack the structural position
from which mediation, witnessing, and discernment can be
exercised without factional contamination. The third party need
not be powerful. It must be *positioned*, trusted, and durable.
Kith-covenant requires liminal institutions, and their design is
constrained by the same structural requirements (sacred
substrate, capture resistance, and the refusal to become a
sovereign) that constrain the compact itself.

## Historical models

### The Iroquois Council Fire at Onondaga

The central institution of the Haudenosaunee (Iroquois)
Confederacy was not a government but a *fire*. The Council Fire
burned at Onondaga, the geographically central nation, and the
Onondaga were designated fire-keepers. Not rulers, not
legislators, but custodians of the space in which the other
nations deliberated.

The structural insight is precise. Onondaga did not vote first;
Onondaga did not set the agenda; Onondaga did not command. The
fire-keeper role was procedural and sacral. Maintain the fire,
host the sachems, ensure the forms were observed, and, above all,
*hold the wampum records*. The custodianship of memory is not a
minor administrative function. It is the mechanism by which the
institution maintains continuity and resists capture by the most
powerful member. The Seneca and Mohawk, at the geographic
extremes, held the military weight; Onondaga held the record and
the ritual. Power and memory were separated by design.

The authority of the Council Fire derived from the Great Law of
Peace (*Kaianere'kó:wa*) and ultimately from the Creator
(*Shöñgwa'ya'di:söñ*). Attacks on the fire (literal or
metaphorical) were attacks on the sacred compact itself, not
merely on the property of one nation. This sacral protection is
not decorative. It is the mechanism that prevented the
fire-keepers from being conquered, co-opted, or simply ignored by
stronger nations.

**Decay mode.** The liminal institution of the Confederacy did not
fail internally. It was fractured by an *external* existential
conflict. During the American Revolution, the Council Fire was
divided. Some nations allied with the British, some with the
Americans, and the fire was symbolically extinguished. The
reconstituted fire at Grand River (Ontario) and the continuing
fire at Onondaga (New York) are both legitimate successors, but
the undivided liminal center was lost. The lesson. A liminal
institution's greatest vulnerability may not be internal capture
but external pressure that forces its members into incompatible
alliances.

### The Hanseatic Kontore

The Hanseatic League maintained four principal *Kontore*
(trading posts with extraterritorial privileges): the Steelyard
in London, Bryggen in Bergen, Peterhof in Novgorod, and the
trading quarter at Bruges. These were not embassies of a single
city. They were institutions of the *Hanse as a whole*, staffed,
regulated, and defended by the League: merchants came from many
member cities, the posts governed themselves by their own
internal regulations, and the collective commercial power of the
League protected them.

The structural position of the Kontore was genuinely liminal.
Although a merchant residing in the Peterhof was subject to
Hanseatic law rather than Novgorodian law, the Peterhof existed
only because Novgorod permitted it, and Novgorod permitted it
because the trade was valuable. The Kontor was neither sovereign
nor subject. It was a *guest institution* whose continued
existence depended on its usefulness to both sides.

The Kontore served all three liminal functions. They were
**neutral ground** for commercial disputes between merchants of
different cities (the Kontor alderman adjudicated). They provided
**witness infrastructure** for League commercial practices (the
Kontor maintained the records, weights, and measures that made
cross-city trade legible). And they cultivated **discernment**
(the ability to distinguish honest traders from fraudsters)
through apprenticeship and reputation networks.

**Funding model.** The Kontore were funded by trade levies, a
percentage of every transaction passing through the post. This
gave them independence from any single city while making them
dependent on the aggregate trade of the League. Levies, volume,
and usefulness held the arrangement together. The capture
vulnerability was structural. When trade patterns shifted (the
Novgorod Kontor became less important as Russian trade routes
changed), the institution withered because its funding base
dried up.

**Decay mode.** Although the Kontore did not fall to capture,
they fell to irrelevance. As the Hanse weakened in the 15th and
16th centuries, host cities revoked the extraterritorial
privileges of the Kontore because the League could no longer
enforce them. The Steelyard was closed by Elizabeth I in 1598.
The Peterhof was destroyed by Ivan III in 1494. The lesson. A
liminal institution's material independence must not be tied so
tightly to a single economic function that structural change in
the economy destroys the institution.

### Temple precincts: Delphi and Jerusalem

The oracle at Delphi occupied a precinct that was, in effect,
international territory within the Greek world. The *amphictyony* (the
league of twelve tribes that administered Delphi) was not a government.
It was a custodial body for a sacred institution. Wars between members
were not forbidden (the amphictyony was no peace league), but attacks
on Delphi itself, its priests, or travelers to the oracle were *sacred
offenses* punished by the league collectively.

The mechanism is structurally identical to the Iroquois Council
Fire. Protection derives from the sacred substrate, not from
military power. Delphi had no army. Enforcement by the
amphictyony was collective religious sanction: exclusion from
the temple, from the Pythian Games, from the ritual life that
gave meaning to the Greek world. The penalty for violating
Delphic neutrality was not death but *excommunication from the
sacred commons*.

The Jerusalem Temple played an analogous role in Israelite
governance before the monarchy. The Ark of the Covenant resided at
Shiloh, later at Jerusalem, and the sacral unity of the tribal
confederacy was located there. The Temple was not a government
building. It was the place where the tribes met before God. Its
destruction by Babylon (586 BC) and by Rome (70 AD) were not
merely military defeats. They were destructions of the liminal
institution itself, with catastrophic consequences for the
cooperative framework that depended on it.

**Decay mode.** Both Delphi and the Jerusalem Temple fell to
external conquest, but in both cases, the conquest was preceded by
a period of *instrumentalization* in which powerful parties
attempted to use the sacred institution for factional advantage.
Philip II of Macedon manipulated the Delphic amphictyony as a
pretext for expanding Macedonian power. The Hasmoneans and
Herodians used the religious authority of the Temple to legitimize
political rule. The pattern. Liminal institutions that are
instrumentalized by powerful members lose their neutrality, and
once neutrality is lost, the sacred protection erodes because the
institution is no longer serving the substrate but serving a
faction.

### Consular courts and the capitulation system

The Ottoman Empire maintained a system of *capitulations.* Treaties
granting foreign communities (Venetian, Genoese, French, later British
and others) the right to adjudicate disputes among their own members on
Ottoman soil, under their own law, through their own consular courts.
The capitulation was not a weakness. It was a deliberate liminal
architecture. Foreigners were welcome to trade but not required to
submit to Ottoman law in their internal affairs.

The consular court was a liminal institution serving the foreign trading
community within the Ottoman context. It was funded by the community it
served, staffed by community members, and recognized by the Ottoman
authorities as having jurisdiction over intra-community disputes. It did
*not* have jurisdiction over disputes between communities or between a
community and Ottoman subjects. Those went to Ottoman courts. The
boundary was clear, and the clarity was the mechanism.

**Decay mode.** The capitulation system decayed when the power
asymmetry between the Ottoman Empire and European states inverted.
What had been a gracious concession by a powerful empire became,
by the 19th century, an instrument of European imperial
interference. European powers used the capitulations to extract
extraterritorial privileges that went far beyond the original
intra-community adjudication function. The lesson. A liminal
institution designed for a specific power balance becomes an
instrument of domination when the balance shifts. The liminal
institutions here must be designed for durability across power
shifts, not calibrated to a particular configuration.

### Medieval fair courts (pie-powder courts)

The *courts of pie-powder* (from French *pieds poudrés*, "dusty
feet", referring to the itinerant merchants who appeared before
them) were pop-up liminal institutions. They convened at fairs and
markets, operated under the *lex mercatoria* (merchant law), and
dissolved when the fair ended. Their jurisdiction was limited to
commercial disputes arising at the fair, and their enforcement
mechanism was reputation. A merchant who defied a ruling faced
exclusion from future fairs.

The pie-powder court is the purest example of a liminal
institution that requires almost no permanent infrastructure. It
needs. A venue (the fair), a recognized adjudicator (often the
fair steward or a panel of experienced merchants), a procedure
(summary, oral, same-day adjudication was the norm. Justice
delayed was justice denied for a merchant who needed to be in the
next town tomorrow), and a reputation-enforcement mechanism (the
network of fairs and merchants that carried those judgments).
Everything else is overhead.

**Decay mode.** The pie-powder courts were absorbed by the state. As
royal courts expanded their jurisdiction from the 14th to the 16th centuries,
the separate merchant-law jurisdiction was folded into the common law
(in England) or the civil law (on the Continent). The lex mercatoria
survived as a body of principles, but the institutional form (the
pop-up court under the sacred protection of the fair) was dissolved.
The lesson. Liminal institutions that lack permanent structural
protection are absorbed by permanent institutions.

### Free ports: Danzig, Livorno, Singapore

The free port is a liminal institution in spatial form. A zone of
*suspended sovereignty* where the normal rules of the surrounding
territory do not fully apply. Danzig (Gdańsk) functioned as a free
city within the Hanseatic network, with a degree of independence from
both the Teutonic Order and the Polish Crown. Livorno, under the
Medici Grand Dukes, was deliberately opened to merchants of all
nationalities and religions (including Jews and Muslims excluded
elsewhere) with guaranteed freedom of worship and trade. Singapore was
established by the British as a free port precisely to create a
neutral point in the Southeast Asian trade network.

The common mechanism is that the sponsoring authority *gains more from
that neutrality than it would from direct control*. This is the
economic logic that underwrites liminal independence. It holds as
long as the economic calculation holds, and fails when the
sponsoring authority decides that control is more valuable than
neutrality.

**Decay mode.** Although free ports serve their sponsors well,
they tend to be absorbed by rising states (Danzig by Prussia and
later Poland), instrumentalized by declining empires (Livorno
lost its special status as the Grand Duchy weakened), or
transformed from neutral spaces into instruments of the
commercial strategy of the sponsoring power (Singapore under the
British East India Company, and later as an independent
city-state, retained its free-port function but under sovereign
rather than liminal governance). The lesson. Spatial liminality
without sacral protection is unstable, because the purely
economic calculation that justifies it is always subject to
revision.

## Structural requirements

What makes a liminal institution *liminal* rather than merely
*weak*? Four structural requirements distinguish the two. First,
it must hold standing without sovereignty. Second, it must take
funding without capture. Third, it must have continuity without
heredity. Finally, it must exercise expertise without
inquisition.

**1. Standing without sovereignty.** The institution must have
sufficient authority to convene parties, adjudicate disputes,
maintain records, and conduct ceremonies. But it must not possess
sovereignty over territory, people, or resources beyond what its
custodial function requires. Sovereignty is the terminal disease
of a liminal institution. Every historical case in which a liminal
institution accumulated sovereign power (the temporal jurisdiction
of the papacy, the quasi-sovereign trajectory of the EU) marks the
beginning of the end of its liminal function.

**2. Funded without captured.** Material sustenance must be
structural rather than dependent on the goodwill of particular
members. The Hanseatic levy, the Delphic offerings, and the
fair-court fees all provided material independence. Although every
funding mechanism creates a capture vulnerability, the
vulnerabilities differ in kind. He who pays the piper calls the
tune. The design problem is to diversify funding sources enough
that no single payer can exert control, while keeping the funding
mechanism legible enough that the compact can verify that
independence.

**3. Continuity without heredity.** Liminal institutions must
persist across generations, but their personnel must not become a
hereditary caste. The Onondaga fire-keepers were selected from
specific clans by clan mothers. A rotation mechanism that ensured
continuity of the role while preventing any individual lineage
from owning it. The Hanseatic Kontor aldermen served terms. The
Delphic priestesses were selected, not bred. Mediators must be
drawn from member communities on some rotation basis, serving
terms rather than lifetimes, and returning to their home
communities afterward.

**4. Expertise without inquisition.** The mediator role requires
discernment. It distinguishes the fallen-but-recoverable from
the demonic, the sincere recitation from the hollow one, and the
good-faith dispute from the bad-faith provocation. Discernment
is a virtue, not a credential, cultivated by practice, habit,
and correction rather than conferred by examination. But every
historical attempt to cultivate discernment in a specialized
class has risked producing inquisitors. People who *enjoy* the
exercise of judgment over others, who mistake procedural
authority for spiritual authority, and who use their position to
enforce orthodoxy rather than to detect inauthenticity. The
antidote is structural: rotation, accountability to the compact
rather than to the institution, and the presence of the jester
function (see [jester_and_dissent.md](jester_and_dissent.md)) as
a check on mediator power.

## The mediator pool

### Minimum viable size

The bounded-conflict escalation ladder
([bounded_conflict.md](bounded_conflict.md)) requires mediators
at several rungs. Envoys, panels, and assemblies: private envoys
for early-stage disputes, arbitration panels for mid-stage
disputes, and a compact-wide deliberative body for the anathema
designation procedure. Although these are different roles
requiring different skills, they draw from the same pool.

The minimum viable pool must satisfy three constraints. First,
it must not overlap with the parties. Second, it must resist
capture. Third, it must supply competence.

- **Non-overlap with parties.** No mediator from a community that is
  party to the dispute. This requires the pool to be large enough that
  at least three qualified mediators are available from non-party
  communities for any bilateral dispute.
- **Capture resistance.** No single community can dominate the pool.
  This requires either proportional representation (each community
  contributes an equal number) or random selection from a larger
  qualified set.
- **Competence.** Mediators must have the discernment and procedural
  knowledge to serve. This requires some form of apprenticeship or
  demonstrated capacity, without creating a certification barrier that
  becomes a gatekeeping mechanism.

For a compact of *n* communities, the minimum mediator pool is
approximately 3*n*, enough for a three-person panel drawn
entirely from non-party communities in any bilateral dispute,
with each community contributing roughly equally. For small
compacts (3 to 5 communities), every community must contribute
at least one mediator, and the pool is too small for formal
rotation. The pie-powder model (ad hoc panels for specific
disputes) is more realistic. For larger compacts (10+), a
standing mediator corps with term limits and formal rotation
becomes feasible.

### The protecting-powers analogue

The Geneva Conventions designate *protecting powers.* Neutral states
that look after the interests of parties to a conflict. The protecting
power has access to prisoners, inspects detention conditions, and
forwards communications. It is not an arbitrator. It is a witness with
standing.

Kith-covenant's analogue. For any dispute that reaches the
mediation stage of the escalation ladder, a non-party community is
designated as the protecting power for each disputant. The
protecting power exists to ensure that the rights of the disputant
under the common-virtue framework are respected during the
dispute. That the escalation ladder is being climbed correctly,
that the disputant has access to the mediators, that
communications are not interdicted, that the dispute is not being
used as a pretext for violations of the common virtues.

### Discernment training

How are mediators prepared? The historical models suggest three
approaches, each with characteristic strengths and failures:

**The apprenticeship model** (Iroquois, Hanseatic). Prospective
mediators serve under experienced mediators for an extended
period, absorbing judgment through practice. The strength is
genuine discernment. The weakness is susceptibility to capture
by the incumbent generation's biases.

**The selection model** (Delphic, some monastic). Mediators are
chosen by a process that is partly or wholly out of human hands:
by lot, by divination, or by discernment of calling. The
strength is resistance to factional control. The weaknesses are
that the selected person may lack competence and that the
selection mechanism may be corrupted.

**The rotation model** (jury systems, some democratic).
Mediators are drawn from a broad population, serve briefly, and
return to ordinary life. The strength is that the model is
maximally capture-resistant. The weakness is that mediators lack
depth of experience.

Kith-covenant's design should combine elements. A qualified pool
populated by apprenticeship, selected for specific cases by lot or
rotation, with term limits that ensure return to the home community.
The structural feature that matters is that mediators *must return home*.
A permanent mediator class is the first step toward an inquisitorial
caste.

## Witness infrastructure for the recognition ceremony

The recognition ceremony described in
[recognition_protocols.md](recognition_protocols.md) requires
witnesses who hear, discern, record, and carry the memory
forward.

1. **Hearing.** The witness is present for the recitation and receives
   it as a speech act. Not reading a transcript, not watching a
   recording, but being physically present for the spoken commitment.
   The asymmetric-psychological-cost mechanism developed in
   recognition_protocols.md depends on the bodily co-presence of
   speaker and witness.

2. **Discernment.** The witness assesses sincerity, not by
   interrogation but by the involuntary signals that spoken
   commitment produces. A hollow recitation is detectable
   (recognition_protocols.md §"Asymmetric psychological cost. A
   closer look"), and the witness is there to detect it. This is
   not a pass/fail examination. It is a discernment that may
   lead to conversation, re-examination, and in the limit a
   recommendation that the compact not accept the recitation.

3. **Recording.** The witness signs the cryptographic record of
   the ceremony. The multi-party signature described in the
   implementation notes of recognition_protocols.md requires
   witnesses as co-signers. That signature attests not merely that
   the ceremony occurred but that *the witness judged the
   recitation sincere*.

4. **Continuity.** The witness carries the memory of the ceremony into
   the future, available for the generational re-affirmation cadence.
   When the next generation re-recites, witnesses from the previous
   generation (or their successors) provide continuity. They know
   what was said before and can assess whether the re-affirmation is
   genuine continuation or drift.

### Witness selection and rotation

Witnesses should be drawn from the mediator pool but need not be the
same individuals who serve as dispute mediators. The compact may
designate a witness corps within the broader mediator pool, with its
own rotation schedule. The key constraint: **witness continuity must
be maintained alongside rotation**. This means staggered terms (never
replacing all witnesses simultaneously) so that institutional memory
survives across cohorts.

A practical model. For each recognition ceremony, three
witnesses are required: one drawn from the outgoing cohort
(continuity), one from the current cohort (institutional
presence), and one from a non-mediator community delegation
(external check). The three-witness structure provides
redundancy and diversity of perspective.

## The emergency anathema procedure

The anathema designation procedure in
[bounded_conflict.md](bounded_conflict.md) is deliberately heavy. Four
conditions must be met, multiple communities must concur, and the
designation carries severe consequences. This weight is a feature, not
a bug. It prevents weaponization.

But the open question from bounded_conflict.md is real: **is the
procedure too heavy for cases where rapid action is genuinely needed?**

Consider the scenario. A community begins actively destroying
the compact communication relays (attacking the knowledge
commons) or systematically harming non-combatants from other
communities. The full designation procedure takes time:
deliberation, examination of evidence, and concurrence of
multiple communities. Meanwhile, the harm continues.

### Proposal. The interim injunction

The liminal institution may issue an **interim injunction.** A
temporary restraining order that applies specific protective measures
*before* full designation is completed. The interim injunction:

- **May be issued by a panel of three mediators** from the active
  mediator pool, acting under emergency authority granted by the
  compact founding documents.
- **Must specify the harm** being addressed and the protective measures
  being applied. It is not a blanket designation. It is a targeted
  response to a specific ongoing harm.
- **Expires after a defined period** (proposal: 90 days) unless
  confirmed by the full compact deliberation process.
- **Triggers mandatory convocation** of the full compact to consider
  the case. The interim injunction is not an alternative to the full
  procedure but a bridge to it.
- **Is itself subject to review.** The affected community may
  challenge the injunction before a different mediator panel. The
  challenge does not suspend the injunction but must be heard within
  a short period (proposal: 14 days).

### Anti-weaponization safeguards

The interim injunction carries the same weaponization risks as any
emergency power. Three safeguards:

1. **The mediator panel that issues the injunction may not serve on the
   full designation panel.** This prevents the emergency panel from
   acquiring momentum toward designation.
2. **If the full compact deliberation does not confirm the injunction
   within the defined period, the injunction lapses and the issuing
   panel is barred from emergency service for a rotation period.**
   This creates a cost for unjustified emergency action.
3. **The injunction itself is published to the entire compact
   immediately upon issuance.** There are no secret injunctions.
   The compact information commons (see
   [jester_and_dissent.md](jester_and_dissent.md)) ensures
   transparency.

### Historical precedent

The interim injunction has precedent. The papal interdict, immediate
ecclesiastical sanction (suspension of sacraments for a territory)
pending full hearing, served exactly this function in medieval
Christendom. The Hanseatic emergency boycott (a temporary trade ban
imposed by a subset of member cities pending the next *Hansetag*) is
the commercial analogue. Both were subject to misuse. Both were
nonetheless necessary because the full deliberative process was too
slow for genuine emergencies. The design question is not whether
emergency powers are needed (they are) but how to structure them so
that misuse is costly and detectable.

## Space application. Shared facilities as liminal spaces

In the space habitat context, liminal institutions take physical
form as shared facilities that belong to no single habitat.
Docks, relays, and beacons are the first three.

**Shared docking facilities.** A neutral docking station where
ships from different habitats can berth, transfer cargo, and
exchange personnel without entering the sovereign space of either
habitat. The docking facility is the space analogue of the free
port. A zone of suspended sovereignty governed by compact rules
rather than the law of any single habitat.

**Communication relays.** Relay stations positioned at Lagrange
points or along high-traffic routes, maintained by the compact
rather than by individual habitats. These relays are the space
analogue of the Hanseatic Kontore. Neutral infrastructure that
enables the trade (information, in this case) that makes the
compact valuable. Attacks on communication relays are attacks on
the compact information commons and constitute a violation of the
"protection of communicative commons" virtue.

**Emergency rendezvous points.** Pre-designated locations where
distressed vessels or habitats can seek assistance from any
compact member. The rendezvous point is protected by the
"sanctuary of the rescuer" virtue. The duty to render aid
regardless of which community the distressed party belongs to.
The liminal institution maintains, staffs, and protects: it
keeps the infrastructure at the rendezvous point, staffs it
where necessary, and ensures that rescue operations are not
interdicted or exploited.

**Navigation beacons and astronomical infrastructure.** Shared
observatories, beacons, and clocks that no single habitat needs
to maintain alone but all require. These are the commons that
commons_federation.md will develop further, and the liminal role
over them is custodial.

The physics of orbital mechanics constrains the design. A
neutral docking station at a Lagrange point is accessible to all
habitats in the system, but AU-distance travel times mean that a
distressed habitat cannot reach it quickly. The solution is a
network of liminal facilities at multiple locations, each
maintained by a subset of nearby compact members under compact
governance. Distributed, redundant, and local, they answer the
distance the physics imposes. A distributed liminal architecture
rather than a single central fire.

## Funding and material independence

A liminal institution that depends on the goodwill of a single member
for its material existence is not liminal. It is a client. The
historical funding models, each with its characteristic capture
vulnerability:

**The trade-levy model** (Hanseatic Kontore). A percentage of
every transaction passing through the institution funds its
operations. Institutional interests are aligned with trade volume,
which is generally aligned with the health of the compact.
**Capture vulnerability.** When trade patterns shift, the funding
base collapses. Also, the largest traders pay the most and may
demand proportional influence.

**The offering model** (temple precincts). Voluntary contributions
from communities that use its services. **Capture vulnerability:**
contributions are voluntary and can be withheld as leverage.
Wealthy communities contribute more and may claim proportional
influence. The institution may develop incentives to please its
donors rather than serve the compact.

**The assessed-contribution model** (modern international
organizations). Each member community pays an assessed share, typically
proportional to some measure of capacity (population, economic output,
resource base). **Capture vulnerability.** The largest contributors
(the most powerful communities) have structural leverage over the
institution (the US/UN dynamic is the canonical example).

**The endowment model** (some monasteries, some universities). The
institution receives an initial endowment sufficient to sustain it
indefinitely through investment returns. **Capture
vulnerability.** Endowment management becomes the primary concern.
Investment decisions create dependencies on external parties. The
real value of the endowment may erode through mismanagement or
inflation.

Kith-covenant's design should combine elements. Proposed. A base
assessed contribution from all member communities (ensuring
material independence from any single member), supplemented by
service fees for specific functions (mediation, witnessing,
record-keeping), with a reserve fund maintained under the
compact founding documents to ensure continuity during crises.
The key structural safeguard. The liminal institution's budget
is set by the compact as a whole, not by the institution itself.
The institution may request; the compact decides.

## The sacred character of liminal space

Every historical liminal institution in the case set (without
exception) was *sacred*. The Council Fire was tended under the
Great Law of Peace; Delphi was the precinct of Apollo. The
pie-powder court sat under the Peace of God. The capitulatory
consulates operated within the Ottoman *millet* system's
recognition of religious community. The free port's special status
was often guaranteed by treaty-oaths sworn before God.

The sacred character was not decorative. It was **load-bearing**. It
performed three functions that secular authority cannot replicate:

**1. It placed attacks on the institution in a different moral
category.** An attack on a trading post is a commercial act. An attack
on a sacred precinct is a violation of the sacred order itself. This
category-shift raises the cost of violation from "we lose a trading
partner" to "we offend the substrate", and the latter cost, for
communities whose oaths rest on the substrate, is existential.

**2. It grounded institutional authority in something beyond its
own power.** A liminal institution that derives its authority from
its own military or economic power is simply a weak state. A
liminal institution that derives its authority from the sacred
substrate can be materially weak and still exercise legitimate
authority, because the authority comes from the same source that
underwrites the recognition ceremony.

**3. It provided a check on the institution's own ambitions.** An
institution that serves the sacred substrate is accountable to
something beyond its own interests. The Onondaga fire-keepers did not
become sovereign because their role was defined by the Great Law, which
they served rather than authored. The Delphic priestesses did not
become political actors (for the most part) because their authority
was oracular, not governmental. The sacred character imposes humility
on the institution, or at least provides the structural resources
by which the compact can demand humility.

For kith-covenant, this means that the liminal institutions must
be explicitly placed under the sacred substrate. Their founding
documents should include a recognition that the institution serves
the same ultimate that the five-movement recognition ceremony
names. Attacks on liminal institutions should be treated as
violations of the "protection of communicative commons" and
"reverence for life" virtues, not merely as property damage or
strategic inconvenience.

## Capture resistance and the sovereignty temptation

The deepest failure mode for a liminal institution is the temptation
to become a sovereign. The institution accumulates standing,
personnel, material resources, procedural knowledge, and (if it
does its job well) moral authority. At some point, the temptation
arises to convert this accumulated capital into *power over* the
compact's members rather than *service to* the compact.

### Historical cases of the sovereignty temptation

**The papacy.** The Bishop of Rome began as a spiritual mediator
among Christian communities, precisely a liminal institution.
Over centuries, the papacy accumulated temporal power (the Papal
States), jurisdictional authority (the right to depose kings),
and institutional infrastructure (the Curia). By the High Middle
Ages, the papacy was no longer liminal. It was a sovereign
competing with other sovereigns. The Reformation was, among
other things, a rejection of a liminal claim to sovereignty.

**The European Union.** The EU began as the European Coal and
Steel Community. A liminal institution for managing a shared
commons (coal and steel production) between former enemies. It has
progressively accumulated regulatory authority, judicial supremacy
(the European Court of Justice), quasi-legislative power (the
directives of the Commission), and (most recently) fiscal
instruments. Whether the EU has crossed the line from liminal
institution to quasi-sovereign is a live political question. That
it has moved decisively in that direction is not seriously
disputed.

**The Iroquois fire-keepers.** The Onondaga fire-keepers did *not*
succumb to the sovereignty temptation. They remained custodians
rather than rulers for centuries. The structural safeguards. The
fire-keepers did not command warriors from the other nations. The
wampum records were held in trust, not owned. The authority of the
Council Fire was procedural (hosting, facilitating) rather than
legislative (deciding, commanding). And the sacred character of
the role placed it under the Great Law rather than above it. This
is the positive model.

### Design principles for capture resistance

From the historical cases, five principles emerge:

1. **Separate power and memory.** The institution that holds the
   records should not be the institution that commands the
   armies. The Onondaga/Seneca separation is the model, with
   custodianship and power in different hands.

2. **Term limits and mandatory return.** Mediators and witnesses serve
   for defined periods and return to their home communities. There is
   no career path within the liminal institution that leads to
   permanent residence.

3. **No self-appointment.** The institution does not select its own
   successors. The compact selects mediators through a process that
   involves member communities (nomination) and a randomization
   element (lot) to resist factional coordination.

4. **Budget control by the compact, not the institution.** The
   institution requests; the compact decides. The institution
   may not independently levy taxes, impose fees, or accumulate
   reserves beyond a defined operating buffer.

5. **The sacred constraint.** The founding documents explicitly
   state that the institution serves the compact substrate, not
   its own interests. Violation of this constraint (an institution
   that begins acting in its own interest rather than for the
   compact) is grounds for the compact to reconstitute the
   institution with new personnel. This is the compact-level
   analogue of the *hostis humani generis* designation. A liminal
   institution that has been captured is no longer serving its
   function and must be reformed.

## Manufactured-event resistance. The structured-skepticism protocol

The capture-resistance principles above protect the liminal
institution itself from sovereignty drift. A complementary capture
vector operates *through* the liminal institution. A sophisticated
adversary deliberately generates an event of high interpretive
charge (a "first contact" claim, an anomalous-physics result, a
manufactured rescue need, a sudden "rediscovery" of historical
interpretation) and exploits the missing evaluation discipline in
the ambient community to push the interpretation in whichever
direction benefits the manufacturer. The mediator pool, as the
standing evaluation competency, is the natural locus for the
counter-mechanism.

The pattern is documented in *Messengers of Deception* (1979)
across UFO contactee cults, intelligence-agency seeded
paranormal narratives, and entrepreneurial religious movements.
The structural analogue in this setting is direct, with concrete
scenarios catalogued in [capture_resistance.md
"Manufactured-event resistance
protocols"](capture_resistance.md#4-manufactured-event-resistance-protocols).
What distinguishes manufactured events from genuine ones is not
their content (the manufacturer can fabricate plausible content)
but their **provenance and timing**: high interpretive charge,
convenient alignment with the interests of one faction, sudden
onset, and origination from witnesses who hold a stake in the
interpretation.

The mediator pool is empowered to invoke the
**structured-skepticism protocol** on its own initiative when an
event meets these qualifying criteria. The protocol does not
prejudge. It does not assume the event is manufactured. It imposes
structural delay and corroboration requirements that buy time for
the ambient community to develop interpretation, depriving the
manufacturer of the loudest-interpreter advantage that enables the
historical pattern.

The protocol's elements:

1. **Structured delay before compact-level action.** The interval scales
   with interpretive charge. Long enough for member communities to
   develop independent interpretation, short enough that genuine
   emergencies (rescue, life-critical commons) are not blocked.
   Life-critical exceptions are explicit and named at protocol-formation.
   The qualifying-criteria check on the exception itself prevents the
   manufactured-rescue-need scenario.

2. **Multi-witness corroboration requirement.** The interpretation
   cannot be advanced by the originating party alone. Witnesses from
   multiple member communities must independently corroborate the
   observable facts (separated cleanly from any interpretation of
   them) before the interpretation enters compact deliberation.

3. **Cross-substrate translation.** The interpretation must be
   translated into the substrates of multiple member communities
   and the translations compared. An interpretation that survives
   only in its originating framing (that fails to render
   coherently into adjacent substrates) is not necessarily wrong
   but is structurally suspect, and the structural suspicion is
   recorded in the audit commons.

4. **Provenance tagging in the audit commons.** The interpretation
   is tagged with the identity of its originator, declared
   incentives, and the information history of its emergence.
   Future reference to the interpretation carries this tag.

5. **Stake disclosure.** Parties advancing the interpretation must
   disclose what they stand to gain from each available framing.
   Failure to disclose, when interest is later discovered, becomes
   evidence of bad faith and is itself a designation-relevant event.

The historical model is the canonical Catholic process for evaluating
apparitions, deliberately slow. Alleged apparitions go through years
or decades of investigation before any authoritative judgment is
issued, and the great majority are never judged at all. The slowness
denies the loudest-interpreter advantage by ensuring the interpretation
must survive a long process during which ambient interpretation can
develop. The KC analogue need not be that slow. The criterion is
**long enough that the originating party cannot dominate the framing**,
calibrated to the event class. For most events this is hours to days.
For high-charge events of major interpretive consequence it may extend
to weeks or months.

Mediator-pool internal review checks that invocation itself is not
used as a tactic. A faction attempting to suppress legitimate
urgent matters by invoking structured skepticism against them. The
check is procedural (the criteria must be documented specifically.
Invocations are auditable) and substantive (the discernment
training of the mediator pool, per [the discernment-training
subsection above](#discernment-training), includes the distinction
between genuine and pretextual invocation).

## Open questions

- What is the **minimum material base** for a liminal institution to
  survive capture attempts by a coalition of the most powerful members?
  The historical cases suggest that material weakness invites absorption,
  but material strength invites the sovereignty temptation. Where is the
  viable band?

- Can liminal institutions be **virtualized**, operating through
  protocol (cryptographic ceremony, distributed records, remote
  mediation) rather than physical space? Or does the
  sacred-substrate finding require physical meeting? The
  asymmetric-psychological-cost analysis in recognition_protocols.md
  strongly suggests that at least the witness function requires bodily
  co-presence. But does *all* mediation require it?

- How does the compact **reconstitute its liminal institutions** after
  a catastrophic disruption. War, plague, infrastructure failure, the
  kind of fire-extinguishing event the Iroquois experienced? The
  reconstitution question is shared with
  [exit_and_succession.md](exit_and_succession.md).

- What is the relationship between the compact's liminal institutions
  and each **member community's internal sacred spaces**? Is the
  liminal institution a separate thing, or does it draw from the same
  sacral well? The Iroquois model suggests the latter (the Council
  Fire was tended under the same Great Law that governed each nation
  internally). The Hanseatic model suggests the former (the Kontore
  were commercial, not sacred, institutions).

- The **collusion vulnerability**
  ([bounded_conflict.md](bounded_conflict.md) §"The collusion
  vulnerability") applies with particular force to liminal
  institutions. A coordinated faction that captures the mediator
  pool captures the primary checking mechanism. The rotation and
  diversity safeguards raise the threshold but do not eliminate
  the vulnerability. Is there a liminal-institution-specific
  defense beyond what the general collusion analysis provides?

- The **jester function** ([jester_and_dissent.md](jester_and_dissent.md))
  is a check on the mediator pool. But who checks the jester? Is there
  a recursive problem here (each checking institution needs a
  further checking institution) or does the sacred substrate provide
  the terminal check?

- Can the **interim injunction** mechanism be tested at small scale
  before deployment? What is the minimum compact size at which the
  injunction architecture becomes meaningful (i.e., the mediator pool
  is large enough that emergency and deliberative panels can be
  fully non-overlapping)?

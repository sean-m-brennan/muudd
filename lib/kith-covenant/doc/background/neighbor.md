# The neighbor: enacted nearness and the limit of categories

## Thesis

"Love your neighbor as yourself" is the oldest cooperation instruction in
the moral inheritance kith-covenant draws on, and the one most often misread
as a rule. Read as a rule it fails at once. It never says who the neighbor
is, and every attempt to say who the neighbor is has been used to shrink the
obligation down to a manageable in-group. The scriptural sources do something
else. They refuse to fix the neighbor as a category settled in advance, and
they make neighboring a thing you enact toward whoever is in front of you.
That refusal is not vagueness. It is the working part, and the finding behind it
is old, textual, and load-bearing. It is why kith-covenant
credentials what a community does rather than what it professes, why
enemy-love is written into the protocol as an operational norm rather than a
sentiment, and why the four standing categories in
[Recognition protocols](recognition_protocols.md#members-neighbors-enemies-the-four-categories)
are built around the encounter rather than around a roster.

## The word behind the command

The command is quoted, not coined. When the Gospels give "love your neighbor
as yourself" (Matthew 22:39; Mark 12:31; and the recitation by the lawyer in
Luke 10:27), they are quoting Leviticus 19:18. The Greek word standing for
neighbor in these texts is *plēsion* (πλησίον). It comes from *pelas*, "near,"
and its plain sense is spatial, the one near you, the person close at hand.
Classical Greek used it for exactly that, the neighbor next door. The pull of
the word is spatial, social, and finally moral. In the
Septuagint and in Jewish usage it narrowed socially, toward a fellow member
of the community, a compatriot.

The Hebrew underneath is *re'a* (רֵעַ), and *re'a* carries the same pull
toward the in-group. Its range runs from neighbor to friend to companion to
fellow. In Leviticus 19:18 it sits inside a sentence addressed to conduct
among "the sons of your own people" (*benei ammekha*), which reads "You
shall not take vengeance or bear a grudge against the sons of your own
people, but you shall love your neighbor as yourself." Read on its own
terms, *re'a* most naturally means a fellow Israelite, a member of the
covenant community. A lawyer who wanted a bounded answer to "who is my
neighbor?" had the text on his side.

The boundary was never quite sealed, though, even in Leviticus. Fifteen verses
later the same chapter extends the same command to the *ger* (גֵּר), the
resident alien, in the words "you shall love him as yourself, for you were
strangers in the land of Egypt" (19:34). The covenant community was already told to love the
outsider living among it with the identical formula. The seam that Christ
later pulls wide open is present in the source.

## The Samaritan reversal

In Luke 10 a lawyer asks Jesus "and who is my neighbor?" It is a good
question in the lexical sense, and it has a defensible answer from *re'a*, namely your
compatriot, your covenant-fellow. What Jesus does with it is the important
move. He does not correct the Greek, appeal to a variant reading, or argue
that *re'a* secretly means more than it says. He tells the parable of the
Samaritan and ends it with a different question, "which of these three proved
to be a neighbor to the man who fell among the robbers?"

The grammar has turned over. The lawyer asked about the neighbor as an
*object*, a class of people to whom the duty is owed and whose edges can
therefore be drawn. Jesus answers in terms of the *subject*, the one who
acted. Neighbor stops being a category you belong to and becomes something you
do. The Samaritan was not near the wounded man in any prior sense, not by
ethnicity, religion, or acquaintance. He made himself near. He saw, stopped, and paid. The
spatial root of the word turns dynamic. The question is no longer "who is
already near me?" but "toward whom will I draw near?"

This is not a claim that the Greek word *plēsion* privately means "action
toward." It does not. The parable takes the spatial openness already latent in
the word, the plain fact that "the one near you" specifies a relation and not a
type, and drives it past the communal reading the lawyer was counting on. The
definition game itself is what Jesus declines to play. Treating the word as a
fence to be surveyed is the evasion, whatever word is on the fence.

## Why no definition ever holds

The reason the definition game cannot be won is that neighbor-love does not
have the shape of a rule. A rule lets you ask "have I done enough?" and get an
answer, and the answer always draws a line with your obligation on one side and
your freedom on the other. Neighbor-love has no such line, because its object
is not a set. It points at *this* person, in *this* situation, now. It is
indexical. The command is total, particular, and uncountable. That is the
property that keeps escaping every legislative attempt
to pin its scope, and several traditions have named the property from their own
angles.

Luther makes the move directly. The neighbor-command cannot be discharged by
rule-following, because rules invite the accounting question, and the
Spirit-turned person does not calculate an obligation to the neighbor but is
simply turned toward them. In *The Freedom of a Christian* the believer is at
once perfectly free and dutiful servant of all, which is another way of saying
the duty is total and therefore uncountable. Kierkegaard gives the most
sustained treatment in *Works of Love*. Neighbor-love, unlike preferential love
for the ones we naturally favor, is *commanded* precisely because it cannot
grow from inclination or social category. It has to come from a third source,
which he locates in God working on the will. Barth insists that the command of
God arrives each time as a fresh and specific address, so that past obedience
cannot be banked and future duty cannot be pre-computed. Every meeting with the
neighbor is a new event of calling.

The Catholic tradition reaches the same place through the doctrine of the New
Law. Aquinas, following Augustine, holds that the New Law is first of all the
grace of the Holy Spirit poured into the heart, and only secondarily a set of
written precepts, which are instrumental to that interior gift (*Summa
Theologiae* I-II, qq. 106-108). The primary law is an indwelling dynamic, not a
text. The distinction Bonhoeffer draws between cheap and costly grace runs along the
same grain, together with his insistence that obedience is always concrete and
particular and never abstract. Across these voices the common finding holds.
Neighbor-love is non-codifiable, because it is indexed to the encounter, and
anything indexed to the encounter cannot be fully specified before the
encounter happens.

## The new-Israel problem

This is where the philology stops being a curiosity and starts doing work for
kith-covenant. Suppose you take the covenant reading of *re'a* seriously and
carry it forward, so that the neighbor is the fellow member of the covenant
people, and
the Church understands itself as the continuation of that people. The bounded
answer the lawyer wanted seems to survive after all, now indexed to profession
of faith rather than to ethnicity. You would love your neighbor by loving your
co-religionists.

It does not survive, and the reason is empirical. The visible community of
professed belief is full of people whose conduct does not enact the belief they
claim, and it excludes people whose conduct enacts it without the profession.
The Samaritan, the outsider by every formal test, is the one who neighbors,
while the priest and the Levite, insiders by every formal test, pass by. If membership by
profession picked out who actually neighbors, the parable would run the other
way. Categorization by creed cannot locate the neighbor, because the thing that
makes someone a neighbor is enacted, Spirit-led, and shows up in what they do
under pressure rather than in the label they wear. The real process is
non-legislatable. You cannot read neighborliness off the stated creed of a
community, in either direction.

## What kith-covenant takes from this

The protocol does not need to settle the theology. It needs the structural
finding, and the finding is directly usable.

First, it fixes what the compact credentials. Because neighbor-love is enacted
rather than professed, and because profession fails to pick out who enacts it,
the inter-community layer cannot admit or trust a community on the strength of
its stated creed. It has to examine operative practice, meaning what a community
actually does, and specifically what it refuses to do, when the pressure is on.
This is the same conclusion the corpus reaches from the failure side in the
Salem case, where a community that held the right doctrine still spiraled, and
where the lesson drawn is that credentialing examines practice and not confessed
doctrine (see
[justus_hostis.md "The Salem witch trials"](justus_hostis.md#the-salem-witch-trials-the-features-can-be-held-and-still-defeated)).
The five anti-spiral features the compact checks at recognition (see
[Recognition protocols](recognition_protocols.md#the-inter-community-layer-thin-grammar-and-credentialing))
are demanded as demonstrated behavior for exactly this reason. A creed can be
recited hollow. A refusal has to be paid for. What the compact reads is conduct,
refusal, and cost.

Second, it explains why the fourth anti-spiral feature, enemy-love, is written
as an *operational* norm rather than a disposition. Feature four asks a
community to owe moral seriousness to the person actually before it, regardless
of which side designated whom as enemy (see the treatment in
[Justus hostis](justus_hostis.md#the-anti-spiral-features-why-anchor-above-self-is-necessary-but-not-sufficient)).
That is the Samaritan structure stated as a protocol requirement. It has to be
operational, because the whole point of the parable is that neighboring is
enacted in the encounter and cannot be certified in the abstract. A community
that professes universal goodwill and expels its designated enemies has failed
feature four no matter how warm the profession.

Third, it grounds the neighbor concept the four standing categories already
rest on. The operational scheme in
[Recognition protocols](recognition_protocols.md#members-neighbors-enemies-the-four-categories)
turns on three claims that the parable supplies and this document has now traced
to their source. The neighbor is actualized by encounter and not by theoretical
inclusion, discernment operates within the encounter, and contact is the
condition of responsibility. The Samaritan helped the man he met, discerned the
situation well enough to rescue the victim rather than the robber, and had no
Samaritan duty toward travelers he never met on that road. That discernment is
what keeps the protocol from devolving in the face of malicious actors.

That last point is also the guardrail. Enacted nearness is not fantasy-universal
love. The parable does not extend neighbor status to all of humanity in the
abstract, which would collapse into either an impossible debt no finite
community can pay or a sentimentalism that professes everything and acts on
nothing. The neighbor is the one encountered, and within the encounter the
response is discerned and specific. This is what keeps enemy-love from softening
into an obligation to treat every actor identically. An enemy encountered
remains a neighbor within the encounter, owed the moral standing the common
virtues protect, and still an enemy, who may be resisted with discernment of
what they are and what they are not (see
[justus_hostis.md "Hostis humani generis"](justus_hostis.md#hostis-humani-generis-limits-of-the-justus-hostis-frame)).
Neighboring binds the compact to the ones it actually meets. It does not
dissolve the compact's judgment about them.

Fourth, the priority established here has a floor under it that does not depend
on compact standing. Making neighbor-status prior to compact-status means the
compact owes something to a party that has signed nothing, and the compact
grounds what it owes on the thin functional minimum it has adopted rather than on
its own authority or on the substrate of any member
([Natural law](natural_law.md#the-ground-the-compact-claims-for-itself)). The parable
supplies the shape of the duty, that it is actualized by encounter and discerned
within it, and the minimum content supplies why the duty is not for the compact to
waive at the frontier when reciprocity is absent. The two work at different
levels, and neither does the work of the other. No functional argument produces
the reversal in the parable, and that reversal is a reading held by a member
community rather than a standard the compact may assert on its own account.

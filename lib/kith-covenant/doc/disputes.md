# When communities disagree

Disputes are the ordinary case. A compact whose members never disagree is a
compact with one member, and a design that treats disagreement as a failure will
handle its first real one badly.

So disagreement gets machinery: a named ladder with reversible rungs, a pool of
mediators drawn by lot rather than appointed, and physical places between
communities that belong to none of them.

The ladder has seven rungs and each is reversible, each has an honor-saving exit,
and each is recorded. Dispute registration. Registered protest. Reciprocal
suspension of specified courtesies. Mediated arbitration. Limited material
sanctions. Compact-authorized collective sanctions. Expulsion.

Anathema designation is a separate track with its own four-condition procedure
rather than a rung on this ladder. A ladder that ended in permanent designation
would make every rung a step toward it.

## Two choices that distinguish this from a rules system

*Common virtues rather than non-negotiable rules.* The protected categories of
the compact are stated as dispositions rather than prohibitions. Reverence for
life. Sanctuary of rescuers. The communicative commons. Passage for travelers.
Innocence of non-combatants. Inheritance of future generations. Memory of the
dead. Good faith of confession.

The reason is that virtue-based standards are harder to game than rule-based
ones. Rules invite optimization against the letter, which is how combatants get
positioned as civilians and an attack gets called a humanitarian intervention.
Dispositions ask whether the act *expresses* the virtue, which is the kind of
question that rewards genuine compliance and exposes performative compliance.

*Mediators rotated rather than appointed.* A standing professional mediator class
would be the single most capturable element in the system. Mediators are drawn
from member communities by lot, serve fixed terms, publish their reasoning, and
rotate out before they accumulate factional weight.

## The pool, and the caps that bind it

The mediator pool is the union of per-community registries rather than a central
roster, which means no single body maintains it and no single body can quietly
prune it.

Four caps put mediator seats through the same roster machinery the examining
seats and the observer corps use. Terms are bounded. A sit-out separates them.
Continuous service is counted as a union across terms rather than per term. And
no single community may hold a majority of the active pool once the pool is past
a floor.

Measuring that is what showed why it mattered. The pool was the third roster in
the system and the one wired to nothing, so a community could hold nine of ten
seats and staff every panel in the compact. Worse, a mediator could be seated
with no end date at all, which is a permanent mediator class, meaning the first
step toward an inquisitorial caste. Every fixture in the test suite had been
seating mediators exactly that way, which is what repairing it actually cost.

## What a panel must do

The structural rules are enforced when a decision is recorded rather than
recommended in prose. Six of them hold.

Every panelist must be an active registered mediator. A configurable minimum sit
on each panel, defaulting to three. The panel must not overlap either party
community. A reasoning trail is mandatory. The panel field is bound to the actual
co-signers. And every case a party has filed must be answered.

Two of those became real only after measuring found they were not.

The specification called the reasoning trail mandatory and nothing enforced it,
so a decision could publish an empty reasoning field.

And the panel field was read by no rule at all. It could name the two *party*
communities, precisely whom the non-overlap rule excludes, while three legitimate
non-party mediators signed, and the record was authorized. Both are now checked,
and the panel field is checked against the co-signers it claims to report.

## Arguments, and what answering them means

An argument record is the structured submission and evidence packaging the
specification asks for. It is append-only, self-signed, and held to a different
rule per role.

A *party* argument is the case of a party, and only the complainant or the
respondent may make one. A *panelist* argument requires an active registered
mediator from neither party community, and that mediator's own signature, since
nobody argues in a mediator's name.

A decision cites the arguments it answers, and two counting rules apply. One
cited panelist argument is required per co-signer, because three signatures on one
author's text is a rubber stamp rather than a panel. And every party argument
already in the log must be answered, because a panel may not decide while
ignoring a case put to it. Insertion order is authoritative, so a later filing
cannot unmake a concluded mediation.

## Recusal, protection, and lineage

Three further records complete the pool directory, and the interesting thing
about all three is that none of them gates anything.

*Recusal*: a party names one panelist on one dispute and states grounds. The
request is recorded and answered, granted only by that mediator stepping down and
never refused by that same mediator.

*Protecting powers*: the Geneva analogue, being a non-party community standing
for a disputant, co-signed by both. The duty is never conscripted, and a
protector a disputant did not accept is a watcher imposed on them.

*Training lineage*: a record of who a mediator apprenticed under.

The mediation decides exactly as it would have without any of them, and the
lineage is read only by the pool board. That restraint is deliberate, because the
doctrine that asks for the record also warns against a certification barrier that
becomes a gatekeeping mechanism.

*Consent to the panel as constituted* is its own record, self-signed because
nobody consents on another's behalf, and naming the panel in full so that consent
to three mediators is not consent to a fourth. Objection had a record and
structural consent had a record; the ordinary case, being that this panel may
decide my dispute, was inferred from the party having filed an argument, which is
a different act.

It gates one thing and deliberately no more. A consent precondition on deciding
would hand either party a veto over its own dispute. But a settled outcome is a
claim that *the parties* settled, so both must have consented to the panel
recording it. Before the rule, a panel could record a settlement neither party
had agreed to, and every fixture in the test suite was doing exactly that.

## Sealed decisions

A decision may seal its reasoning under threshold custody, opened later by a
record that presents enough shares.

What is sealed is the reasoning alone. The dispute, the panel, and the outcome
stay public, so a compact can see that a matter was decided, by whom, and how it
came out, and every rule above keeps running. What waits is why.

An unsealing must present enough opened shares to recover the text, and that text
must open the commitment the decision published, so a quorum of custodians cannot
publish a reasoning the panel never wrote.

The custodians are *member communities rather than mediators*, and that was a
finding rather than a preference. The doctrine asks for the keys to sit with the
mediator pool *and* for a deterministic reveal at twenty-five years. A mediator
seat is capped at two years, so a share sealed to a mediator is held by a key the
archive stops resolving two decades before the seal opens, which leaves the
reveal dead by construction and the signature on the opening unverifiable.
Community identities resolve at any instant.

The substance of the doctrine is kept by arithmetic instead. No party may hold a
share, and no quorum of shares may sit with the communities of the deciding
panel, because a panel that can unseal itself has deferred publication at its own
discretion rather than sealed anything.

## Manufactured events

A high-interpretive-charge event, meaning an incident that conveniently justifies
escalation, is placed under a structured-skepticism hold before any binding
response.

The hold has teeth rather than a flag: an action premised on a qualifying,
under-corroborated event is refused.

Although the teeth were always there, what counted as qualifying was four
booleans the originating community set on its own report, so the teeth rested on
the word of the party the protocol exists to constrain. Measured: an originator
that declared its own event mundane was never held, and a full non-party mediator
pool sitting in the same archive could do nothing about it.

Either source now places the hold, and the window is the longer of the two.

The review runs against the *hold* rather than against the invocation record,
which is what lets it reach the self-declared case, being the one that answered to
nobody. A finding of pretext releases the event from a hold of either origin.
Mediators who co-signed an invocation may not review it, the originating
community may neither invoke nor review, and reasoning is required on both
answers, because an audit legible only when the hold falls says nothing about the
holds that stood.

## Interim injunctions

Between a dispute arising and a panel deciding, something may need to stop
immediately.

Injunctions issue, are confirmed, and may be challenged, with an emergency panel
route and capability gating on who may act at all. The expedited designation path
carries a covenant-facing floor: an expedited designation must cite an attested
adverse act by the subject. Without that floor, relaxing the ordinary
four-condition test leaves the emergency route resting on records about the
internal life of a community, or on citations naming nothing.

## The third place

Recognizable in every long-running historical case is a third place between
communities, drawing its authority from the compact rather than from any member.
The Iroquois Council Fire. The Hanseatic *Kontore*. The pie-powder courts. The
free ports. The Geneva of the Red Cross.

The version here is the distributed mediator pool plus shared physical
infrastructure, being docking stations at Lagrange points, communications relays,
emergency rendezvous, and navigation beacons, funded by assessed contribution
from all members so that no single member can starve it.

Three things protect such institutions from the historical failure mode where
they ossify into a fourth power: the rotation rules, the funding model, and the
deliberate refusal to grow into a sovereign of their own.
